# Pratibha - Pulaar Tradition (Pre-Islamic Fulɓe Practice)
**Corpus entry:** Pulaar / Fulfulde pastoral religion recovered from public-domain ethnography
**Primary sources (public domain):**
- Élisée Reclus, *Nouvelle géographie universelle*, t. XII, *L'Afrique occidentale* (Paris: Hachette, 1887), notice on the Foula. Internet Archive: `nouvellegograp12recl`. US and French public domain (author d. 1905).
- J. de Crozals, *Les Peulhs: étude d'ethnologie africaine* (Paris: Maisonneuve et Leclerc, 1883). Internet Archive: `bnf-bpt6k57208690`. US public domain (published 1883). Crozals cites Mungo Park, *Travels in the Interior Districts of Africa* (1799); G. Mollien, *Voyage dans l'intérieur de l'Afrique* (1820); and J. Bayol in *Revue des Deux-Mondes*, 15 December 1882.
- Dr. Alexandre Lasnet, *Une mission au Sénégal: ethnographie, botanique, zoologie, géologie* (Paris: A. Challamel, 1900), chapter "Peuls," sections "Mœurs. Coutumes," "Accouchement," "Circoncision," "Mariage," "Funérailles." Internet Archive: `unemissionausn00lasn`. US public domain (published 1900).
- Maurice Delafosse, *Les civilisations négro-africaines* (Paris: Stock, 1925), on clan honor-names and ancestor-instituted *interdits*. UQAM Classiques. US public domain (published 1925); French public domain (author d. 1926).

**What these sources do not contain:** the later literary cosmology often retold as "Gueno / Geno created the world from a drop of milk," the ur-cow Itoori, and the initiation cycle *Koumen*. That form is twentieth-century (Amadou Hampâté Bâ and Germaine Dieterlen, *Koumen*, 1961, and related retellings). It is not public domain and is not used here. Louis Tauxier, *Mœurs et histoire des Peuls* (1937), and Henri Gaden, *Proverbes et maximes peuls* (1931), are likewise too late for this corpus.

**How to read these units:** Pulaar (*Pulaar* / *Fulfulde*) is the language of the Fulɓe (Peul, Foulah, Fulani). By the late nineteenth century most Fulɓe groups known to European observers were Muslim. Pre-Islamic practice survives in the PD record as (a) remaining pagan pastoral pockets whose only cult is the herd, (b) remnants inside Islam (herd-love, spirits at birth, green-leaf greeting of the dead, cow bridewealth, the mother's counsel, clan *yettôdé* and ancestor *interdit*). There is no indigenous written scripture analogous to a sūtra. The Pratibhā layers restore the philosophical claim and the practice; they do not adopt the observers' racial ranking, "superstition" vocabulary, or surgical detail as teaching. Where Crozals and Lasnet say "idolatry" or "fetishism," the sources themselves already correct the picture: idolatry is the exception; the remaining cult is the herd.

## The Remaining Cult Is the Herd
**Source:** Crozals, *Les Peulhs* (1883), citing Bayol, *Revue des Deux-Mondes*, 15 December 1882

"Même aujourd'hui, on trouve dans le Wassallah et le Kankan des Peulhs nomades qui n'ont qu'un culte, celui de leurs troupeaux, qu'ils font prospérer le mieux qu'ils peuvent, sans se préoccuper du lendemain."

---

### Original
Même aujourd'hui, on trouve dans le Wassallah et le Kankan des Peulhs nomades qui n'ont qu'un culte, celui de leurs troupeaux, qu'ils font prospérer le mieux qu'ils peuvent, sans se préoccuper du lendemain.

### IAST
Not applicable (French ethnographic report of Fulɓe oral practice). Key Pulaar terms later sources use, not named by Crozals: *nagge* / *na'i* (cow, cattle); *pulaaku* (the Fulɓe code of conduct). The philosophical claim of this unit is the observer's sentence itself: the remaining cult is the herd.

### Pratibha Translation
Even today, in Wassallah and Kankan, one finds nomad Peuls who have only one cult: that of their herds. They make the herds prosper as best they can, without concern for tomorrow.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is liturgical, not zoological. Crozals, compiling Park, Mollien, and Bayol, is hunting for "idolatry" among the Fulɓe and cannot find a temple. What he finds instead, among the last openly pagan nomads of the Upper Niger hinterland, is a cult with one object: the herd. "Only one cult" (*n'ont qu'un culte*) is the philosophical sentence. It does not mean that cows are carved as idols. Crozals says in the next breath that idolatry among the Peuls is an exception of the poorest and most isolated. The remaining religion is not a statue. It is a relation of care that has no other shrine.

"Without concern for tomorrow" is not improvidence as a moral failing. It is the pastoral present tense. The herd is fed, watered, moved, and protected today. Cosmology, if it exists, is not stored in a book; it is stored in the continued life of the animals. Later Fulɓe literature will name a creator and an ur-cow. These 1883 pages do not. They record what a public-domain observer could still see: where Islam has not yet replaced the old center, the center is cattle.

Existentially the teaching cuts two modern errors. One is that a people without a temple has no religion. The other is that "animism" must mean a crowded pantheon. Here the world is already full, but the cult is single. You do not collect gods. You keep the herd alive. That keeping *is* the rite.

### Key Terms
**culte des troupeaux** - "cult of the herds" -> Crozals/Bayol's name for the last pagan liturgical center -> "cattle-raising" as mere economy misses that the observers reached for a religious word because no other cult was visible.

**Peulhs nomades** - nomad Fulɓe, as opposed to the settled Muslim majority of Futa Toro and Futa Jallon -> the remaining pre-Islamic type in this source is pastoral and mobile, not a village mosque community.

**sans se préoccuper du lendemain** - "without concern for tomorrow" -> pastoral present, not fecklessness -> English "they don't plan" is a mistranslation of a life whose planning *is* the next pasture.

**boolâtrie** - Reclus/Lasnet's learned name for the same structure (next unit) -> "cow-worship" as a European -latry coinage; the field report here is plainer: one cult, the herd.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Vedic *go* and the cow as wealth-and-rite:** Both locate the sacred in living cattle rather than in a manufactured image.
*Divergence:* Vedic ritual is a named sacrifice with priests and meter; this Pulaar remainder has no temple and no recorded hymn in the PD sources — only the prospering of the herd.

**Serer first-fruits at the baobab (this corpus):** Both refuse to empty the near world for a distant God.
*Divergence:* Serer practice pours milk and millet at a tree or stone; remaining pagan Fulɓe, in this sentence, have no other cult than the animals themselves.

**Daoist wandering:** Both prefer a mobile life that does not stockpile tomorrow.
*Divergence:* Zhuāngzǐ's wandering is a freedom from fixed use; Fulɓe mobility in this source is bound to pasture and water.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Today, take one living being that depends on you — a person, an animal, a plant — and make *its* prospering the whole of the rite. Do not add a prayer on top. Do not postpone care until a better cosmology arrives. The cult is the keeping.

## The Primitive Cult Was Cattle-Worship
**Source:** Reclus, *Nouvelle géographie universelle*, t. XII (1887), Foula notice; cited by Lasnet, *Une mission au Sénégal* (1900), "Peuls"

"Le culte primitif paraît avoir été la boolâtrie, et dans leur mahométisme actuel se sont maintenues de nombreuses coutumes qui témoignent de leurs anciennes pratiques. La propreté qu'ils observent dans leurs bouveries a quelque chose de religieux."

---

### Original
Le culte primitif paraît avoir été la boolâtrie, et dans leur mahométisme actuel se sont maintenues de nombreuses coutumes qui témoignent de leurs anciennes pratiques. La propreté qu'ils observent dans leurs bouveries a quelque chose de religieux.

### IAST
Not applicable. Key term is Reclus's coinage *boolâtrie* (from Latin *bos*, ox, on the model of *idolâtrie*). Pulaar does not need this word; the practice is the clean cattle-pen and the customs that outlive conversion.

### Pratibha Translation
The primitive cult appears to have been cattle-worship, and in their present Mohammedanism many customs have been maintained that witness to their ancient practices. The cleanliness they observe in their cattle-pens has something religious about it.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is historical and diagnostic at once. Reclus does not claim to have watched a Fulɓe cow-temple. He infers a primitive cult from what Islam did not erase. That inference is the honest shape of the PD record: pre-Islamic Pulaar religion is mostly visible as remainder. Lasnet repeats the sentence almost verbatim in 1900, which tells you how thin the ethnographic file still was. There is no Gueno here, no initiation cave, no milk-cosmogony. There is a European -latry word, and then a concrete rite: the cattle-pen is kept as if it were a holy place.

"Something religious" (*quelque chose de religieux*) in the cleanliness of the *bouveries* is the philosophical hinge. Hygiene can be merely practical. Reclus sees that it is not merely practical. The pen is where the cult-object lives. To keep it clean is to keep the relation intact. Conversion to Islam changes the Friday and the prayer-direction; it does not empty the pen of its older gravity.

Existentially the teaching is about what conversion does not convert. A new confession can rename the sky and still leave the daily shrine standing. If you want the pre-Islamic Pulaar world, do not start with a reconstructed myth. Start with the place that is still treated as if it were sacred: the enclosure of the herd.

### Key Terms
**boolâtrie** - Reclus's name for a primitive cattle-cult -> a scholar's -latry, not a Pulaar autonym -> "idolatry of cows" overstates images; the next clause (clean pens, surviving customs) is the actual content.

**culte primitif** - "primitive cult" -> chronological claim: Islam is later; cattle-relation is earlier -> "primitive" in 1887 French is evolutionary ranking; the usable sense is *prior*.

**bouveries** - cattle-pens, byres -> the built form of the cult -> "barn" is too agricultural-European; this is the nomad or semi-nomad enclosure that still receives guests in Bondou, Reclus says, as a mark of respect.

**mahométisme actuel** - "present Mohammedanism" -> the overlay, not the vacuum -> the unit's point is remnant, not a claim that Fulɓe Islam is fake.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Hebrew *mishkan* and care of the holy enclosure:** Both treat the kept enclosure as a religious fact, not a shed.
*Divergence:* The tabernacle is portable because God dwells there; the Fulɓe pen is holy because the herd lives there.

**Shinto shrine sweeping:** Both locate reverence in cleanliness before any spoken doctrine.
*Divergence:* Shinto cleanliness has kami and a shrine lineage; Reclus has no named spirit of the pen — only "something religious."

**Serer refusal to address Roog directly (this corpus):** Both keep the highest from being the only practice; everyday sanctity is local.
*Divergence:* Serer locality is tree, stone, and *mammam*; Fulɓe locality in this source is the cattle-pen.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Clean one place that keeps what you live from — kitchen, stall, desk, threshold — as if the cleanliness were already a rite. Do not add incense. Notice whether the care feels merely practical or whether it has "something religious" in it. That notice is the remnant.

## The Herd Decides the Path
**Source:** Lasnet, *Une mission au Sénégal* (1900), "Peuls — Mœurs. Coutumes"

"Sans cesse au milieu de leurs troupeaux, ils les connaissent admirablement et s'en font aimer, d'ailleurs ils ont pour eux une véritable affection, plus grande souvent que pour leurs propres enfants; pour les faire abreuver ils ne se contentent pas de les conduire vers les bords les moins escarpés, mais ils leur font une route en taillant un plan incliné de façon à faciliter l'accès de la rivière; quand une vache a perdu son veau, pour que son lait ne tarisse point ils font un mannequin avec la peau de l'animal et n'hésitent pas à la téter eux-mêmes. Ce sont les besoins de leurs troupeaux qui déterminent leurs déplacements."

---

### Original
Sans cesse au milieu de leurs troupeaux, ils les connaissent admirablement et s'en font aimer, d'ailleurs ils ont pour eux une véritable affection, plus grande souvent que pour leurs propres enfants; pour les faire abreuver ils ne se contentent pas de les conduire vers les bords les moins escarpés, mais ils leur font une route en taillant un plan incliné de façon à faciliter l'accès de la rivière; quand une vache a perdu son veau, pour que son lait ne tarisse point ils font un mannequin avec la peau de l'animal et n'hésitent pas à la téter eux-mêmes. Ce sont les besoins de leurs troupeaux qui déterminent leurs déplacements.

### IAST
Not applicable. The practice-terms are French ethnographic: *troupeaux*, *plan incliné*, *mannequin* of calf-skin. Later Pulaar pastoral vocabulary (*egga*, transhumance; *wuro*, camp) is not in Lasnet; the structure is: the herd's need is the law of motion.

### Pratibha Translation
Always in the midst of their herds, they know them admirably and make themselves loved by them; they have for the animals a true affection, often greater than for their own children. To water them they do not merely lead them to the least steep banks; they cut them a road, carving a ramp so the river can be reached. When a cow has lost her calf, so that her milk will not dry they make a dummy from the animal's skin and do not hesitate to suckle her themselves. It is the needs of their herds that determine their displacements.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is cosmological in the only form the 1900 source can state it: the world is not a map on which cattle are cargo. The world is a network of pasture, water, and milk, and human motion is a function of that network. "The needs of their herds determine their displacements" is a metaphysics of path. You do not choose a destination and then bring the cows. The cows' thirst and hunger choose, and you cut the bank so they can drink.

The scandal sentence — affection often greater than for children — is colonial sensationalism and also a real ranking of care. Lasnet wants you to gasp. The philosophical reading is colder: in a pastoral ontology the herd is the continuing body of the group. Children matter; the herd is how children eat. Cutting a ramp into a riverbank is liturgy in earth. The calf-skin dummy is liturgy in skin: a refusal to let milk, which is life, stop because a particular calf has died. The dummy is not a fetish that one worships. It is a device that keeps the cow in relation. Pre-Islamic Pulaar practice, as this passage can recover it, is full of such devices. They look like husbandry. They are husbandry *as* cult.

Existentially the teaching is against a spirituality that leaves the body of the world unmanaged. If your "path" is only inward, the riverbank stays a cliff and the milk dries. Here the path is a ramp you cut with your hands.

### Key Terms
**besoins de leurs troupeaux** - "needs of their herds" -> the law of motion -> "economic necessity" is true and too thin; Lasnet places this sentence inside *mœurs*, not inside a chapter on markets.

**plan incliné** - the carved ramp to water -> a made path for the sacred-economic body of the herd -> "engineering" misses that this is how affection looks when it has tools.

**mannequin** - calf-skin dummy to keep milk flowing -> a stand-in that preserves relation after death -> not an idol; an anti-drying rite.

**affection véritable** - "true affection" -> Lasnet's word for a love that outranks even children in his ranking -> do not moralize it as cruelty to children; read it as the herd being the group's ongoing life.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Īśāvāsya Upaniṣad 1 (*īśā vāsyam idam sarvam*):** Both refuse to treat the living world as dead property.
*Divergence:* The Upaniṣad covers the world with the Lord; this passage covers the path with the herd's need.

**Christian Good Shepherd:** Both make care of the flock the image of right relation.
*Divergence:* The Gospel metaphor points past sheep to persons; Lasnet's Fulɓe point *at* the cattle as the actual center of care.

**Zhuāngzǐ's butcher Ding:** Both locate mastery in knowing a living body's grain so well that action becomes easy.
*Divergence:* Ding's ox is already for the blade; the Fulɓe ox is for continued life, milk, and motion.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Before you move today — house, job, opinion — ask what living need is actually choosing the displacement. If you can, cut one "ramp": remove one unnecessary steepness between a dependent life and water, food, rest, or you. Let the need, not the map, decide the next hour.

## Pagan Pockets Were Still Attested
**Source:** Crozals, *Les Peulhs* (1883), compiling Park (1799) and Mollien (1820)

"À la fin du XVIIIe siècle la conversion des différents groupes Peulhs n'est pas complète. Il arrive que dans un même État, à côté des Peulhs musulmans formant la masse, une minorité païenne s'est maintenue. Mungo-Park parle de ces Peulhs qui « restent attachés à leurs anciennes superstitions ». Au commencement de ce siècle, dans le Djolof, les Peulhs étaient tous païens, et, s'il faut en croire Mollien, portaient une haine violente aux musulmans. À deux journées de Timbo, le Sangarari, visité par Mollien, est alors habité par des Peulhs païens. Près de Kankoli, à l'ouest du Fouta-Djalon, le même voyageur trouve également un village de Peulhs païens."

---

### Original
À la fin du XVIIIe siècle la conversion des différents groupes Peulhs n'est pas complète. Il arrive que dans un même État, à côté des Peulhs musulmans formant la masse, une minorité païenne s'est maintenue. Mungo-Park parle de ces Peulhs qui « restent attachés à leurs anciennes superstitions ». Au commencement de ce siècle, dans le Djolof, les Peulhs étaient tous païens, et, s'il faut en croire Mollien, portaient une haine violente aux musulmans. À deux journées de Timbo, le Sangarari, visité par Mollien, est alors habité par des Peulhs païens. Près de Kankoli, à l'ouest du Fouta-Djalon, le même voyageur trouve également un village de Peulhs païens.

### IAST
Not applicable. Place-names in the sources: Djolof (Jolof, Senegal); Timbo (capital of Futa Jallon); Sangarari; Kankoli, west of Futa Jallon. Park's English, as Crozals quotes it: Peuls who "remain attached to their ancient superstitions."

### Pratibha Translation
At the end of the eighteenth century the conversion of the different Peul groups is not complete. It happens that in one and the same state, beside the Muslim Peuls who form the mass, a pagan minority has held on. Mungo Park speaks of those Peuls who "remain attached to their ancient superstitions." At the beginning of this century, in the Djolof, the Peuls were all pagan and, if Mollien is to be believed, bore a violent hatred toward Muslims. Two days from Timbo, Sangarari, visited by Mollien, is then inhabited by pagan Peuls. Near Kankoli, west of Futa Jallon, the same traveler also finds a village of pagan Peuls.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is historical before it is doctrinal: pre-Islamic Pulaar practice is not a hypothesis projected backward from twentieth-century myth. It is a demographic fact in the travel literature of 1799–1820, still summarized in 1883. Conversion is incomplete. In one state two Fulɓe populations coexist. The Jolof Peuls are reported as entirely pagan around 1800. Pagan villages stand within two days of Timbo, the theocratic capital of Futa Jallon. Reclus will add, for the Upper Benue, tribes "who have remained pagan and whose men still keep their herds."

Park's word "superstitions" is contempt and should not be adopted as teaching. What it marks is attachment to an older cult that Islam had not replaced. Mollien's "violent hatred" of Muslims, if accurate, is the political edge of that attachment in a century of Fulɓe jihads: the pagan pastoral remnant is not merely leftover custom; it is a party. Crozals's larger book is about Muslim Fulɓe empire. This paragraph is his admission that the empire did not cover the whole people.

Existentially the teaching is against the myth of total conversion. A tradition is allowed to have a remainder. The remainder is where the older ontology is still practiced rather than remembered. For this corpus, that remainder is the warrant for the other units: cattle-cult, spirit-warding, green-leaf greeting, clan *interdit*. They are not "folklore inside Islam" only. They are what the last pagans were still doing, and what Muslims still do without calling it a second religion.

### Key Terms
**minorité païenne** - "pagan minority" -> Crozals's demographic name for Fulɓe who have not taken Islam -> "animist" is Delafosse's better later word; "fetishist" is Lasnet's worse one.

**anciennes superstitions** - Park via Crozals: "ancient superstitions" -> the older cult under a Protestant traveler's insult -> the usable content is *ancient* and *attached*, not *superstition*.

**Djolof** - Jolof, Senegal -> a Wolof-dominated country where Fulɓe herders are reported still pagan ca. 1800 -> proof that Senegambian Fulɓe religion is not identical with Futa Toro Islam.

**Timbo / Fouta-Djalon** - the Muslim imamate's capital, with pagan Peul villages two days away -> the map of remainder: not far from the mosque, the old cult still has a village.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Late antique pagans beside Christian empire:** Both show conversion as a patchwork inside one people, not a clean replacement.
*Divergence:* Mediterranean paganism left temples and texts; Pulaar paganism in these sources leaves herds, villages, and hostility, not a written liturgy.

**Japanese *shinbutsu* (kami practice beside Buddhism):** Both keep an older local cult under or beside a later universal religion.
*Divergence:* Japan institutionalizes the coexistence; Crozals's Fulɓe coexistence is still conflictual (Mollien's "hatred").

**Serer resistance to conversion in Lasnet (this corpus):** Both Senegambian cases show Islam as incomplete overlay.
*Divergence:* Serer sources in this corpus still describe Roog, *mammam*, and baobab libation as a living public religion; Fulɓe paganism is already a minority report.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Name one older practice in your own life that a later "conversion" — religious, professional, or intellectual — did not actually erase. Do not despise it as leftover superstition. Keep it for one day as the remainder that tells the truth about what you still serve.

## Bridewealth Is Always Named in Cows
**Source:** Lasnet, *Une mission au Sénégal* (1900), "Peuls — Mariage"

"La dot est proportionnelle à la fortune du mari et l'honore d'autant qu'elle est plus élevée : pour une jeune fille, elle peut aller jusqu'à quinze vaches, à dix pour une femme déjà mariée; cette dot est toujours exprimée en vaches."

---

### Original
La dot est proportionnelle à la fortune du mari et l'honore d'autant qu'elle est plus élevée : pour une jeune fille, elle peut aller jusqu'à quinze vaches, à dix pour une femme déjà mariée; cette dot est toujours exprimée en vaches.

### IAST
Not applicable. Pulaar later names bridewealth *sadaaki* (from Arabic *ṣadāq*) when the frame is Islamic; Lasnet's ethnographic fact is that the unit of honor remains the cow even when payment is made in silver, cloth, or sheep.

### Pratibha Translation
The bridewealth is proportional to the husband's fortune and honors him the more as it is higher: for a young woman it may go as far as fifteen cows, ten for a woman already married. This bridewealth is always expressed in cows.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is that cattle are not only food and motion; they are the language of honor in alliance. Lasnet is describing marriage "regulated by the laws of the Qur'an," with a marabout reciting prayers. Inside that Islamic frame the dowry-word is still bovine. You may pay in money, sheep, or cloth, but you *name* the gift in cows. The pre-Islamic measure survives as the unit of speech. That is how a cult remains after the temple is gone: it becomes the currency of respect.

"Honors him the more as it is higher" is a ranking that can become vanity. The philosophical core is prior to the vanity. A people whose primitive cult was the herd will measure a binding of families in the same beings that determine the path. Fifteen cows is not a price-tag on a woman in the market sense Lasnet's "achetée" wants. It is the herd acknowledging a new household. The cow is the sentence in which the group says: this alliance is real.

Existentially the teaching is about what you still count when you think you have changed religions. If your sacred is cattle, your contracts will speak cow even on a mosque day. Ask what unit *you* always express things in — money, credentials, time, attention. That unit is your remaining cult.

### Key Terms
**dot ... toujours exprimée en vaches** - "bridewealth always expressed in cows" -> the cow as unit of speech, even when payment is substituted -> "dowry" in the European sense (goods the bride brings) is the wrong direction; this is what the husband-side gives, named in cattle.

**l'honore** - "honors him" -> bridewealth as honor, not only transfer -> *pulaaku* later names honor-shame; Lasnet has the structure without the word.

**quinze vaches** - the upper figure for a previously unmarried woman -> a number that makes the herd-cult countable -> do not treat the number as a shopping price; treat it as how large a piece of the sacred herd is pledged.

**vaches** - cows, specifically, not generic livestock -> milk-life, not meat-wealth only -> substituting sheep or cloth is allowed; substituting the *name* is not.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Vedic bride-gift and cattle as *dakṣiṇā*:** Both make cattle the measurable form of a sacred bond.
*Divergence:* Vedic cattle-gift is priestly and hymnic; Fulɓe cattle-gift here is affinal and remains so under Qur'anic law.

**Aristotle on *timē* (honor):** Both treat honor as something that must be publicly measured.
*Divergence:* Greek honor is civic and martial; this honor is pastoral and nuptial.

**Serer first-fruits (this corpus):** Both offer living produce as the true gift, not a token.
*Divergence:* Serer first-fruits go to spirits at tree and stone; Fulɓe cows go to the wife's people as the named measure of the bond.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Take one obligation you owe a person — thanks, help, repair. Name it in the unit that is actually sacred to you, not in the unit that is convenient. Then give a piece of that, not a substitute that keeps the name empty.

## Ward the Spirits Until the Seventh Day
**Source:** Lasnet, *Une mission au Sénégal* (1900), "Peuls — Accouchement"

"Comme chez les autres musulmans le baptême a lieu le septième jour après la naissance et la mère porte jusque-là un poignard destiné à écarter les mauvais esprits."

---

### Original
Comme chez les autres musulmans le baptême a lieu le septième jour après la naissance et la mère porte jusque-là un poignard destiné à écarter les mauvais esprits.

### IAST
Not applicable. Lasnet's "baptême" is the Islamic naming on the seventh day (*simmol* / *innde* in later Pulaar usage). The remnant is the dagger against *mauvais esprits* — evil spirits — worn until that naming.

### Pratibha Translation
As among other Muslims, the naming-baptism takes place on the seventh day after birth, and until then the mother wears a dagger meant to keep off the evil spirits.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is that the week between birth and name is a spiritually open interval, and that Islam's seventh-day naming did not abolish the older danger, only scheduled its close. Lasnet calls the ceremony a baptism because he is writing for French readers. It is the Muslim name-giving. Beside it, not instead of it, the mother is armed. A dagger is a poor tool against a spirit if you think in mosque categories. It is a precise tool if you think, as the remnant does, that the unnamed child and the newly delivered mother are exposed to persons who are not flesh.

This is the same structure Delafosse names for West African animism generally: the near world is populated by free souls that can act, and the living take measures. The measure here is metal worn on the body. The seventh day does not only confer a name; it ends a vigil. Pre-Islamic Pulaar practice, in the only form this source can show it at the cradle, is apotropaic: keep the spirits off until the child is spoken into the human group.

Existentially the teaching is that beginnings are not safe by default. A birth is not yet a belonging. Belonging waits on a rite, and until the rite the world is leaky. You do not have to accept Lasnet's dagger as your object. You do have to notice which of your own beginnings you treat as spiritually unguarded, as if a new life, a new love, or a new work needed no vigil.

### Key Terms
**mauvais esprits** - "evil spirits" -> Lasnet's French for harmful non-human persons around birth -> not the Islamic *jinn* as a learned category, and not a psychology of "bad mood"; a populated interval between birth and name.

**poignard** - dagger worn by the mother until day seven -> apotropaic metal -> "weapon" as crime-tool is the wrong picture; this is a ward, like an amulet that happens to be a blade.

**septième jour** - seventh-day naming, Islamic in Lasnet's framing -> the scheduled end of exposure -> the remnant is not the naming (Muslim) but the need for a ward *until* the naming (older).

**baptême** - Lasnet's Christian word for the naming feast -> do not import original sin; import only the public reception of the child into named life.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Greek *amphidromia* and Roman name-day:** Both treat the days after birth as incomplete belonging.
*Divergence:* Classical rites walk the child or purify the house; this remnant arms the mother.

**Jewish *brit* on the eighth day:** Both close an interval with a public religious act.
*Divergence:* Circumcision-as-covenant is not this unit (and is not taught here); the Pulaar seventh day in Lasnet is naming, with a spirit-ward beside it.

**Serer *mammam* (this corpus):** Both populate the near world with addressable, dangerous powers.
*Divergence:* Serer address the spirits with libation; this Fulɓe remnant drives them off with a blade until the name is given.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
If something in your life is newly born — a project, a recovery, a household — keep a seven-day vigil. Do not announce it as finished on day one. Each day, perform one small warding act: a boundary, a silence, a refusal of premature display. On the seventh day, give it a name out loud.

## Honor Is Impassibility
**Source:** Lasnet, *Une mission au Sénégal* (1900), "Peuls — Circoncision"

"Pendant toute l'opération les jeunes gens doivent conserver l'impassibilité la plus grande; il y aurait déshonneur à se plaindre. [...] Le soir, ils écoutent les légendes et les fables de leurs gardiens."

---

### Original
Pendant toute l'opération les jeunes gens doivent conserver l'impassibilité la plus grande; il y aurait déshonneur à se plaindre. Le soir, ils écoutent les légendes et les fables de leurs gardiens.

### IAST
Not applicable. Later Fulɓe name the ethic *pulaaku* (Fulɓe-ness: reserve, endurance, shame-honor). Lasnet does not use that word. He records the visible rule: impassibility, and the seclusion-school of legends told by elder guardians.

### Pratibha Translation
Through the whole operation the young men must keep the greatest impassibility; it would be dishonor to complain. In the evening they listen to the legends and fables of their guardians.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is ethical before it is anatomical. Lasnet's chapter is a colonial medical description of circumcision; this unit refuses the surgical content and keeps the philosophical rule that the description accidentally recorded. Pain is not the teaching. The teaching is that a public ordeal is the place where honor is either kept or lost, and that the keeping looks like stillness. Dishonor is not the cut. Dishonor is the complaint.

This is as close as the PD ethnography comes to *pulaaku* without naming it. Reserve, self-mastery, the refusal to make inner weather into noise: Lasnet sees it on the initiation ground and also, elsewhere, in the "continual gravity" of the adult Peul who rarely dances. The evening of legends is the other half of initiation. Impassibility without transmission would be only toughness. The guardians tell the stories that make the boys into a people. Pre-Islamic and Islamic Fulɓe both circumcise; what the remnant shares with the older world is not the knife but the school: seclusion, elder speech, a body that must not betray itself.

Existentially the teaching is not "seek pain." It is: when you are already in a necessary difficulty, do not add the second wound of display. Keep the face. Then, in the evening, listen. Initiation that is only endurance is brutality. Initiation that is endurance plus story is how a tradition reproduces.

### Key Terms
**impassibilité** - impassibility, the face that does not leak pain -> honor as composure -> "stoicism" is a fair cross-tradition gloss if you remember it is public and communal here, not a private Roman notebook.

**déshonneur** - dishonor in complaining -> shame as social ontology, not mere etiquette -> later *pulaaku* / *semteende* (reserve, shame); Lasnet has the sanction without the Pulaar word.

**légendes et fables** - legends and fables told at night by guardians -> oral curriculum of seclusion -> the PD sources do not transcribe these stories; they only attest that initiation is also hearing.

**gardiens** - elder guardians of the seclusion-house -> transmitters, not only nurses of wounds -> the philosophical office is to speak the people into the next generation.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Stoic *apatheia*:** Both treat the uncomplaining face as a form of freedom.
*Divergence:* Stoic impassibility is an inward judgment about what is "up to us"; Fulɓe impassibility here is honor before witnesses.

**Spartan and Masai ordeal-ethics:** Both make a youth's public endurance a gate into adult belonging.
*Divergence:* Those comparanda have their own liturgies; this unit's second clause — night legends — is the specifically traditional half, not the toughness half.

**Kashmir Śaiva *vīra* (heroic) posture:** Both demand a steadiness that does not flinch.
*Divergence:* The tantric hero's steadiness is in consciousness amid appearance; this steadiness is in a social body that must not shame the group.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Choose one necessary difficulty you are already in. For one day, do not advertise it and do not complain of it. In the evening, listen to someone older tell a story — live, if you can — as if endurance without transmission were incomplete.

## Greet the Dead with Green Leaves
**Source:** Lasnet, *Une mission au Sénégal* (1900), "Peuls — Funérailles"

"Les Peuls ont, comme tous les Musulmans, beaucoup de respect pour les morts ; profaner une tombe est un sacrilège et chaque fois que des Peuls en voyage rencontrent une tombe ou un cimetière, ils jettent en passant une poignée d'herbes ou de feuilles vertes, sorte de salut qu'ils adressent aux trépassés."

---

### Original
Les Peuls ont, comme tous les Musulmans, beaucoup de respect pour les morts ; profaner une tombe est un sacrilège et chaque fois que des Peuls en voyage rencontrent une tombe ou un cimetière, ils jettent en passant une poignée d'herbes ou de feuilles vertes, sorte de salut qu'ils adressent aux trépassés.

### IAST
Not applicable. The rite is a handful of green grass or leaves thrown in passing: a *salut* to the dead. Lasnet frames it as Muslim respect; the offering itself is forage — the same green the herd lives on.

### Pratibha Translation
The Peuls have, like all Muslims, much respect for the dead. To profane a tomb is a sacrilege, and every time Peuls on a journey meet a tomb or a cemetery, they throw as they pass a handful of grass or green leaves, a kind of greeting they address to those who have gone over.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is that the dead remain addressable, and that the correct address from a pastoral people is green plant-matter thrown in passing. Lasnet wants this to be generic Islam: respect, sacrilege, tombs. The specific gesture is not generic. A handful of *herbes* or *feuilles vertes* is what you would give a living herd. The greeting to the dead is forage. The ancestor is still, in the remnant logic, a being who can be met on the road and who understands the gift of grass.

"In passing" (*en passant*) matters. This is not a festival of the dead and not a sacrifice that stops the caravan. Mobility continues; relation continues inside mobility. Nomad respect cannot always be a vigil at a fixed grave. It can be a toss of green as you go by. The dead do not require you to become sedentary. They require that you not pass them as if they were stones.

Existentially the teaching is a correction to both amnesia and ostentation. You do not have to build a shrine at every loss. You do have to refuse to walk past the dead as scenery. Give them something living and green, and keep moving. That is pastoral piety.

### Key Terms
**poignée d'herbes ou de feuilles vertes** - handful of grass or green leaves -> forage as greeting -> "flowers on a grave" is the nearest European picture and still too garden; this is pasture thrown to the dead.

**salut** - greeting, salute -> the dead are met as persons on the road -> "offering" is true; "greeting" is more exact: you acknowledge, you do not bargain.

**trépassés** - those who have passed over -> the dead as having gone a way, not as having become nothing -> the same root of passage as the travelers who greet them.

**en voyage** - on a journey -> the rite belongs to mobility -> a sedentary memorial mass is another religion's tempo.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Serer speaking into the ear of the dead (this corpus):** Both refuse to treat the corpse-side of the world as mute.
*Divergence:* Serer speech is intimate and at the body; Fulɓe greeting is a toss of green from the road.

**Japanese *ohaka-mairi* and roadside *jizō*:** Both let travel include a gesture to the dead without ending the journey.
*Divergence:* Japanese gesture is often water, incense, or stone; here the matter is grass.

**Homeric *choai* (drink-offerings to the dead):** Both give the dead a portion of the living world's goods.
*Divergence:* Greek libation is wine and blood at a pit; this portion is pasture.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
On one walk today, when you pass a cemetery, a roadside memorial, or even a tree that marks a death you know, do not pass as if it were scenery. Stop long enough to set down something green — a leaf, a blade of grass. Greet, do not speechify, and go on.

## Consult the Mother Before a Great Act
**Source:** Lasnet, *Une mission au Sénégal* (1900), "Peuls — Mariage"

"La femme peule est beaucoup plus considérée que chez les autres peuplades du Sénégal, elle exerce souvent un grand ascendant sur son mari [...] elle est respectée et très écoutée de ses enfants, un Peul n'accomplit jamais une action importante sans consulter sa mère."

---

### Original
La femme peule est beaucoup plus considérée que chez les autres peuplades du Sénégal, elle exerce souvent un grand ascendant sur son mari et, malgré la latitude que donne à celui-ci le Coran, il est rare qu'elle laisse pénétrer une seconde femme dans sa case; elle est respectée et très écoutée de ses enfants, un Peul n'accomplit jamais une action importante sans consulter sa mère.

### IAST
Not applicable. The practice-term is French *consulter sa mère*. Later Fulɓe social language names the mother's people and the strength of the maternal line; Lasnet records the decision-rule: no important act without her.

### Pratibha Translation
The Peul woman is held in much higher regard than among the other peoples of Senegal. She often exerts a great ascendancy over her husband, and despite the latitude the Qur'an gives him it is rare that she lets a second wife into her house. She is respected and closely listened to by her children. A Peul never accomplishes an important action without consulting his mother.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is a decision-procedure, not a compliment to "the status of women." Lasnet is comparing colonial ethnic files. Strip the ranking. What remains is a rule of counsel: before a great act, ask the mother. Islam's legal polygamy is in the same paragraph and is described as practically checked by the wife. The deeper remnant is maternal consultation as a condition of action. Authority is not only the almamy, the marabout, or the *ardo*. Authority is the woman from whom one was born, still speaking.

In a cattle-people this is not a sentimental exception to patriarchy; it is how a mobile household keeps a memory that is not written. The mother is the one who has seen the herd, the marriages, the failures. To act without her is to act without the household's archive. Pre-Islamic West African social philosophy, as Delafosse will say of clans generally, runs through ancestors. This sentence makes the living mother the nearest ancestor who can still answer.

Existentially the teaching is against heroic solitude. An "important action" that cannot survive being told to one's mother is probably vanity. Consultation is not permission-as-infantilism. It is the test that the act belongs to a line and not only to a mood.

### Key Terms
**consulter sa mère** - consult one's mother -> the condition of a great act -> "ask mom" as joke misses that Lasnet says *never* (*jamais*) and *important action*.

**ascendant** - the wife's ascendancy over the husband -> practical authority inside a Qur'anic frame that legally favors the man -> remnant: the house is not the Qur'an's diagram.

**très écoutée** - closely listened to -> children as a listening body -> the opposite of the colonial cartoon of the silent African woman.

**action importante** - important action -> the threshold at which counsel becomes mandatory -> daily acts may be free; founding acts are not.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Confucian filial consultation:** Both make the parent a living source of right action.
*Divergence:* Confucian counsel is often paternal and ritualized as *xiao*; this sentence specifies the mother.

**Delphic counsel before a great act:** Both refuse to let a founding decision be merely private.
*Divergence:* Delphi is an oracle of the god; here the oracle is the mother.

**Serer household genius (this corpus):** Both keep the house as a spiritually thick place, not a male club.
*Divergence:* Serer thickness is a *génie de la case*; Fulɓe thickness in this unit is a speaking woman.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Before one important action this week, consult a living elder woman in your line — mother, aunt, teacher — and tell her the act in plain speech. Do not perform the consultation as theater. Change the act if her hearing finds it thinner than you thought.

## The Clan Honor-Name and the Ancestor's Interdit
**Source:** Delafosse, *Les civilisations négro-africaines* (1925), on clan names and *interdits*; Peul *yettôdé*, Dialloubé and Hanhanbé

"De l'accord unanime des indigènes interrogés à cet égard, il résulte que c'est l'ancêtre du clan qui aurait institué ce nom et cet interdit et aurait solennellement transmis l'un et l'autre à tous ses descendants à venir. [...] on opposera, par exemple, chez les Peuls Dialloubé aux Hanhanbé c'est-à-dire les gens du clan Diallo aux gens du clan Kan. [...] les diverses langues en usage dans l'Afrique noire désignent en général le nom de clan par une expression que l'on pourrait traduire par « terme honorifique » ou « titre de noblesse », comme diamou (ce qui grandit) en mandingue ou yettôdé (ce qui honore) en peul. [...] L'interdit a été institué par l'ancêtre en même temps que le nom de clan."

---

### Original
De l'accord unanime des indigènes interrogés à cet égard, il résulte que c'est l'ancêtre du clan qui aurait institué ce nom et cet interdit et aurait solennellement transmis l'un et l'autre à tous ses descendants à venir. Ailleurs, par exemple chez les Peuls, on opposera les Dialloubé aux Hanhanbé, c'est-à-dire les gens du clan Diallo aux gens du clan Kan. Les diverses langues en usage dans l'Afrique noire désignent en général le nom de clan par une expression que l'on pourrait traduire par « terme honorifique » ou « titre de noblesse », comme diamou (ce qui grandit) en mandingue ou yettôdé (ce qui honore) en peul. L'interdit a été institué par l'ancêtre en même temps que le nom de clan.

### IAST
Not applicable. Key Pulaar term: *yettôdé* (Delafosse: "ce qui honore," that which honors — the clan honor-name). Clan examples: Dialloubé (Diallo) and Hanhanbé (Kan). The paired institution is the *interdit* (avoidance, often an animal, plant, or object the ancestor bound the descendants not to harm or use).

### Pratibha Translation
By the unanimous agreement of those questioned on this point, it is the ancestor of the clan who instituted this name and this prohibition and solemnly transmitted both to all descendants to come. Among the Peuls, for example, one opposes the Dialloubé to the Hanhanbé — the people of clan Diallo to the people of clan Kan. The languages of Black Africa generally call the clan name by an expression one might translate as "honorific term" or "title of nobility," such as *diamou* ("that which enlarges") in Manding or *yettôdé* ("that which honors") in Peul. The prohibition was instituted by the ancestor at the same time as the clan name.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is that identity is a double gift from an ancestor: a name that honors, and a prohibition that binds. Delafosse is writing a pan-West-African theory; he pauses on the Fulɓe because they illustrate the structure without always wearing the clan name as a surname in daily address. You still say Dialloubé against Hanhanbé when you need to name a people. *Yettôdé* means "that which honors." To call someone by the clan name is to say they have known ancestors — "birth," Delafosse says. The *interdit* is not a random food taboo. It is usually the species that saved the ancestor, or the object bound up with the founding escape. You do not eat or harm what saved the one who made you a people.

This is the pre-Islamic Fulɓe social cosmology that the cattle-cult units do not yet reach. Herds explain motion, milk, and bridewealth. Clan *interdit* explains why the human group is not just a herd of humans. An ancestor acted, was saved, named the name, and laid a forever-avoidance on the descendants. Islam can add a *silsila* of marabouts; it does not have to erase Diallo and Kan. The remainder here is kinship as liturgy.

Existentially the teaching is that you are not a self-invented individual with preferences. You are a descendant under a name and under a refusal. The refusal is the more philosophical half. Anyone can wear a proud name. The *interdit* is the name's cost: there is a living kind you will not destroy, because your ancestor's life came through it.

### Key Terms
**yettôdé** - Pulaar "that which honors"; the clan honor-name -> not a nickname and not a European surname -> to address someone by it is homage; Delafosse says you thereby affirm that the person has known ancestors.

**interdit** - ancestor-instituted avoidance (animal, plant, object, sometimes another human group) -> often the helper-species of the founding story -> "taboo" is usable if you keep the ancestor as legislator; "totem" is what Delafosse warns against, because the clan is not always named after the respected animal.

**Dialloubé / Hanhanbé** - people of Diallo vs people of Kan -> Fulɓe clan as collective, even when the name is not stacked on the personal name in daily speech.

**ancêtre du clan** - the clan ancestor who instituted name and prohibition together -> the legislator of identity -> without this figure the honor-name is just branding.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Israelite tribes and food laws:** Both bind a people by ancestor-given name plus prohibition.
*Divergence:* Torah prohibitions are theistic and written; clan *interdit* is ancestral and oral, often tied to a rescue-story of a particular species.

**Serer *lâ-siri* / ancestor–land fusion (this corpus, Delafosse):** Both make the dead the ongoing law of the living group.
*Divergence:* Serer fusion in that unit is ancestor-and-land; Fulɓe *interdit* is ancestor-and-species (or plant, or object).

**Confucian surname and ancestral hall:** Both treat the name as honor that must be lived up to.
*Divergence:* The Chinese name is patrilineal ritual with tablets; *yettôdé* is a spoken honorific whose partner-institution is an avoidance, not a tablet.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Name one prohibition you actually live by that you did not invent — a food you will not eat, a cruelty you will not do, a place you will not spoil — because someone before you bound you. Keep it today on purpose, as if the ancestor were still the legislator. If you cannot name any such prohibition, you are living as if you had no *yettôdé*.

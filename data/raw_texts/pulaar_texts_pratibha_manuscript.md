# Pratibha - Pulaar Texts (Futa Toro)
**Corpus entry:** Pulaar / Poular literary texts from Futa Toro, written by literate speakers in Ajami and published in Latin transcription
**Primary source (public domain):** Henri Gaden, *Le Poular: dialecte peul du Fouta sénégalais*, t. I, *Étude morphologique. Textes* (Paris: E. Leroux, 1913). Internet Archive: `le-poular-etude-morphologique-textes`. US public domain (published 1913). French public domain (author d. 1939; 70 years p.m.a.).

Gaden did not invent these pieces. He asked Futa literates accustomed to writing Pulaar in Arabic script to compose or dictate; he prints their Pulaar and a facing French version. Named authors in this selection include Mahmadou Alfa and Tyerno Aoudi.

**What this collection is:** the earliest substantial *public-domain* body of Pulaar as a language of teaching — tale formula, a father's dying counsel, and *mallol* (proverbs / proverbial locutions). The dialect is Futa Toro (Senegal river valley), which Gaden calls *poulàr*.

**What it is not:** the later Gueno / *Koumen* cosmology (Hampâté Bâ 1961, not PD); Gaden's own *Proverbes et maximes peuls* (1931, US PD only in 2027); Mohammadou Aliou Tyam's qasida *La vie d'El Hadj Omar* (Gaden 1935, not PD). Reichardt 1876 printed Fulde traditions, but the OCR is too broken to use as base text; Guiraudon 1894 re-edited some of that material as a grammar specimen.

**Orthography:** Gaden's Latin transcription of Futa Pulaar, cleaned from the 1913 plate (long vowels marked). This is not modern Pulaar spelling (which uses ɓ ɗ ƴ ŋ). The IAST layer notes the modern correspondences where they matter.

## What Was Here, What Will Be, What Will Not Be
**Source:** Gaden, *Le Poular* (1913), "Contes populaires," opening formula of tale 10 (and of the subsequent tales)

won ko wonnō do, ina wona wonatā, ko tindol.

---

### Original
won ko wonnō do, ina wona wonatā, ko tindol.

### IAST
Futa Toro Pulaar in Gaden's 1913 Latin transcription. *won* is the verbal root of existence; *wonnō do* "what was here"; *wona* "what will be"; *wonatā* "what will not be"; *tindol* (rac. *tin*, to hear) "a thing for hearing" — a tale.

### Pratibha Translation
This is what was here; it will be, or it will not be. It is a tale — a thing to be heard.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is ontological before it is narrative. Gaden notes that European "once upon a time" is only the past. Futa Pulaar refuses that thin tense. *won* names existence without locating it. The facts were here formerly (*wonnō do*). Perhaps they will happen later (*wona*). Perhaps they will never happen (*wonatā*). The telling exists because an audience is here that wants to hear (*tindol*). Time is not the container of the teaching. Hearing is.

This is how a pastoral-Islamic people, whose pre-Islamic cult the ethnographers could not find as a book, still philosophize in their own language. The tale is not a lie about the past. It is a mode of existence offered to listeners. Gaden adds that a Futa tale usually ends with a moral. The formula already contains the method: do not ask first whether this "happened." Ask whether you can hear it.

Existentially the sentence trains a discipline against both historicism and fantasy. You do not need the event to have been dated. You need an ear. A teaching that cannot survive *wonatā* — "it will not be" — was only news. A teaching that can be heard whether or not it occurs is already a practice.

### Key Terms
**won** - verbal root of existence, without tense -> not "to be" as copula only; the tale's events *exist* in a tenseless field -> English "there was" flattens the formula into a past anecdote.

**wonnō do / wona / wonatā** - was here / will be / will not be -> three modalities of the same root -> the teaching is not located in one of them; it is the hearing that holds all three.

**tindol** - "thing for hearing," the tale as audible object (rac. *tin* hear) -> "story" is too literary; "folktale" is too folkloric; this is existence offered to an ear.

**ko** - relative / focusing particle: "it is (that which)..." -> Gaden: *ko* often stands before a noun to specify it.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Zhuāngzǐ's "once there was" parables:** Both use a narrative that is not bound to a dated event.
*Divergence:* Zhuāngzǐ's wanderings dissolve the need for a moral; Futa *tindol* usually ends in one.

**Upaniṣadic *asti* / *nāsti*:** Both refuse to trap the real in a single tense of being or non-being.
*Divergence:* The Upaniṣad aims at the Self; *tindol* aims at an audience.

**Heraclitus on listening (*akousai*):** Both make hearing, not seeing a fact, the gate of the teaching.
*Divergence:* Heraclitus' *logos* is one; Futa tales are many, each opened by the same formula.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Today, tell one true thing as if it were a *tindol*: it was here, it may be, it may never be. Do not argue its date. Speak it to one living ear. If no one can hear it, it is not yet a teaching.

## A Father's Counsel
**Source:** Gaden, *Le Poular* (1913), tale 15, "Les conseils d'un père"; Pulaar by a Futa author, French by Gaden

mido rēntinma gede: neno wonā dādīdo, lāmdo wonā gidirādo, lembel wonā debbo, dutten wonā biddo; wata woppu bibbe bāmma nanga bibbe yumma, sabu bi bāmma ina yidanma maide, kono yidanāma koyēra.

---

### Original
mido rēntinma gede: neno wonā dādīdo, lāmdo wonā gidirādo, lembel wonā debbo, dutten wonā biddo; wata woppu bibbe bāmma nanga bibbe yumma, sabu bi bāmma ina yidanma maide, kono yidanāma koyēra.

### IAST
Gaden's Futa Pulaar. *neno* (rac. *nen*, to flatter): a person of an artisan or griot caste, who lives by praise. *lāmdo*: king / chief. *lembel*: second wife. *dutten*: stepson. *koyēra*: shame, dishonor — the Fulɓe hinge that later sources name inside *pulaaku*.

### Pratibha Translation
I am going to put you on guard against some things: a flatterer is not a companion; a king is not a comrade; a second wife is not a wife; a stepson is not a son. Do not abandon your father's children to take your mother's children, because your father's children will wish death for you, but they will not wish shame for you.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is a map of false nearness. Four relations look like intimacy and are not: the *neno* who praises you, the king who rules you, the second wife, the stepson. Then a fifth, harder: even blood splits. Father's children and mother's children are not the same loyalty. The scandal is the last clause. Agnatic kin may wish you dead. They will not wish you shamed. Death is a fact. Shame is the destruction of the name.

This is Pulaar ethics in Pulaar, not a French report that the Peul "consults his mother." The dying father speaks *koyēra*. Honor is the thing kin will not spend even on an enemy-brother. The rest of Gaden's tale is a plot that proves the counsel by violating it. The unit keeps the counsel. Practice is the list, not the plot twist.

Existentially: name the relations you have mistaken for companionship. A patron is not a friend. A second arrangement is not the first. And when you must choose a loyalty, ask not who loves you, but who would refuse your shame.

### Key Terms
**neno** - flatterer of artisan/griot caste (rac. *nen*) -> not "griot" as musician only; the warning is structural: one who lives by your praise cannot be your *dādīdo* (companion).

**koyēra** - shame, dishonor -> the worse-than-death in this sentence -> later *semteende* / *pulaaku*; Gaden's 1913 text already has the ranking.

**bibbe bāmma / bibbe yumma** - father's children / mother's children -> split lineage inside one household -> "siblings" in English hides the fork the father is pointing at.

**wonā** - "is not" -> four negations of false equivalence -> the counsel is not advice to be cold; it is a refusal to name the wrong thing as the right thing.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Confucian rectification of names:** Both insist a king is not a comrade and a second is not a first.
*Divergence:* Confucius aims at ritual-political order; this father aims at a son's survival inside caste and polygyny.

**The previous Pulaar Tradition unit "Consult the Mother":** Both take kinship counsel as a condition of action.
*Divergence:* Lasnet's ethnography is French *about* Pulaar life; this unit is Pulaar speech.

**Ecclesiasticus / Proverbs on false friends:** Both warn that proximity is not loyalty.
*Divergence:* Biblical wisdom is theistic gnomic verse; this is a dying man's list, then a tale.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Write four names you have been using wrongly: someone you called friend who is a patron, someone you called kin who is a faction. For one day, use the accurate word. Do not abandon anyone. Stop lying about the relation.

## Everything Is Done by a Cause
**Source:** Gaden, *Le Poular* (1913), proverb 66

hüde fuf, ko sababu wadata.

---

### Original
hüde fuf, ko sababu wadata.

### IAST
*hüde fuf*: every thing / affair. *sababu* (from Arabic *sabab*): cause, motive. *wadata*: does it, makes it happen.

### Pratibha Translation
Every affair — it is a cause that does it.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is causal without being mechanical. Nothing just occurs. A *sababu* does it. Gaden places this as the first of his "proverbes et locutions proverbiales," and the next proverb rhymes it: water is not salty without a reason. Futa Pulaar, already deep in Islam, uses an Arabic loan for cause and a Pulaar verb for the doing. The world is not a pile of accidents. It is a field of reasons, including reasons people will not admit.

This is not yet Western determinism. *Sababu* in Sahelian usage includes motive, pretext, the hidden why, the social cause. To say *ko sababu wadata* is to refuse both "God did it, so do not look" and "it just happened." Look for the cause. The looking is the practice.

Existentially: stop calling your pattern bad luck. Name the *sababu*. If you cannot name one, you are not done looking. The proverb does not say the cause is always just. It says there is one.

### Key Terms
**hüde** - thing, affair, matter -> not a physical object only; a happening -> "everything" in English is too cosmic; this is every *case*.

**sababu** - cause, reason (Arabic *sabab* in Pulaar) -> loanword as conceptual tool -> "reason" in the philosophical sense, not "excuse" only, though pretext is included.

**wadata** - does / makes (it) -> the cause is an agent-like doer -> English "is caused" is too passive.

**fuf** - all, every -> totality of affairs, not of atoms.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Aristotle on *aitia*:** Both refuse uncaused happenings.
*Divergence:* Aristotle's four causes are a technical system; *sababu* is a proverb's one word for why.

**Qur'anic *sabab*:** Both inherit the Arabic root.
*Divergence:* The Qur'an can mean a cord, a way, a means to heaven; Futa usage here is everyday causality.

**Heraclitus' hidden harmony:** Both say the presented event is not the whole story.
*Divergence:* Heraclitus hides a *logos*; this proverb demands you find a *sababu*.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Take one thing that "just happened" this week. Do not stop at luck or fate. Name one *sababu* you can actually point to — a choice, a hunger, a fear, a word. If the first name is false, look again.

## A Small Act Returns a Small Act
**Source:** Gaden, *Le Poular* (1913), proverb 68

badel fuf ko battel.

---

### Original
badel fuf ko battel.

### IAST
*badel*: a small action (diminutive). *battel*: a small action in return. Gaden: the law of talion, including good for good.

### Pratibha Translation
Every small act is a small act in return.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is reciprocity at the scale where people pretend nothing counts. Not only blood-feud and not only royal justice: *badel*, a little deed, is already *battel*, a little deed coming back. Gaden glosses talion, and then the more important half: one renders good for good and, especially, evil for evil. The proverb's compactness is the teaching. There is no leftover smallness that does not re-enter the world.

This is Pulaar social physics. The cattle-world of the ethnography already lived it: bridewealth named in cows, green leaves tossed to the dead. Here the language itself says that action is echo. You do not get to do a small cruelty as if it evaporated. You also do not get to do a small kindness as if it were nothing.

Existentially: stop storing petty acts in a bin marked "doesn't matter." They matter in the diminutive. That is their whole size, and they return at that size.

### Key Terms
**badel** - small action (diminutive of an act) -> the proverb's subject is not war or law; it is the little thing -> "deed" is too grand.

**battel** - small action in return -> echo, not a different category -> "revenge" is only the dark reading; Gaden includes good for good.

**fuf** - every -> no exempt petty act.

**ko** - it is (that) -> identity, not mere similarity: the return *is* the act's other face.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Gospel "measure for measure":** Both bind outgoing act to returning act.
*Divergence:* Matthew aims at mercy beyond talion; this proverb states the law the mercy would have to exceed.

**Karma as action-return:** Both refuse a deed that dies at the doer.
*Divergence:* Indic karma is often multi-life; *battel* is this-world social echo.

**Serer Takhar (this corpus):** Both make justice a structure of the world, not a mood.
*Divergence:* Takhar is a spirit in a tree; *battel* is the act's own rebound, unnamed as a god.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Do one deliberately small good today that you will not advertise. Do not cancel one small harm by a speech. Watch whether a *battel* appears. If none appears, you still did the *badel*. That was the point.

## Truth Catches the Lie
**Source:** Gaden, *Le Poular* (1913), proverb 69

so pene dawi, so gōnga hirdi. mā hebto pene.

---

### Original
so pene dawi, so gōnga hirdi. mā hebto pene.

### IAST
*pene*: lies. *dawi*: set out early. *gōnga*: truth. *hirdi*: set out in the evening. *mā hebto*: it will overtake.

### Pratibha Translation
If the lies set out early and the truth in the evening, it will overtake the lies.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is temporal and almost pastoral: departure times. Lies leave at dawn, hustling. Truth leaves in the evening, late. The proverb does not say truth is faster in essence. It says truth *catches up*. Gaden's gloss is the European flattening: "truth always comes out." The Pulaar image is a road. Two parties have started. One had a head start. The late one still arrives on the early one's back.

This belongs with Fulɓe mobility. A herder knows that the one who left late can still meet the one who left first, if the first is a lie and the second is *gōnga*. The moral is patience without passivity. You do not have to win the morning. You have to be the thing that overtakes.

Existentially: if you are late with the true word, do not despair of the morning's rumor. Set out. The proverb is a promise to the late-teller, and a warning to the early-liar.

### Key Terms
**gōnga** - truth -> not "fact" only; the true word / true state -> "honesty" is too moralistic; this is what can travel and catch.

**pene** - lies (plural) -> they travel as a group, with a head start.

**dawi / hirdi** - leave early / leave in the evening -> pastoral clock, not clock-time -> dawn and dusk are when a camp actually moves.

**hebto** - overtake, catch up -> physical verb for a moral event.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Dào Dé Jīng on the slow conquering the fast:** Both trust the late, unhurried term.
*Divergence:* Daoist softness wins by emptiness; here truth wins by pursuit on a road.

**Gospel "what is whispered will be shouted":** Both promise exposure.
*Divergence:* Christian apocalypse is a last day; this overtaking can happen this evening.

**Heraclitus' hidden nature that loves to hide:** Both delay the true.
*Divergence:* Heraclitus' nature stays partly hidden; *gōnga* is expected to arrive.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
If a false version of something you know has already left this morning, do not match its speed with a louder lie. Speak the true version once, even late. Then stop. Let *hebto* be the proverb's job, not your panic.

## The Heart Is Not a Joint
**Source:** Gaden, *Le Poular* (1913), proverb 70

berde wana hofūru, sako hofe.

---

### Original
berde wana hofūru, sako hofe.

### IAST
*berde*: heart (as seat of feeling). *hofūru*: a joint (articulation that bends). *sako hofe*: so that it might be bent / folded.

### Pratibha Translation
The heart is not a joint, that it might be bent.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is that feeling is not an obedient limb. A joint exists to flex on command. The heart does not. Gaden: sentiment cannot be ordered. This is the Pulaar refusal of a certain Islamicate and colonial fantasy — that the inner can be drilled like the body at circumcision, where Lasnet saw impassibility. Impassibility is the face. *Berde* is not the face. You can keep the face still. You cannot hinge the heart.

The proverb is not a romantic license to follow every impulse. It is a limit on tyranny, including self-tyranny. You may require conduct. You may not require love, grief, or desire to take the angle you prefer. *Pulaaku* as later named is composure. This *mallol* is the remainder: composure is not the same as a bent heart.

Existentially: stop commanding yourself to feel otherwise as if the heart were an elbow. Change the situation, the company, the act. Leave the heart its unhinged nature. That honesty is already a practice.

### Key Terms
**berde** - heart as organ of feeling -> not anatomical cardiology; the inner that will not take orders.

**hofūru** - joint, articulation -> the body's obedient bend -> the metaphor's insult: do not treat the heart as a knee.

**sako** - so that / as if it were the kind of thing that... -> counterfactual purpose: it is *not* that kind of thing.

**wana** - is not -> the same negation-pattern as the father's *wonā*.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Pascal's reasons of the heart:** Both deny that the heart is a syllogism or a lever.
*Divergence:* Pascal's heart knows God; this proverb does not name what the heart knows, only that it will not bend on command.

**Stoic *propatheia* vs judgment:** Both distinguish inner weather from commanded virtue.
*Divergence:* Stoics train judgment to withhold assent; this proverb does not promise training will hinge the *berde*.

**Kashmir Śaiva heart (*hṛdaya*) as center:** Both put the heart at the center of a person.
*Divergence:* Śaiva *hṛdaya* is consciousness itself; Pulaar *berde* is stubborn feeling.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Catch one sentence today that begins "I should feel..." Stop it. Keep the outer duty if there is one. Do not order the heart as if it were a joint. Notice what it actually does when you stop bending it.

## Nature Is a Birthmark Rolling Does Not Remove
**Source:** Gaden, *Le Poular* (1913), proverb 79

dikku ko būlol tallāde 'ittatā.

---

### Original
dikku ko būlol tallāde 'ittatā.

### IAST
*dikku*: innate manner of being, natural conduct. *būlol*: a natural mark (birthmark). *tallāde*: rolling (as one rolls a hide or a body). *'ittatā*: does not take away.

### Pratibha Translation
Innate manner is a natural mark: rolling does not remove it.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is that character is not surface dirt. A birthmark survives being rolled. Gaden: the naturally well-conducted stay so in every circumstance; the badly natured likewise. This can become fatalism. The philosophical use is stricter: do not trust a change that is only tumbling. Travel, conversion, a new language, a new mosque, a new job — *tallāde* — do not by themselves lift *dikku*. If you want a different mark, something other than rolling is required, and the proverb does not pretend to name that other thing. It only kills the fantasy of the tumble.

Read beside the cattle ethnography: the herd's way is transmitted. Read beside "what the cow ate, the heifer suckles." Futa Pulaar keeps saying that the given is sticky. Islam in Futa is a real conversion. The proverb still warns that a converted mouth can carry an unconverted *dikku*.

Existentially: stop relocating to escape yourself. The mark goes with the hide. If you must change, change a practice that is not mere motion.

### Key Terms
**dikku** - innate manner of being, natural bearing -> "character" is close; "personality" is too modern-psychological.

**būlol** - natural mark, birthmark -> visible given on the body as analog of invisible given in conduct.

**tallāde** - rolling -> motion that looks like processing (hides are rolled, bodies are rolled) -> "travel" and "ordeal" both sit inside the verb.

**'ittatā** - does not remove -> a hard negative future; the mark will still be there after the roll.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Heraclitus' *ēthos anthrōpōi daimōn*:** Both bind a person's way as a given companion.
*Divergence:* Heraclitus' *daimōn* is a divine allotment; *dikku* is more like a hide-mark.

**Confucian *xing* (nature) debates:** Both ask whether nature can be worked.
*Divergence:* Mencius waters sprouts; this proverb only says rolling is not the work.

**Serer soul-as-bird (this corpus):** Both treat a given inner as not a costume.
*Divergence:* The Serer soul can leave and return; *dikku* does not leave because you tumbled.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Name one *dikku* you have been trying to roll away by changing rooms, jobs, or company. Keep the body still for one day and watch the mark. If it is harmful, choose one non-rolling act — apology, abstinence, a craft — instead of another move.

## What the Cow Ate, the Heifer Suckles
**Source:** Gaden, *Le Poular* (1913), proverb 145

ko nagge nāmi, dūm nale muinata.

---

### Original
ko nagge nāmi, dūm nale muinata.

### IAST
*nagge*: cow. *nāmi*: has eaten. *nale*: heifer / young cow. *muinata*: suckles, drinks as milk. The herd-vocabulary of the previous collection, now inside a Pulaar sentence.

### Pratibha Translation
What the cow has eaten is what the heifer suckles.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is transmission as milk, not as lecture. The calf does not suckle a theory. She suckles what already became the mother's body. Gaden: qualities and faults of parents pass to children. The image is more precise than "like father like son." Diet becomes blood becomes milk becomes the next body. A people whose remaining cult was the herd will say inheritance in the language of nursing. This is the Pulaar text that the ethnography of boolâtrie was circling and could not quote.

The proverb is also a warning to the eater, not only a law about children. You are what will be drunk. Your *nāmi* — what you take in — is not private. It will be somebody's milk. That is pastoral metaphysics in one clause.

Existentially: look at what you are eating, hearing, envying, repeating. That is the future's milk. Change the cow's mouth if you care about the heifer.

### Key Terms
**nagge** - cow -> the sacred-economic animal of Fulɓe life, here the parent-body.

**nale** - heifer, young female cattle -> the next generation as a nursing animal, not as an abstract "child."

**nāmi / muinata** - has eaten / suckles -> two mouths, one substance -> "inherit" in English loses the mouths.

**dūm** - that (very thing) -> identity of what was eaten and what is drunk; no remainder, no filter.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Pulaar Tradition "The Remaining Cult Is the Herd" (this corpus):** Both put cattle at the center of meaning.
*Divergence:* The ethnography is French *about* a cult; this is Pulaar *as* teaching.

**Upaniṣadic food-chain (*annam*):** Both make eating a cosmological act.
*Divergence:* The Upaniṣad generalizes food as Brahman; this proverb stays in the cow-heifer pair.

**Biblical "the parents have eaten sour grapes":** Both transmit the eaten to the child.
*Divergence:* Jeremiah can refuse inherited guilt; this *mallol* states the milk-law without a prophet's override.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Before you take something in today — food, a story, a grievance — ask: would I want a young life to suckle this? If no, do not eat it. If yes, eat it as if you were already milk.

## The Good Morning Is Known from Dawn
**Source:** Gaden, *Le Poular* (1913), proverb 146

subaka moddo 'andeté ko gila fadiri.

---

### Original
subaka moddo 'andeté ko gila fadiri.

### IAST
*subaka*: morning (also the Islamic dawn prayer's time). *moddo*: good, excellent. *'andeté*: is known / recognized. *gila fadiri*: from the first light, from dawn.

### Pratibha Translation
The good morning is recognized from the first light.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is that quality shows at the start, not at the advertisement of noon. Gaden: one sees in the child what the adult will be; one knows from the outset what a new chief is worth. The next proverb repeats it botanically: what fruits begins by flowering. Futa speech trusts beginnings. This is not fortune-telling. It is attention. Dawn is small. It is already the day, or it is not.

*Subaka* is also the hour of *subaka* prayer. A good morning in an Islamic river valley is both weather and worship. The proverb does not need to choose. First light is enough to know. If you must wait until noon to see whether a person, a project, or a rule is good, you have been refusing dawn.

Existentially: look at the first five minutes. They are not a draft. They are already the morning. Adjust there, not in a speech at midday.

### Key Terms
**subaka** - morning, dawn-time -> Islamic and pastoral at once -> "morning" in English misses the prayer-hour.

**fadiri** - first light, aurora -> the smallest beginning that already tells.

**moddo** - good, as a morning can be good -> qualitative, not moralistic "virtuous."

**'andeté** - is known (passive / middle of knowing) -> recognition, not inference from a report.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Dào Dé Jīng on the tiny that is already the great:** Both read the dawn as the day.
*Divergence:* Daoist tiny is uncarved; this dawn is already classed as *moddo* or not.

**Matthew "by their fruits":** Both read early signs.
*Divergence:* Fruit is an end; *fadiri* is a beginning that already counts as knowledge.

**Proverb 147 in the same Gaden list (flower then fruit):** Both Futa *mallol* teach beginning-as-verdict.
*Divergence:* 147 is botanical sequence; 146 is temporal recognition.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Tomorrow, do not wait until the day has an opinion of itself. At first light — or at the first five minutes of a task — name whether it is *moddo*. If it is not, change the opening, not the alibi at noon.

## If an Elder Dances, Let Her Bind Her Hair
**Source:** Gaden, *Le Poular* (1913), proverb 93

so maudo 'ami, yō mōro.

---

### Original
so maudo 'ami, yō mōro.

### IAST
*maudo*: elder, great person. *'ami*: dances. *yō mōro*: let her/him bind the hair / coiffe. Gaden: an elder must not go to the dance unkempt; that is for children. Also: if you enter an affair, give yourself to it entirely.

### Pratibha Translation
If an elder dances, let them bind their hair.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is that dignity is not abstinence from joy; it is the way joy is worn. An elder may dance. The proverb does not say *maudo* must sit. It says: if dancing, then *mōro* — hair bound, the head prepared. Children may arrive undone. Age may not. Gaden's second gloss is the inner teaching: when you launch into an affair, devote yourself entirely. Half-dancing is the real indecency.

This is *pulaaku*-adjacent without the later word: reserve is not joylessness. It is completeness of form. The ethnography saw Fulɓe gravity and rare dancing. The Pulaar proverb is more interesting than the stereotype. Dance, but do not dance as if you were still unformed.

Existentially: pick one joy or one task you have been doing sloppily because you thought seriousness meant refusal. Do it. Bind the hair. Whole form, or don't enter the circle.

### Key Terms
**maudo** - elder, a person of weight -> age and status; not merely "adult."

**'ami** - dances -> allowed, not forbidden; the if-clause is permission plus condition.

**mōro** - bind the hair, make the coiffure -> bodily readiness as the image of total commitment.

**yō** - hortative "let..." -> a command of form, not a ban on the dance.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Confucian ritual even in pleasure:** Both require form when the great person moves.
*Divergence:* *Li* is a code of occasions; *mōro* is one concrete gesture standing for total entry.

**Nietzsche's dancing god:** Both refuse a dignity that cannot dance.
*Divergence:* Nietzsche's dance is unbinding; this dance is bound hair.

**Father's counsel (this collection):** Both teach a right way to occupy a role.
*Divergence:* The father lists what relations are not; this proverb tells an elder how to be in joy.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Enter one thing today that you have been half-doing — a conversation, a craft, a rest. If you enter, bind the hair: prepare, dress the part, stay until the form is complete. If you will not, do not step into the circle and call it humility.

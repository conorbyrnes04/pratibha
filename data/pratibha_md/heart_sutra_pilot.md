# Pratibha — Heart Sūtra (Pilot)
**Corpus entry:** Mahāyāna Prajñāpāramitā (Sanskrit) / c. 1st–5th century CE
**Anchor:** F. Max Müller (trans.), *The Prajñā-Pāramitā-Hṛidaya Sūtra*, Sacred Books of the East vol. 49 (1894, public domain); Sanskrit from GRETIL shorter recension
**Pilot:** The core emptiness sūtra — form/emptiness, no-attainment, the mantra of going beyond

---

## Form Is Emptiness, Emptiness Is Form
**Source:** Avalokiteśvara to Śāriputra, *Prajñāpāramitāhṛdaya* (HS_001; GRETIL Sanskrit / Müller 1894 anchor)

Here, Śāriputra, form is emptiness and emptiness is form; emptiness is not other than form, form is not other than emptiness. Whatever is form, that is emptiness; whatever is emptiness, that is form. The same holds for feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness.

---

### Original
rūpaṃ śūnyatā śūnyataiva rūpaṃ, rūpān na pṛthak śūnyatā śūnyatāyā na pṛthag rūpaṃ, yad rūpaṃ sā śūnyatā yā śūnyatā tad rūpaṃ; evam eva vedanā-saṃjñā-saṃskāra-vijñānam.

*(Sanskrit, IAST, source-verified from GRETIL shorter recension; Devanagari not fully source-verified for this pilot.)*

### IAST
rūpaṃ (form) śūnyatā (emptiness); śūnyatā eva rūpaṃ (emptiness itself is form); rūpāt na pṛthak śūnyatā (emptiness not separate from form); yad rūpaṃ sā śūnyatā (what is form, that is emptiness). evam eva (just so) vedanā (feeling), saṃjñā (perception), saṃskāra (formations), vijñāna (consciousness).

### Pratibha Translation
Here, Śāriputra: form is emptiness [śūnyatā], and emptiness is exactly form. Emptiness is not something standing apart from form; form is not something standing apart from emptiness. What we call form simply is emptiness; what we call emptiness simply is form. And it is the same for the other four aggregates [skandha]—feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness.

### Pratibha Commentary
The claim is an identity, not a ranking: emptiness is not a truer thing hidden behind the false screen of form, and form is not a consolation prize beneath a higher void. Śāntideva's later school will call this the refusal of both extremes, but the sūtra states it as flat equation—*rūpaṃ śūnyatā*, *śūnyataiva rūpaṃ*—and the counterintuitive move is the second half. It is easy to hear "form is empty" as world-denial; the sūtra immediately reverses direction so that emptiness cannot congeal into a new absolute to escape into. Emptiness here means *svabhāva-śūnya*, empty of own-being: nothing carries a self-standing essence, because everything arises dependently. To see the five aggregates as empty is not to watch them vanish but to stop mistaking their momentum for a solid, defensible self. In the Prajñāpāramitā context this is Avalokiteśvara's "deep practice"—wisdom that liberates precisely by leaving appearances exactly where they are while dissolving the grasping that clutches them. Existentially, it undercuts the retreatant's temptation to treat meditation as an exit from experience: there is no elsewhere to reach, only this—seen without the fiction of a core.

### Key Terms
**śūnyatā (शून्यता)** — Etymology: from *śūnya* (swollen/hollow, hence "empty, zero") + abstract suffix *-tā*. In Prajñāpāramitā it never means blank nothingness; it names the absence of independent essence (*svabhāva*) in things that nonetheless appear and function. Default "emptiness" invites nihilism; the sūtra's grammar of identity blocks that reading by equating it with form itself.
**rūpa (रूप)** — Etymology: "shape, visible form," the first of the five *skandhas*. Here it stands in for the whole material-experiential world. The point is not that matter is illusion but that its apparent solidity is borrowed, relational, and therefore unclutchable.
**skandha (स्कन्ध)** — Etymology: "heap, bundle, trunk of a tree." The five heaps (form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness) are the disassembly kit of the self: the sūtra runs the emptiness-equation through each so no residue is left to hide a "me."

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Nāgārjuna, MMK 24.18 (dependent arising = emptiness):** Both texts identify emptiness with the very things that appear rather than with a realm behind them; Nāgārjuna supplies the argument (dependent arising) for which the sūtra gives the meditative slogan.
*Divergence:* the sūtra is liturgical and performative (spoken to Śāriputra as instruction), whereas Nāgārjuna is dialectical, dismantling essence through explicit reductio.
**Heraclitus, DK B12 (river):** the river is real and steppable yet has no fixed substance to step into twice—an early Western intuition that identity is process, resonant with form-as-emptiness.
*Divergence:* Heraclitus keeps a governing Logos ordering flux; the Heart Sūtra removes any such underlying ordering principle as itself empty.
**Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.8 (tat tvam asi):** both climax in a terse identity-formula meant to collapse the seeker's distance from reality.
*Divergence:* the Upaniṣad affirms a fullest Self (ātman/Brahman) as what you are; the sūtra denies exactly such a self-substance, making the two formulas mirror-opposite in metaphysics though twinned in function.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Choose one solid-seeming object in the room and one solid-seeming feeling in the body. For each, say silently: "this is form, and this is emptiness—not two." Do not try to make either disappear; only notice the exact moment your mind reaches to grip it as a fixed thing, and let that grip open by a fraction. Rest there for three breaths.

## No Attainment, and So No Fear
**Source:** Avalokiteśvara to Śāriputra, *Prajñāpāramitāhṛdaya* (HS_002; GRETIL Sanskrit / Müller 1894 anchor)

There is no knowledge, no attainment, and no non-attainment. Therefore, since there is nothing to attain, the bodhisattva, relying on the perfection of wisdom, dwells with a mind free of any covering. With no covering of the mind, there is no fear; passing beyond all distortion, he reaches final nirvāṇa.

---

### Original
na jñānaṃ na prāptir nāprāptiḥ. tasmāc chāriputra aprāptitvād bodhisattvasya prajñāpāramitām āśritya viharaty acittāvaraṇaḥ. cittāvaraṇa-nāstitvād atrasto viparyāsātikrānto niṣṭhā-nirvāṇaḥ.

*(Sanskrit, IAST, source-verified from GRETIL shorter recension.)*

### IAST
na jñānaṃ (no knowledge) na prāptiḥ (no attainment) na aprāptiḥ (nor non-attainment). aprāptitvāt (because of no-attainment) bodhisattvaḥ prajñāpāramitām āśritya (relying on the perfection of wisdom) viharati acitta-āvaraṇaḥ (dwells without mental covering). citta-āvaraṇa-nāstitvāt (from the absence of mental covering) atrastaḥ (unafraid), viparyāsa-atikrāntaḥ (having transcended distortion), niṣṭhā-nirvāṇaḥ (reaching final nirvāṇa).

### Pratibha Translation
There is no knowing, no attaining, and no failing-to-attain. Precisely because there is nothing to be attained [aprāptitva], the bodhisattva leans on the perfection of wisdom and lives with a mind that has no covering over it [acittāvaraṇa]. Because there is no covering, there is nothing to be afraid of; having stepped clear of every inversion of seeing [viparyāsa], he comes to nirvāṇa complete.

### Pratibha Commentary
The sūtra derives fearlessness from a subtraction. Ordinary spiritual striving runs on a gap—between where I am and the attainment I lack—and that gap is exactly what fear guards. Here Avalokiteśvara removes the goal-object itself: "no attainment, and no non-attainment," so the ledger of spiritual gain and loss is voided rather than balanced. The pivotal term is *āvaraṇa*, a covering or veil laid over the mind; fear is presented not as an emotion to be conquered but as a symptom of that veil, which is woven from the belief in something to get and someone to get it. When the belief in attainment drops, the covering has nothing to hang on, and *atrasta*—untrembling—is the natural residue, not a heroic achievement. This is why the passage matters for retreat: the deepest obstacle late in practice is often the subtle ambition to "get somewhere," which quietly reinstalls the anxious self it was meant to dissolve. Nirvāṇa here is *niṣṭhā*, "consummate," not a place entered but the exhaustion of the very grasping that made distance seem real. The bodhisattva's calm is thus structural: no goal, no gap, no guardian fear.

### Key Terms
**prāpti / aprāpti (प्राप्ति / अप्राप्ति)** — Etymology: from *pra-√āp*, "to reach, obtain." The sūtra negates both attainment and its negation to forestall a clever mind that would make "non-attainment" the new prize. Standard "attainment" hides how the goal-structure itself sustains bondage.
**āvaraṇa (आवरण)** — Etymology: from *ā-√vṛ*, "to cover, enclose." The mind's covering is the film of grasping and distortion; wisdom does not add insight so much as remove the veil. "Obstruction" undertranslates the image of something draped over awareness.
**viparyāsa (विपर्यास)** — Etymology: "turning-about, inversion." The four classic inversions take the impermanent as permanent, the painful as pleasant, the impure as pure, the selfless as self. Transcending *viparyāsa* is seeing rightly, not seeing something new.
**atrasta (अत्रस्त)** — Etymology: *a-* (not) + *trasta* (trembling, from *√tras*, to tremble). Fearlessness as the absence of trembling once the grasped self is unfound—a negative, bodily word, not a triumphant one.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Epictetus, Enchiridion 1–2:** fear tracks what we treat as ours to gain or lose; freedom comes by relocating concern away from externals. The Heart Sūtra radicalizes this by voiding the internal goal too.
*Divergence:* Epictetus keeps a firm agent (the *prohairesis*) that chooses rightly; the sūtra dissolves the very agent whose choices Stoicism fortifies.
**Meister Eckhart, on *Abgeschiedenheit* (detachment):** the soul empty of "this and that," even empty of a wanted God, becomes fearless and free—structurally the *acittāvaraṇa* move of an uncovered mind.
*Divergence:* Eckhart's emptied ground opens onto God's birth within; the sūtra's uncovering opens onto no ground at all, nirvāṇa as cessation of grasping rather than union.
**Tao Te Ching 48 ("do less and less until nothing is done"):** attainment by subtraction, freedom through the loss of grasping aims.
*Divergence:* the Daoist telos is spontaneous accord with the Way (*ziran*); the sūtra's telos is the extinction of the distortions that project any "way" to arrive along.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Sit and name, once, the thing you are quietly trying to attain in your practice—calm, insight, a state, an identity as "one who practices." Then set it down with the sentence: "there is nothing to attain." Notice whether a small fear or restlessness appears when the goal is removed; let it be there, uncovered, and keep sitting without reinstalling the goal.

## Gone Beyond — the Mantra of Wisdom
**Source:** Avalokiteśvara to Śāriputra, *Prajñāpāramitāhṛdaya* (HS_003; GRETIL Sanskrit / Müller 1894 anchor)

Therefore the perfection of wisdom is to be known as the great mantra, the mantra that stills all suffering, true because it is not false. The mantra proclaimed in the perfection of wisdom runs: gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā.

---

### Original
prajñāpāramitā mahā-mantraḥ ... sarva-duḥkha-praśamanaḥ satyam amithyatvāt. tadyathā: **gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā.**

Devanagari (mantra, source-verified): गते गते पारगते पारसंगते बोधि स्वाहा

*(Sanskrit, IAST, source-verified from GRETIL; mantra Devanagari cross-checked.)*

### IAST
gate (gone) gate (gone) pāragate (gone beyond) pārasaṃgate (gone wholly beyond) bodhi (awakening) svāhā (so be it / hail). Frame: *mahā-mantraḥ* (great mantra), *sarva-duḥkha-praśamanaḥ* (stiller of all suffering), *satyam amithyatvāt* (true because not false).

### Pratibha Translation
So the perfection of wisdom [prajñāpāramitā] is to be known as the great mantra—the utterance that quiets all suffering, and is true for the plain reason that it does not lie. This is the mantra it speaks: *gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā*—gone, gone, gone over, gone entirely over to the far shore; awakening; so be it.

### Pratibha Commentary
After pages of relentless negation—no form, no attainment, no path—the sūtra ends not in silence but in a chant, and the shift is the teaching. Wisdom that has emptied every object now condenses into a verb of motion repeated to exhaustion: *gate, gate*—gone, gone. The counterintuitive claim embedded in *satyam amithyatvāt* ("true because not false") is that this mantra's truth is not correspondence to a fact but the performance of release; it works the way a threshold works, by being crossed. The grammar matters: *gate* is feminine vocative/locative, addressed to wisdom herself or spoken from the far shore looking back, so the line enacts the crossing it names rather than describing it from safety. *Pārasaṃgata*—"gone completely beyond"—closes the door on any residue of a beyond still held as a place, and then *bodhi* lands: awakening is not a further destination but the recognition that the going and the gone were never two. For a retreatant the pastoral point is decisive: analysis has a floor, and past it wisdom becomes something you say with the body and breath, a rhythm that carries what argument cannot. *Svāhā* seals it—the ritual "amen" that hands the whole movement over and stops the mind from re-grasping even the mantra.

### Key Terms
**mantra (मन्त्र)** — Etymology: often glossed *man* (mind, from *√man*, to think) + *-tra* (instrument): "that which protects/carries the mind." Here the whole perfection of wisdom is compressed into a *mahā-mantra*, marking the passage from discursive insight to performative utterance.
**pāra / pāragata (पार / पारगत)** — Etymology: *pāra*, "the far bank, the further shore"; *pāra-gata*, "gone to the far shore." The classic image of liberation as crossing a river; *pārasaṃgata* intensifies it to "gone wholly across," refusing any half-measure or lingering.
**bodhi (बोधि)** — Etymology: from *√budh*, "to wake, to know." Not a doctrine but an awakening; placed after all the "gone"s, it reveals that the crossing was awakening all along, not its prerequisite.
**svāhā (स्वाहा)** — Etymology: ancient Vedic ritual exclamation of offering into fire, "hail! / so be it!" It consecrates and releases the mantra, ending grasping even at wisdom by giving the utterance away.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Plotinus, Enneads VI.9 (the flight of the alone to the Alone):** both end philosophy in a movement of ascent/crossing that exceeds discursive reason and must finally be undergone, not argued.
*Divergence:* Plotinus arrives at the plenitude of the One; the sūtra arrives at emptiness and awakening with no One to arrive at—crossing into no-thing rather than into fullness.
**Tao Te Ching 1 ("the way that can be spoken is not the constant Way"):** language is honored yet marked as a threshold to be passed; the mantra is speech that dismantles reliance on speech.
*Divergence:* the Daoist gesture rests in wordless naturalness; the Buddhist mantra remains a spoken ritual act performed within a path of awakening.
**Rūmī, Mathnawī (the reed torn from the reed-bed, longing for return):** wisdom voiced as chant/song that carries the practitioner across a separation words alone cannot bridge.
*Divergence:* Rūmī's crossing is reunion of lover and Beloved; the sūtra's crossing dissolves the lover-Beloved duality itself as empty.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Recite *gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā* slowly nine times, letting the breath, not the meaning, carry it. On each "gate," release one thing you are holding—a plan, a grievance, a self-image—so the chant becomes an actual letting-go rather than a description of one. End on *svāhā* and sit for one silent minute without reaching for anything, including the mantra.

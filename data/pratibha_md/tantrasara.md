## The Heart Born of the Divine Pair
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 1 (Verse 1)

The Great Mother appears as the ever-new creation resting upon the stainless power.  
The Father is fullness itself, quietly delighting in His fivefold light.  

From their union there arises the heart, formed from the outflow of their shared radiance.  
May this heart of mine, seat of the nectar of the Highest, awaken and shine.

---

### Devanagari
विमलकलाश्रयाभिनवसृष्टिमहा जननी भरिततनुश्च पञ्चमुखगुप्तरुचिर्जनकः।  
तदुभययामलस्फुरितभावविसर्गमयं हृदयमनुत्तरामृतकुलं मम संस्फुरतात्॥१॥

### IAST

vimalakalāśrayābhinavasṛṣṭimahā jananī bharitatanuś ca pañcamukhaguptarucir janakaḥ  
tadubhayayāmalasphuritabhāvavisargamayaṃ hṛdayam anuttarāmṛtakulaṃ mama saṃsphuratāt

---

### Pratibha Translation

The Great Mother appears as the ever-new creation resting upon the stainless power.  
The Father is fullness itself, quietly delighting in His fivefold light.  

From their union there arises the heart, formed from the outflow of their shared radiance.  
May this heart of mine, seat of the nectar of the Highest, awaken and shine.

---

### Pratibha Commentary

Tantrasāra opens at the only true beginning: the embrace of Śakti and Śiva.  

Śakti is the immediacy of experience. She is the flash of sensation, thought, breath, color and sound. Every moment is abhinava sṛṣṭi, a fresh creation with no residue, arising from vimala kalā, the stainless capacity of awareness to give birth to form.  

Śiva is the still fullness in which all of this appears. His five faces are His powers: consciousness, bliss, will, understanding and action. Through these powers the world becomes visible. They are the living architecture of perception itself.  

Their inseparable intimacy is called yāmala, the Couple. From that intimacy the hṛdaya, the heart, comes forth. Not the emotional heart, but the subtle field where stillness and movement meet.  

Abhinavagupta names this heart the kula of the nectar of Anuttara. The heart is not a private chamber. It is the place where the Absolute tastes its own sweetness. When the heart is recognized in this way, it is no longer a personal center but a shrine of the union of Śiva and Śakti.  

In this single verse, the essence of Tantra is present: awareness and appearance, emptiness and form, the One tasting itself as Two, and the Two recognized as One within the heart.

---

### Key Terms

**Śakti (शक्ति)** — The dynamic aspect of consciousness appearing as sensation, perception and manifestation.

**Abhinava-sṛṣṭi (अभिनवसृष्टि)** — The continual arising of experience as fresh, alive and traceless.

**Vimala-kalā (विमलकला)** — The immaculate subtle potency that supports manifestation.

**Śiva (शिव)** — The unmoving fullness of consciousness in which all experiences appear.

**Pañca-mukha (पञ्चमुख)** — Symbol of Śiva’s five powers: consciousness, bliss, will, knowledge and action.

**Yāmala (यामल)** — The union of Śiva and Śakti, stillness and movement.

**Hṛdaya (हृदय)** — The subtle center where awareness and its radiance are directly known.

**Visarga (विसर्ग)** — The creative outpouring of consciousness through which the universe appears.

**Anuttara (अनुत्तर)** — The Highest, unsurpassed Absolute.

**Amṛta (अमृत)** — Nectar, immortality, bliss.

**Kula (कुल)** — The sacred seat or community of energies centered in the heart.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Unit 1, Chapter 1:** structural homology between the union of Śakti and Śiva and the formation of the hṛdaya as a subtle field where stillness (Śiva) and movement (Śakti) meet
*Divergence:* the text emphasizes that the heart is not an emotional center but a shrine of union, diverging from common yogic notions of the heart as a personal locus of feeling

**Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Unit 1, Chapter 1:** structural homology between the concept of ‘abhinava sṛṣṭi’ (ever-new creation) and the continual renewal of the heart’s nectar, mirroring the stainless power of Śakti
*Divergence:* while abhinava sṛṣṭi is presented as an ongoing cosmic process, the heart’s nectar is portrayed as a singular, fixed sweetness that the Absolute tastes, limiting the perpetual aspect

**Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Unit 1, Chapter 1:** structural homology between the fivefold light of Śiva (consciousness, bliss, will, understanding, action) and the fivefold radiance that flows from the union to form the heart
*Divergence:* the passage treats the fivefold light as a static attribute of Śiva, whereas in other Tantric texts it is often dynamic, evolving through stages of meditation

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Place a hand on the chest.  
Feel the movement of breath and sensation.  
Sense the quiet space that holds it.  
Let the heart reveal itself as the place where both are one.

## The Essence Offered in Straight Speech
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 1 (Verse 2)

The Tantrāloka is vast and not easy for all to enter.  
Therefore, listen instead to this Essence of Tantra, composed in clear and straightforward speech.

---

### Devanagari
विततस्तन्त्रालोको विगाहितुं नैव शक्यते सर्वैः।  
ऋजुवचनविरचितमिदं तु तन्त्रसारं ततः शृणुत॥२॥

### IAST

vitatas tantrāloko vigāhituṃ naiva śakyate sarvaiḥ  
ṛjuvacanaviracitam idaṃ tu tantrasāraṃ tataḥ śṛṇuta

---

### Pratibha Translation

The Tantrāloka is vast and not easy for all to enter.  
Therefore, listen instead to this Essence of Tantra, composed in clear and straightforward speech.

---

### Pratibha Commentary

Abhinavagupta recognizes the scale of Tantrāloka. It is an ocean of mantra, ritual, ontology and subtle philosophy. Few can dive deep enough to reach its floor.  

Tantrasāra is given as the essence. Here the same realization is distilled and poured into ṛju vacana, straight speech. No unnecessary complication, no deliberate obscurity. The intention is not to impress but to transmit.  

This verse sets the tone for the entire work. Tantrasāra is not a watered-down summary. It is the bright marrow of Tantrāloka, the inner sap of recognition offered in a form a sincere practitioner can digest.

---

### Key Terms

**Tantrāloka (तन्त्रालोक)** — Abhinavagupta’s monumental masterwork that systematizes Trika Śaiva doctrine and practice.

**Ṛju-vacana (ऋजुवचन)** — Language that does not hide its meaning, aimed at immediate understanding.

**Tantrasāra (तन्त्रसार)** — Abhinavagupta’s distilled presentation of the core tantric vision contained in Tantrāloka.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Unit 1, Chapter 1, “The Essence Offered in Straight Speech”:** structural homology
*Divergence:* Abhinavagupta parallels the traditional ‘Sūtra‑style’ exposition of the Upanishads, presenting a condensed, clear form (ṛju vacana) rather than the elaborate, symbolic language of the Tantrāloka

**Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Unit 1, Chapter 1:** structural homology
*Divergence:* While the Upanishads employ a dialogic, question‑answer format, Abhinavagupta adopts a monologic, declarative tone, emphasizing direct transmission over pedagogical dialogue

**Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Unit 1, Chapter 1:** structural homology
*Divergence:* The Tantrāloka traditionally interweaves ritual, mantra, and metaphysics in a dense, multi‑layered text; Abhinavagupta distills these layers into a single, lucid verse, thereby breaking the customary multi‑textual structure

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Bring to mind everything you think you must master.  
Let the breath out slowly and say inwardly:  
“Show me the essence.”

## The Heart-Lotus Opened by the Guru’s Light
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 1 (Verse 3)

By the fall of the radiant feet of the sun-like Master Śambhunātha,  
the contraction of this heart-lotus of Abhinavagupta has been dissolved in light.  
Examine this lotus, for in doing so you worship Maheśa.

---

### Devanagari
श्रीशम्भुनाथभास्करचरणनिपातप्रभापगतसङ्कोचम्।  
अभिनवगुप्तहृदम्बुजमेतद्विचिनुत महेशपूजनहेतोः॥३॥

### IAST

śrīśambhunāthabhāskaracaraṇanipātaprabhāpagatasaṅkocam  
abhinavaguptahṛdambujam etad vicinuta maheśapūjanhetoḥ

---

### Pratibha Translation

By the fall of the radiant feet of the sun-like Master Śambhunātha,  
the contraction of this heart-lotus of Abhinavagupta has been dissolved in light.  
Examine this lotus, for in doing so you worship Maheśa.

---

### Pratibha Commentary

Abhinavagupta bows to his guru Śambhunātha, likening him to a sun whose rays melt the tightness in the heart. When the teacher’s presence touches the disciple, saṅkoca, the contracted sense of “I,” begins to release.  

He then calls Tantrasāra his own heart-lotus. A lotus grows in mud yet remains unstained, opening petal by petal to the sun. In the same way, these teachings emerge from the circumstances of a human life yet carry a clarity untouched by them.  

“Examine it,” he says. Not read it casually, but examine it like one who gently opens the petals of a sacred flower. That act of intimate attention is itself worship of Maheśa, the Great Lord, because the text is the opening of Śiva’s heart in human language.

---

### Key Terms

**Śambhunātha (शम्भुनाथ)** — Described as “sun-like” for his illuminating grace that dissolves contraction.

**Bhāskara (भास्कर)** — Highlights the guru as a source of spiritual light.

**Saṅkoca (सङ्कोच)** — The tightening of awareness into a small, separate sense of self.

**Hṛd-ambuja (हृदम्बुज)** — Image for the subtle heart as a lotus opening under the sun of recognition.

**Maheśa (महेश)** — Here, the supreme form of consciousness worshiped through deep study and contemplation.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Abhinavagupta Tantrasāra, Unit 1, Chapter 1, Passage:** structural homology of the heart‑lotus as a metaphor for spiritual unfolding
*Divergence:* Unlike the classical lotus that grows in mud, here the lotus is the text itself, a vessel that remains unstained by worldly circumstances

**Abhinavagupta Tantrasāra, Unit 1, Chapter 1, Commentary:** structural homology of the guru’s light as sun‑like radiance dissolving the disciple’s ego (saṅkoca)
*Divergence:* In traditional hatha yoga, the sun is an external force; here the guru’s presence is internalized as the source of dissolution

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Before reading any passage of Tantrasāra, pause.  
Acknowledge silently the teachers and conditions that brought you here.  
Let gratitude soften the heart for a few breaths.

## On Knowledge and Ignorance
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 1 (Prose Section 1)

Here, in this tradition, knowledge is the cause of liberation, because it opposes ignorance, which is the cause of bondage. Ignorance is of two kinds: one lodged in the intellect, and one pertaining to the Self.  

The ignorance that resides in the intellect has the form of uncertainty and of certainty directed the wrong way, such as taking the non-Self to be the Self.  

The ignorance pertaining to the Self has the form of vikalpas, limiting thoughts. It is a contracted display of awareness and is the root cause of saṃsāra. I will explain this more fully in the discussion on mala.  

This Self-ignorance can be removed by initiation and related means. Yet even initiation is not possible when there is intellectual ignorance in the form of non-discernment, because initiation, which purifies the principles and joins one to Śiva, must be preceded by clear certainty about what is to be abandoned and what is to be embraced.  

Therefore, knowledge firmly established in the intellect, decisive knowledge, is primary. Cultivated and practiced, it also destroys Self-ignorance, since practice of awareness in the form of vikalpas reaches its completion in awareness free of vikalpa.  

The Self is of the nature of Śiva, a light of consciousness not cramped by thought. For that reason, knowledge that is thoroughly certain and rooted in all things as they are is what is to be cultivated.  

Such knowledge arises on the basis of scripture. Scripture, when it is the speech of the Supreme Lord, is true authority. Other scriptures may be partially accepted where they agree, and reasoning that goes beyond them can clarify the nature of Śiva, but the knowledge taught in those other āgamas only frees from bondage to a certain extent, not fully.  

The scripture of the Supreme Lord alone frees in every respect. It flows in five streams and is differentiated into groups of ten, eighteen, and sixty-four Tantras. From among them, the six-and-a-half Trika scriptures are the essence, and among these Mālinīvijaya is supreme.  

The meaning contained there cannot be fully expressed when gathered together in one place. And without a clear determination of the reality to be known, there is neither liberation nor liberating power, for only purified knowledge has that nature.  

Since the highest human aim rests on well-practiced knowledge, this work is undertaken in order to establish that knowledge.

### Pratibha Commentary

Abhinavagupta turns from invocation to diagnosis. The problem is ajñāna, ignorance. It is not just a lack of information, but a split in knowing itself.  

One strand lives in the intellect. It shows up as confusion, as not being sure what is real, or as placing certainty in what is false, such as taking the body, mind or prāṇa to be the true Self.  

The deeper strand belongs to the Self. This is the subtle habit of awareness to appear as limited, as a stream of contracted vikalpas. This is the root of saṃsāra, the wandering in thoughts, identities and worlds.  

Initiation and ritual can remove Self-ignorance, but Abhinava insists that even initiation depends on discernment. One must know what to turn away from and what to turn toward. Without this clarity in the buddhi, the rite cannot do its work.  

So he elevates decisive knowledge as primary. When the intellect is aligned with reality and that alignment is practiced, the very stream of thoughts becomes a vehicle toward thought-free awareness. Vikalpa, when seen clearly, leads back to avikalpa, the open field of consciousness.  

He then praises scripture, but not in a narrow sectarian way. The words of Śiva are the highest authority. Other traditions can be honored and partially accepted. Reasoning that goes beyond them can further reveal the nature of consciousness itself. Yet only the scriptures that spring from the Supreme Lord free from every layer of bondage.  

Among these, the Trika āgamas are the distilled essence, and Mālinīvijaya is the crown. Even so, their full meaning cannot be contained in a single exposition.  

This leads to the last point: liberation depends on clear recognition of what truly is. Vague spirituality is not enough. The highest human purpose rests on stable, practiced knowledge of reality. Tantrasāra is written to plant and nourish that knowledge in the heart.

### Key Terms

**Ajñāna (अज्ञान)** — Misidentification and non-recognition that obscure the Self.

**Buddhi-gata ajñāna (बुद्धिगत अज्ञान)** — Confusion and wrong certainty about what is real.

**Saṃsāra (संसार)** — The movement through states, identities and births under the spell of ignorance.

**Dīkṣā (दीक्षा)** — A rite that purifies the principles and joins the individual to Śiva.

### Pratibha Commentary

Abhinavagupta now names the problem in a single verse. Ajñāna, ignorance, is the root of bondage. The tradition calls it mala, a subtle grime that dulls the shine of awareness.

## Introduction: The One Light
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 1 (Upodghātaḥ)

Here, the essential nature alone is what is truly to be chosen. That essential nature is nothing but light, the shining of awareness in all things, since it is impossible for non-light to be the nature of anything at all.  

This light is not many, because there is no second kind of essential nature that could penetrate it. There is no division in its own being. Space and time do not divide it either, since they too have that light as their nature. Thus there is only one light, and that light is consciousness. Consciousness is the shining of objects. On this all are agreed.  

This light does not depend on another. To be dependent is to be something that merely becomes illuminated. Illumination always relies on another light. But there is no other light apart from this. Therefore there is one light that is svatantra, completely free.  

Through that very freedom it is not cut off by space, time or any form. It is all-pervading, eternal, of the nature of all forms and at the same time beyond every form. Its freedom is the power of bliss. Its inner thrill is the power of will. Its luminosity is the power of consciousness. Its capacity to reflect and know is the power of knowledge. Its connection with all forms is the power of action.  

Thus, though this uninterrupted light is adorned with these principal powers, it is in essence one continuous shining, endowed with will, knowledge and action, resting in its own bliss, and this is called Śiva.  

Out of that freedom He makes Himself appear as contracted and is then called the atomic self, the aṇu. Again, by that same freedom, He reveals Himself and appears as the uninterrupted light of Śiva once more.  

Even there, by the force of freedom, He may reveal Himself without any means, or with means. When there are means, they are of three types: will, knowledge or action. These are the three upāyas, the Śāmbhava, Śākta and Āṇava ways of entry. In this work, all four modes of immersion, with and without means, will be taught in sequence.

### Pratibha Commentary

Abhinavagupta now states the central view. The only thing ultimately worth embracing is svabhāva, essential nature. That nature is prakāśa, pure light. Not a physical radiance, but the simple fact that anything appears at all.  

There is no second nature behind or beside this light. Space and time do not cut it into pieces, because they too are ways in which light shows itself. One light, one consciousness, appearing as the entire field of experience.

Because it is free, it is not trapped by before and after, here and there, form and formlessness. It pervades everything and cannot be located.  

In this light are recognized the great powers: bliss, will, consciousness, knowledge and action. These are not separate beings but facets of the same living radiance. In their wholeness, this radiance is called Śiva, undivided awareness resting in its own joy.  

Out of freedom, there is a movement of contraction. Śiva appears as the limited self, the one who says “I” and feels small. Out of freedom as well, contraction relaxes and the same “I” is known to be Śiva again.  

Sometimes this revelation happens without any path. Sometimes it unfolds through means. When it does, the means can be of the flavor of Śiva, of Śakti, or of the individual: direct recognition, energetic expansion or careful practice. Tantrasāra promises to unfold all these ways as modes of entering the light we already are.

### Pratibha Commentary

The teaching now becomes personal. The Self that each one says “I” to is none other than Śiva, a body of light, completely free.

## The One for Whom No Means Is Needed
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 2 (Prose Section)

Now, I will explain the path of no-path, Anupāya.  

When someone has been struck by a firm descent of Śakti, and after hearing the Guru’s words only once, reflects in this way on his own, then his absorption into the Divine is ever-arising and free of all means.  

Here, reasoning alone is the limb of Yoga. If it is asked, “How does he reflect in this way?”, this is the answer:  

This very Supreme Lord, whose nature is self-luminous, is my own Self.  
What could be accomplished through any means with regard to Him?  

It cannot be the attainment of one’s true nature, because that nature is eternal.  
It cannot be some new knowledge of Him, because He is self-revealing.  
It cannot be the removal of some veil, because no veil can exist there.  
It cannot be entering into Him, because there is no separate “enterer” apart from Him.  

So what “means” can there be, when even the idea of something separate from Him is impossible?  

Therefore, all of this is one single reality made only of consciousness:  
not measured by time,  
not bounded by space,  
unstained by limiting conditions,  
not confined by forms,  
not pointed out by words,  
not unfolded by any means of knowledge.  

From time down to the instruments of knowledge, everything that appears is only its own free play. This reality is a compact mass of freedom and bliss, and that alone am I.  

In that very Self-within-me, the entire universe shines as a reflection.  

One who sees this clearly and firmly is always already immersed in the Supreme Lord without any means at all. For such a one, there is no binding obligation of mantra, worship, meditation or observance.

yo’yaṃ parameśvaraḥ svaprakāśa-rūpaḥ svātmā tatra kim upāyena kriyate na svarūpa-lābho nityatvān na jñaptiḥ svayam-prakāśamānatvān nāvaraṇa-vigama āvaraṇasya kasyacid apy asambhavān na tad-anupraveśo’nupraveṣṭur vyatiriktasyābhāvāt |  
kaścātr’opāyas tasyāpi vyatiriktasyānupapatteḥ tasmāt samastam idam ekaṃ cin-mātra-tattvaṃ kālena akalitaṃ deśena aparicchinnaṃ upādhibhir amlānam ākṛtibhir aniyantritaṃ śabdair asandiṣṭaṃ pramāṇair aprapañcitaṃ kālādeḥ pramāṇa-paryantasya svecchayāiva svarūpa-lābha-nimittaṃ ca svatantram ānanda-ghanaṃ tattvaṃ tad eva cāham tatraivāntar mayi viśvaṃ pratibimbitam |  
evaṃ dṛḍhaṃ viviñcānasya śaśvad eva pārameśvaraḥ samāveśo nirupāyaka eva |

### Pratibha Translation

Now, I will explain the path of no-path, Anupāya.  

When someone has been struck by a firm descent of Śakti, and after hearing the Guru’s words only once, reflects in this way on his own, then his absorption into the Divine is ever-arising and free of all means.  

Here, reasoning alone is the limb of Yoga. If it is asked, “How does he reflect in this way?”, this is the answer:  

This very Supreme Lord, whose nature is self-luminous, is my own Self.  
What could be accomplished through any means with regard to Him?  

It cannot be the attainment of one’s true nature, because that nature is eternal.  
It cannot be some new knowledge of Him, because He is self-revealing.  
It cannot be the removal of some veil, because no veil can exist there.  
It cannot be entering into Him, because there is no separate “enterer” apart from Him.  

So what “means” can there be, when even the idea of something separate from Him is impossible?  

Therefore, all of this is one single reality made only of consciousness:  
not measured by time,  
not bounded by space,  
unstained by limiting conditions,  
not confined by forms,  
not pointed out by words,  
not unfolded by any means of knowledge.  

From time down to the instruments of knowledge, everything that appears is only its own free play. This reality is a compact mass of freedom and bliss, and that alone am I.  

In that very Self-within-me, the entire universe shines as a reflection.  

One who sees this clearly and firmly is always already immersed in the Supreme Lord without any means at all. For such a one, there is no binding obligation of mantra, worship, meditation or observance.

---

### Pratibha Commentary

Here Abhinavagupta turns to **Anupāya**, the “no-method” method, the pathless path. This is not a technique but a description of what happens when **śaktipāta is very strong** and the disciple is already ripe.  

A single word of the Guru lands, and something in the heart recognizes itself. From that point, practice is not a ladder but a remembering.  

Abhinava takes a razor to the very idea of “upāya” here:  

- You cannot “attain” your nature because it is not absent.  
- You cannot “know” Śiva as an object because He is the very light of knowing.  
- You cannot “remove a veil” in Him because nothing can cover light from itself.  
- You cannot “enter” Śiva because there is no second thing that could enter.  

Every project based on “I am here, Śiva is there, I must get there” collapses.  

What remains is a radical recognition:  
**All that exists is a single, self-luminous field of consciousness.**  

It is:  
- not counted by time,  
- not boxed in by space,  
- not stained by limitations,  
- not defined by forms,  
- not caught by words,  
- not fully laid out by any pramāṇa (means of valid knowledge).  

And then the pivot: *tad eva cāham* — “that alone am I.”  
Not “I will become that,” but “I am that, now, and have never been otherwise.”  

In that “I,” the universe appears as a reflection, like a dream within awareness, vivid but not other. Whoever sees this steadily is already in perpetual samāveśa, divine immersion, **without any need for means**.  

For such a one, mantra, pūjā, dhyāna and observances are no longer obligations or conditions for freedom. They may still happen, but as expressions of freedom, not steps toward it.

### Key Terms

**Anupāya (अनूपाय / अनुपाय)** — The mode of awakening in which no method is required because recognition is immediate.

**Śaktipāta (शक्तिपात)** — The sudden impact of divine energy that awakens recognition in the disciple.

**Samāveśa (समावेश)** — The state of being fully suffused by the recognition of one’s identity with Śiva.

**Tarka (तर्क)** — Here, the sharp contemplation that cuts away incoherent notions of separation.

**Cin-mātra-tattva (चिद्मात्रतत्त्व / चिन्मात्रतत्त्व)** — The principle of pure consciousness alone.

**Upādhi (उपाधि)** — Limiting adjunct, conditioning factor.

**Pramāṇa (प्रमाण)** — Means of valid knowledge (perception, inference, scripture, etc.).

**Svatantra / Svātantrya (स्वतन्त्र / स्वातन्त्र्य)** — The absolute freedom of consciousness to appear in any way.

**Ānanda-ghana (आनन्दघन)** — Compact mass of bliss.

**Pratibimba (प्रतिबिम्ब)** — The universe as a luminous mirror-play in awareness, not separate from it.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra 2.1, passage on Anupāya:** structural homology with the Advaita Vedānta notion of *parāśakti* (supreme power) as the ultimate reality that cannot be attained or known through conventional means; both systems posit that realization is a spontaneous, non-dual recognition rather than a process
*Divergence:* While Advaita frames this as the *Brahman* that is already present and cannot be approached by *upāya*, Abhinavagupta emphasizes the dynamic descent of *Śakti* and the guru’s single word as the trigger, underscoring a more performative, experiential entry point

**Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra 2.1, comparison of “no veil” and “no separate enterer”:** structural homology with the Buddhist *sunyata* doctrine that emptiness is self-luminous and contains no inherent subject or object; both deny any external means or duality in attaining the ultimate truth
*Divergence:* Unlike the Buddhist emphasis on emptiness as a negation of inherent existence, Abhinavagupta presents it as a positive, self-revealing light that is the very *Śiva* itself, thereby retaining a theistic identity rather than a purely philosophical negation

**Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra 2.1, description of the universe as a reflection within the Self:** structural homology with the Kashmir Shaivism concept of *Siva as the All* where the cosmos is a manifestation of consciousness; both see the world as a reflection of the ultimate reality
*Divergence:* In Abhinavagupta’s account the reflection is achieved through a single, instantaneous realization via the guru’s word, whereas in classical Kashmir Shaivism the reflection is often cultivated through systematic practices like *Kundalini* awakening and *tapas*

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Allow this one line to sink in:  
> “That alone am I, and in Me the universe shines as a reflection.”  

Repeat it slowly a few times, then sit in silence for a minute.  
Notice any part of you that still feels “separate” from this.  
Gently include that feeling in the same light.

## The Sun Does Not Need a Lamp
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 2 (Verse 6)

The net of means does not reveal Śiva,  
just as a clay pot cannot make the thousand-rayed sun shine.  

One whose vision is noble,  
and who reflects in this way,  
enters self-luminous Śiva in an instant.

विवेचयन्नित्थमुदारदर्शनः स्वयम्प्रकाशं शिवमाविशेत्क्षणात्॥६॥

vivecayan nittham udāra-darśanaḥ svayam-prakāśaṃ śivam āviśet kṣaṇāt ||6||

---

### Pratibha Translation

The net of means does not reveal Śiva,  
just as a clay pot cannot make the thousand-rayed sun shine.  

One whose vision is noble,  
and who reflects in this way,  
enters self-luminous Śiva in an instant.

---

### Pratibha Commentary

This verse is a hammer-blow to spiritual consumerism.  

No collection of upāyas, no matter how refined, can “make” Śiva appear. Methods can refine, purify, prepare, soften. But they do not manufacture the sun.  

Abhinavagupta’s image is ruthless and kind at once: a clay pot does not help the sun shine. It can at best limit or obscure the light. In the same way, methods can also, if clung to, create smaller containers for what is naturally infinite.  

The “noble-seeing” person is not the one with the most exotic practices, but the one who sees the simple truth of Anupāya:  
- Śiva is already self-luminous.  
- Methods are at best expressions of that luminosity, not its cause.  

By seeing this clearly, not as a concept but as a living insight, such a person “enters” Śiva at once. In reality, Śiva enters Himself by relaxing the pretense of being someone else.

---

### Key Terms

**Upāya-jāla (उपायजाल)** — Net or multitude of means.

**Sahasra-dīdhiti (सहस्रदीधिति)** — Thousand-rayed one, the sun.

**Udāra-darśana (उदारदर्शन)** — Noble-seeing, one of elevated vision.

**Svayam-prakāśa (स्वयम्प्रकाश)** — That which shines by itself and not by another.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Abhinavagupta Tantrasāra, Unit 2, Verse 1:** structural homology
*Divergence:* The clay pot analogy parallels the Buddhist ‘net of means’ (pratyāhāra) critique: both emphasize that skillful means cannot create the ultimate reality (Śiva/Śūnyatā); they only serve as instruments, not the source.

**Abhinavagupta Tantrasāra, Unit 2, Commentary:** structural homology
*Divergence:* The notion of ‘noble-seeing’ person mirrors the Advaita concept of ‘noble perception’ (śuddha-dṛṣṭi) in Shankaracharya’s Brahmasūtra commentary, yet diverges by framing it as an experiential instant of entering Śiva rather than a gradual purification of ignorance.

**Abhinavagupta Tantrasāra, Unit 2, Verse 1:** structural homology
*Divergence:* The image of the sun that cannot be made to shine by a pot echoes the Tantric idea of ‘siddhi’ as a natural emanation, contrasting with the Western notion of skillful means as a tool for achieving enlightenment; the divergence lies in the ontological status of Śiva as self-luminous versus a conditioned phenomenon.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

For one moment, let yourself imagine that *nothing you do* can increase or decrease the light of awareness.  
Let practice drop for three breaths.  
Rest as the “sun” rather than the “pot.”

## World as Reflection in the Sky of Awareness
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 3 (Prose Section)

This Light, which has been described as the reality of Śiva, sometimes cannot be entered directly as the undivided circle of pure radiance. When that happens, the practitioner contemplates the Power of Absolute Freedom as something that stands out more clearly, and by seeing that, he experiences a thought-free immersion in Bhairava. For such a practitioner, the instruction is as follows.  

All this manifold of existing things is nothing but a reflection in the sky of awareness. It has the marks of a reflection. The mark of a reflection is this: whatever cannot appear on its own and only shows up mixed with something else is a reflection.  

A face appears in a mirror, taste appears in the saliva of the mouth, smell appears in the nostrils, the touch of union appears in the pleasure-organ, the pain of spear and lance appears in the inner organ of touch, an echo appears in space.  

The taste in the mouth is not the primary taste, since we do not see it healing disease and producing the other effects that real food does. Smell and touch in the sense organs are also not primary, because, without the true object that bears qualities, they cannot set off a chain of effects. And yet they do exist, as we can see from the body’s shaking, emission and so on.  

Even sound is not primary, since someone must utter it, and we hear it coming back as echo.  

In just this way, as these examples shine forth as reflections, so the entire universe shines as a reflection in the Light of the Supreme Lord.  

You might ask, “What, then, is the original thing of which this is a reflection?” Let there be nothing separate at all.  

You might say, “Then is this reflection without a cause?” No. The question of the cause leads us to this: there is no need to invent an external original. The cause is the Power of the Supreme Lord, whose other name is Svātantrya, Absolute Freedom.  

Because She bears the reflection of the universe, that Power is nothing other than the Lord as the universe. The universe is made of consciousness and is the field where consciousness manifests. The universe itself is the reversed image of that One here, which means it is held as reflection within Him.  

This universality of the Lord is His very nature. It is not unconscious, because for the nature of consciousness, a lack of self-awareness is impossible.  

If there were no self-awareness in the very essence of consciousness, that essence would be inert. This awareness is not conventional or symbolic. It is immediately inherent in the nature of consciousness and is said to be the embryo of the supreme sound, para-nāda. As such, it is the entire cluster of Śakti of the Supreme Lord, which regulates and orders the universe, and it is aware of that whole extent.  

Within that cluster, three powers of the Lord are primary: **Anuttara**, **Icchā**, and **Unmeṣa**. These three are a triad of awareness, expressed as the sounds **a, i, u**. From this triad, the whole proliferation of powers is generated.  

In Anuttara alone there is rest, which is Ānanda.  
In Icchā alone there is rest, which is Īśana.  
In Unmeṣa alone there is rest, which is Ūrmi, the first stirring of the Power of Action.  

These three rests form another triad of awareness, **ā, ī, ū**.  

The first triad, a, i, u, is solar, because it is the essence of the portion of pure light.  
The second triad, ā, ī, ū, is lunar, because it is saturated with the delight of repose.  
Up to this point, the element of activity has not yet entered.  

When activity enters into Icchā and Īśana, then the object-to-be-known and the object-to-be-ruled appear. In that situation, the sounds **ra** and **la** arise: ra as the pure streak of light, la as the streak of repose. Because ra and la have the nature of slightly stopping or thickening the flow of light, the “object-to-be-known” is not clear like an external object. If it were fully clear, it would already be a formed creation, and not simply the play of Icchā and Īśana.  

Precisely because they are not clear, ra and la remain mere tones rather than full consonants. These four sounds, **ṛ, ṝ, ḷ, ḹ**, hold the shadow of both vowel and consonant and are therefore considered neuter.  

When Anuttara and Ānanda expand into Icchā and the rest, two more vowels arise, **e** and **o**. When Anuttara and Ānanda again collide in that flow, two further vowels arise, **ai** and **au**.  

This is the Power of Action. She is expressed as the four vowels **e, ai, o, au**.  

When the Power of Action completes her play, everything that had come into the state of “effect” turns back toward Anuttara. Before that reabsorption, it abides as Bindu, “am”, the point which is nothing but light, the very essence of knowing.  

Then, within that same Bindu, the emission of Anuttara arises as “aḥ”.  

In this way, the sixteen modes of awareness that are the vowels are called the seed. Arising from them is the consonantal field, the womb.  

From Anuttara comes the **ka-group**; from faith-filled Icchā the **ca-group**; from Icchā endowed with activity come the **ṭa-group** and **ta-group**; from Unmeṣa the **pa-group**. By the connection with the five primary powers, this forms a fivefold set of consonant groups.  

From the threefold Icchā come **ya, ra, la**. From Unmeṣa comes **va**. From the same threefold Icchā come **śa, ṣa, sa**. From Visarga arises **ha**. From the union of consonants emerges **kṣa**.  

Thus this Lord Anuttara is the Lord of Kula. He has one Kaulikī Power of Emission. From the first vibration of bliss out to the last edge of external manifestation, the various letter-groups are simply shapes of awareness that have gained the status of outer principles.  

This Visarga, this emission, is threefold:  
- Āṇava, the form of the mind’s repose.  
- Śākta, the form of the mind’s awakened illumination.  
- Śāmbhava, the form of the mind’s dissolution.  

Thus, Visarga itself is the Lord’s power in the generation of the universe.  

So, when awareness is taken as utterly undivided, there is only the One Lord. When awareness is taken in divided form, as seed and womb, there appears Śiva, the possessor of Śakti, and Śakti.  

If awareness is considered in eightfold sets, together with the Lord of the circle, the letter kṣa, there are nine groups. When we emphasize each awareness-portion individually, we obtain fifty letters. If we further take into account the possible subdivisions of their measure, we speak of eighty-one.  

In truth, there are six basic modes of awareness. Through expansion and reabsorption, they become twelve, and these twelve nourish the fullness of the Lord’s universal power. These very awareness-forms are the goddesses known as the revered Kālikās.  

These pure modes of awareness, whose nature is Śakti, in the level of Śuddhavidyā appear as the “parāpara” state. Through a light contraction in Māyā’s unfolding they take the forms of Vidyā and Vidyeśvara.  

In Māyā proper, where division is clearly articulated, they become mayic letters. As these pass through Paśyantī, Madhyamā and Vaikharī, they become the ordinary letters of speech and thought, eventually hardening into the external categories of experience. Even as mayic forms, they can appear like lifeless bodies.  

When, however, they are revivified by the pure living awarenesses mentioned above, they regain power and become instruments of both enjoyment and liberation.  

Thus, for one who sees his own Self as the mere repose of all these modes of awareness, and as the place where the whole variety of worlds and principles appears as reflection, there is liberation while living through a Śāmbhava immersion that is free of thought.  

Here too, as before, there is no binding requirement of mantra or any other practice.

अनुत्तरानन्दयोरिच्छादिषु यदा प्रसरस्तदा वर्णद्वयम् ए-ओ इति। तत्रापि पुनरनुत्तरानन्दसङ्घट्टाद्वर्णद्वयम् ऐ-औ इति। सेयं क्रियाशक्तिस्तदेव च वर्णचतुष्टयम् ए-ऐ ओ-औ इति। ततः पुनः क्रियाशक्त्यन्ते सर्वं कार्यभूतं यावदनुत्तरे प्रवेक्ष्यति तावदेव पूर्वं संवेदनसारतया प्रकाशमात्रत्वेन बिन्दुतयास्ते - अमिति। ततस्तत्रैवानुत्तरस्य विसर्गो जायते - अः इति। एवं षोडशकं परामर्शानां बीजस्वरूपमुच्यते। तदुत्थं व्यञ्जनात्मकं योनिरूपम्।  

तत्रानुत्तरात्कवर्गः श्रद्धाया इच्छायाश्चवर्गः सकर्मिकाया इच्छाया द्वौ टवर्गस्तवर्गश्चोन्मेषात्पवर्गः - शक्तिपञ्चकयोगात्पञ्चकत्वम्। इच्छाया एव त्रिविधाया य-र-ला उन्मेषाद्वकार इच्छाया एव त्रिविधायाः श-ष-सा विसर्गाद्धकारो योनिसंयोगजः क्षकारः। इत्येवं एष भगवाननुत्तर एव कुलेश्वररूपः। तस्य चैकैव कौलिकी विसर्गशक्तिर्ययानन्दरूपात्प्रभृतीयता बहिःसृष्टिपर्यन्तेन प्रस्पन्दतो वर्गादिपरामर्शा एव बहिस्तत्त्वरूपतां प्राप्ताः। स चैष विसर्गस्त्रिधाणवः चित्तविश्रान्तिरूपः शाक्तश्चित्तसम्बोधलक्षणः शाम्भवश्चित्तप्रलयरूप इति। एवं विसर्ग एव विश्वजनने भगवतः शक्तिः।  

इत्येवमियतो यदा निर्विभागतयैव परामर्शस्तदैक एव भगवान्बीजयोनितया भागशः परामर्शे शक्तिमाञ्छक्तिश्च। पृथगष्टकपरामर्शे चक्रेश्वरसाहित्येन नववर्ग एकैकपरामर्शप्राधान्ये पञ्चाशदात्मकता। तत्रापि सम्भवद्भागभेदपरामर्शन एकाशीतिरूपत्वम्। वस्तुतस्तु षडेव परामर्शाः प्रसरणप्रतिसञ्चरणयोगेन द्वादश भवन्तः परमेश्वरस्य विश्वशक्तिपूर्णत्वं पुष्णन्ति। ता एव एताः परामर्शरूपत्वाच्छक्तयो भगवत्यः श्रीकालिकेति निरुक्ताः। एते च शक्तिरूपा एव शुद्धाः परामर्शाः शुद्धविद्यायां परापररूपत्वेन मायोन्मेषमात्रसङ्कोचाद्विद्याविद्येश्वररूपतां भजन्ते। मायायां पुनः स्फुटीभूतभेदविभागा मायीयवर्णतां भजन्ते ये पश्यन्तीमध्यमावैखरीषु व्यावहारिकत्वमासाद्य बहीरूपतत्त्वस्वभावतापत्तिपर्यन्तास्ते च मायीया अपि शरीरकल्पत्वेन यदा दृश्यन्ते यदा च तेषामुक्तनयैरेतैर्जीवितस्थानीयैः शुद्धैः परामर्शैः प्रत्युज्जीवनं क्रियते तदा ते सवीर्या भवन्ति ते च तादृशा भोगमोक्षप्रदा इत्येवं सकलपरामर्शविश्रान्तिमात्ररूपं प्रतिबिम्बितसमस्ततत्त्वभूतभुवनभेदमात्मानं पश्यतो निर्विकल्पतया शाम्भवेन समावेशेन जीवन्मुक्तता। अत्रापि पूर्ववन्न मन्त्रादियन्त्रणा काचिदिति।

### Pratibha Translation

This Light, which has been described as the reality of Śiva, sometimes cannot be entered directly as the undivided circle of pure radiance. When that happens, the practitioner contemplates the Power of Absolute Freedom as something that stands out more clearly, and by seeing that, he experiences a thought-free immersion in Bhairava. For such a practitioner, the instruction is as follows.  

All this manifold of existing things is nothing but a reflection in the sky of awareness. It has the marks of a reflection. The mark of a reflection is this: whatever cannot appear on its own and only shows up mixed with something else is a reflection.  

A face appears in a mirror, taste appears in the saliva of the mouth, smell appears in the nostrils, the touch of union appears in the pleasure-organ, the pain of spear and lance appears in the inner organ of touch, an echo appears in space.  

The taste in the mouth is not the primary taste, since we do not see it healing disease and producing the other effects that real food does. Smell and touch in the sense organs are also not primary, because, without the true object that bears qualities, they cannot set off a chain of effects. And yet they do exist, as we can see from the body’s shaking, emission and so on.  

Even sound is not primary, since someone must utter it, and we hear it coming back as echo.  

In just this way, as these examples shine forth as reflections, so the entire universe shines as a reflection in the Light of the Supreme Lord.  

You might ask, “What, then, is the original thing of which this is a reflection?” Let there be nothing separate at all.  

You might say, “Then is this reflection without a cause?” No. The question of the cause leads us to this: there is no need to invent an external original. The cause is the Power of the Supreme Lord, whose other name is Svātantrya, Absolute Freedom.  

Because She bears the reflection of the universe, that Power is nothing other than the Lord as the universe. The universe is made of consciousness and is the field where consciousness manifests. The universe itself is the reversed image of that One here, which means it is held as reflection within Him.  

This universality of the Lord is His very nature. It is not unconscious, because for the nature of consciousness, a lack of self-awareness is impossible.  

If there were no self-awareness in the very essence of consciousness, that essence would be inert. This awareness is not conventional or symbolic. It is immediately inherent in the nature of consciousness and is said to be the embryo of the supreme sound, para-nāda. As such, it is the entire cluster of Śakti of the Supreme Lord, which regulates and orders the universe, and it is aware of that whole extent.  

Within that cluster, three powers of the Lord are primary: **Anuttara**, **Icchā**, and **Unmeṣa**. These three are a triad of awareness, expressed as the sounds **a, i, u**. From this triad, the whole proliferation of powers is generated.  

In Anuttara alone there is rest, which is Ānanda.  
In Icchā alone there is rest, which is Īśana.  
In Unmeṣa alone there is rest, which is Ūrmi, the first stirring of the Power of Action.  

These three rests form another triad of awareness, **ā, ī, ū**.  

The first triad, a, i, u, is solar, because it is the essence of the portion of pure light.  
The second triad, ā, ī, ū, is lunar, because it is saturated with the delight of repose.  
Up to this point, the element of activity has not yet entered.  

When activity enters into Icchā and Īśana, then the object-to-be-known and the object-to-be-ruled appear. In that situation, the sounds **ra** and **la** arise: ra as the pure streak of light, la as the streak of repose. Because ra and la have the nature of slightly stopping or thickening the flow of light, the “object-to-be-known” is not clear like an external object. If it were fully clear, it would already be a formed creation, and not simply the play of Icchā and Īśana.  

Precisely because they are not clear, ra and la remain mere tones rather than full consonants. These four sounds, **ṛ, ṝ, ḷ, ḹ**, hold the shadow of both vowel and consonant and are therefore considered neuter.  

When Anuttara and Ānanda expand into Icchā and the rest, two more vowels arise, **e** and **o**. When Anuttara and Ānanda again collide in that flow, two further vowels arise, **ai** and **au**.  

This is the Power of Action. She is expressed as the four vowels **e, ai, o, au**.  

When the Power of Action completes her play, everything that had come into the state of “effect” turns back toward Anuttara. Before that reabsorption, it abides as Bindu, “am”, the point which is nothing but light, the very essence of knowing.  

Then, within that same Bindu, the emission of Anuttara arises as “aḥ”.  

In this way, the sixteen modes of awareness that are the vowels are called the seed. Arising from them is the consonantal field, the womb.  

From Anuttara comes the **ka-group**; from faith-filled Icchā the **ca-group**; from Icchā endowed with activity come the **ṭa-group** and **ta-group**; from Unmeṣa the **pa-group**. By the connection with the five primary powers, this forms a fivefold set of consonant groups.  

From the threefold Icchā come **ya, ra, la**. From Unmeṣa comes **va**. From the same threefold Icchā come **śa, ṣa, sa**. From Visarga arises **ha**. From the union of consonants emerges **kṣa**.  

Thus this Lord Anuttara is the Lord of Kula. He has one Kaulikī Power of Emission. From the first vibration of bliss out to the last edge of external manifestation, the various letter-groups are simply shapes of awareness that have gained the status of outer principles.  

This Visarga, this emission, is threefold:  
- Āṇava, the form of the mind’s repose.  
- Śākta, the form of the mind’s awakened illumination.  
- Śāmbhava, the form of the mind’s dissolution.  

Thus, Visarga itself is the Lord’s power in the generation of the universe.  

So, when awareness is taken as utterly undivided, there is only the One Lord. When awareness is taken in divided form, as seed and womb, there appears Śiva, the possessor of Śakti, and Śakti.  

If awareness is considered in eightfold sets, together with the Lord of the circle, the letter kṣa, there are nine groups. When we emphasize each awareness-portion individually, we obtain fifty letters. If we further take into account the possible subdivisions of their measure, we speak of eighty-one.  

In truth, there are six basic modes of awareness. Through expansion and reabsorption, they become twelve, and these twelve nourish the fullness of the Lord’s universal power. These very awareness-forms are the goddesses known as the revered Kālikās.  

These pure modes of awareness, whose nature is Śakti, in the level of Śuddhavidyā appear as the “parāpara” state. Through a light contraction in Māyā’s unfolding they take the forms of Vidyā and Vidyeśvara.  

In Māyā proper, where division is clearly articulated, they become mayic letters. As these pass through Paśyantī, Madhyamā and Vaikharī, they become the ordinary letters of speech and thought, eventually hardening into the external categories of experience. Even as mayic forms, they can appear like lifeless bodies.  

When, however, they are revivified by the pure living awarenesses mentioned above, they regain power and become instruments of both enjoyment and liberation.  

Thus, for one who sees his own Self as the mere repose of all these modes of awareness, and as the place where the whole variety of worlds and principles appears as reflection, there is liberation while living through a Śāmbhava immersion that is free of thought.  

Here too, as before, there is no binding requirement of mantra or any other practice.

---

### Pratibha Commentary

Śāmbhavopāya is the path where **recognition** happens through a sharp, direct tilt of awareness, not through elaborated practice. Chapter 3 gives you two key images to live with:  

1. **World as reflection in the sky of awareness**  
2. **Letters and sounds as crystallized modes of divine self-awareness (parāmarśa)**  

Abhinavagupta starts in a very practical place. Sometimes, you cannot enter directly into the sense of “I am the undivided circle of Śiva.” That is too abstract, too vast. So he gives you a doorway: notice the **Power of Svātantrya**, the sheer freedom with which your experience arises. Contemplate that freedom as the real “subject,” and a thoughtless Bhairava-state opens.  

Then he explains how everything that appears is a **pratibimba**, a reflection, not an independent thing.  

- Taste in the mouth is not the original taste of food.  
- The ache in the body is not pain in itself, but a reflected sensation.  
- An echo is not the original sound.  

Reflections only appear in another medium: mirror, saliva, air, awareness. They cannot appear on their own.  

Abhinava’s point: **the universe is just like that.**  
It is not a second thing beside Śiva. It is the way Śiva’s own awareness appears, reflected in Himself, in the “sky of bodha,” the open field of awareness.  

He then heads off an important mistake. If the universe is a reflection, maybe there must be some separately existing original object that it copies? He refuses that. Instead, the “cause” of the reflection is **Śiva’s own power of freedom**. The universe is not a copy of something outside awareness.  
It is awareness itself, showing its own capacity to appear as many.  

At the heart of this is **parāmarśa**, self-awareness, the “I am this” pivot of consciousness. Without that, consciousness would be inert. That subtle self-awareness is the embryo of the supreme sound, para-nāda.  

From here he unpacks a symbolic cosmology: each **vowel and consonant** is a way this divine awareness turns toward itself.  

- The basic triad: **a, i, u** – Anuttara, Icchā, Unmeṣa.  
- The resting triad: **ā, ī, ū** – the same three powers tasted as repose and bliss.  
- Further combinations yield e, o, ai, au.  
- The sixteen vowels are the “seed,” the pure pulses of self-awareness.  
- The consonants are the “womb,” the shaping matrix of manifestation.  

For our purposes, you do not need to memorize the full phonology. Feel the point:  

> Sound is not merely a tool of speech.  
> It is the **shape that awareness takes on its way from Silence into world**.  

The goddesses known as Kālikās are these very modes of awareness. When they are pure, they are powers of recognition. When they descend into Māyā, they become letters, words, stories, karmic scripts. They can be dead and heavy, like a body with no breath, or reanimated by a return to parāmarśa.  

Śāmbhavopāya, then, is:  

- Seeing your entire experience as **reflected play in the sky of awareness**, and  
- Recognizing every movement of thought, speech and sensation as a **modulation of one divine “I-taste.”**  

Abhinava ends very quietly: the one who sees his own Self as the resting ground of all parāmarśas, who sees all worlds and principles as reflected in that Self, is already **jīvanmukta**, liberated while living, by a Śāmbhava absorption that has no concepts and no compulsion to practice anything.  

Practice may arise, mantra may arise, but not as obligation. They are the echo of freedom, not the cause of it.

*Divergence:* The notion of the universe as a reflection in the sky of awareness parallels the Buddhist concept of pratītyasamutpāda (dependent origination) where phenomena arise only in relation to causes and conditions, yet Abhinavagupta diverges by attributing the reflection to an absolute, self‑existing divine power (Svātantrya) rather than to interdependent co‑arising causes.

**Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Unit 3, Chapter 3, Passage:** structural homology
*Divergence:* The comparison of sensory reflections (taste, smell, echo) to cosmic reflection mirrors the Advaita Vedānta idea of māyā (illusion) where the world appears superimposed on Brahman; however, Abhinavagupta diverges by insisting that the reflection is not an illusion but a real manifestation of the Supreme Lord’s power, thereby rejecting the dualism between reality and appearance.

**Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Unit 3, Chapter 3, Passage:** structural homology
*Divergence:* The emphasis on the Power of Absolute Freedom (Svātantrya) as the cause of the reflection echoes the Kashmir Shaivite concept of Śiva as the dynamic, self‑free consciousness, yet Abhinavagupta diverges by framing Svātantrya as the sole ontological source of both the reflector and the reflected, eliminating any intermediary principle or external original.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

1. Sit for a few minutes and let this one sentence circulate inside:  
   **“All this is a reflection in the sky of awareness.”**  

2. Pick anything in your present field: a sound, a thought, an ache, a desire.  
   Ask softly, *“Where does this appear?”*  

3. Let attention shift from the “object” to the **space that holds it**.  
   Feel that space as **you**, prior to name and story.  

4. For one breath, forget method.  
   Just rest as that sky.

## The Mirror and the Taste of Vimarśa
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 3 (Verse 7)

Here, the entire universe shines inwardly in the Self,  
just as a variegated pattern appears inside a mirror.

### Pratibha Translation

Here, the entire universe shines inwardly in the Self,

### Pratibha Commentary

Abhinavagupta gives you the whole Śāmbhava view in one verse.  

- The **mirror** is the subtle body–mind complex.  
- The **pattern** inside it is the world of forms.  
- But the one who actually knows, tastes, and claims “this is my experience” is **bodha**, awareness itself.  

The mirror only hosts images. It does not love them, suffer them, seek them.  
Awareness, through **nija-vimarśa-rasa**, the flavor of its own self-recognition, actually embraces the universe.  

Śāmbhavopāya is to shift identity:  
from the mirror, which constantly changes,  
to the **taster**, the one who savors all of this as flavors in its own being.  

Once that shift is stable, practice becomes a kind of play.  
You are no longer polishing the mirror to reach the sun.  
The sun knows itself and the mirror is just one of its games.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Look at anything in front of you, or close your eyes and notice an inner image.  

- First, feel it as if in a mirror: “This is an image in the mind.”  
- Then ask, “Who is tasting this? Who knows this is appearing?”  

Rest as that knower for a few breaths.  
Do not try to stop the images.  
Let them pass like colors in a mirror that you no longer mistake for yourself.

## Refining Thought – Right Reasoning as Śakti’s Path
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 4 (Prose Section)

When the practitioner gradually purifies and refines his thoughts in order to enter the essential nature described just before, then the method of **bhāvanā** – contemplative dwelling – becomes useful. This bhāvanā proceeds on the foundation of three: **right reasoning (sat-tarka)**, **authentic scripture (sad-āgama)**, and **the instruction of a true Guru (sad-guru-upadeśa)**.  

Bound beings imagine themselves bound **only because of thought**. Through the force of vikalpa they take themselves to be limited, and this mistaken self-image is the cause that keeps saṃsāra going.  

Yet that same faculty of thought can arise in an opposite form: as a vikalpa that **confronts and breaks apart** the binding vikalpa. When thought appears in this “counter-form,” it crushes the thought that caused saṃsāra and becomes the cause of ascent and awakening.  

What does such a liberating thought reveal? It reveals that what truly exists is **nothing but unbounded awareness**, something that transcends all the limited tattvas from Śiva downwards. That alone is the ultimate reality, the ground where all “things” stand, the very vigor of the universe; by That the universe lives.  

And that Reality is “I”:  
> “That alone am I; therefore I am both beyond the universe and the Self of the universe.”  

For those blinded by Māyā this insight does not arise, because right reasoning and its companions are absent. Attached to narrow scriptures, they never turn toward higher vision and even come to despise genuine reasoning, true scripture, and the Guru’s instruction.  

Therefore only those who have been pierced by a firm descent of Śambhu’s grace refine their thoughts through authentic scripture and the rest, and in that way enter their supreme nature.  

You might object: “If the Supreme Reality is reached through thought, does that not make it *itself* a thought-constructed object?”  

Abhinava answers: No. Thought has done its work the moment it **cuts the scent of duality**. The Supreme Reality always shines as all forms everywhere by its own light, and in relation to That, thought is not needed either to construct or to destroy anything.  

For one who has received a very firm descent of power, **right reasoning arises spontaneously**, like a natural clarity. Such a being is said to be “initiated by the goddesses themselves.” For others, this right reasoning is born through the gradual process of scripture and teaching.  

The Guru’s role is to clarify and unfold the Āgama. The role of Āgama is to generate a stream of doubt-free, mutually supporting thoughts. The **continuous flow of such purified vikalpas** is called **sat-tarka**, right reasoning.  

That right reasoning itself is what Abhinava calls **bhāvanā**: even something indistinct or past is made as vivid as present by being brought into clear awareness again and again.

### Pratibha Translation

When the practitioner gradually purifies and refines his thoughts in order to enter the essential nature described just before, then the method of **bhāvanā** – contemplative dwelling – becomes useful. This bhāvanā proceeds on the foundation of three: **right reasoning (sat-tarka)**, **authentic scripture (sad-āgama)**, and **the instruction of a true Guru (sad-guru-upadeśa)**.  

Bound beings imagine themselves bound **only because of thought**. Through the force of vikalpa they take themselves to be limited, and this mistaken self-image is the cause that keeps saṃsāra going.  

Yet that same faculty of thought can arise in an opposite form: as a vikalpa that **confronts and breaks apart** the binding vikalpa. When thought appears in this “counter-form,” it crushes the thought that caused saṃsāra and becomes the cause of ascent and awakening.  

What does such a liberating thought reveal? It reveals that what truly exists is **nothing but unbounded awareness**, something that transcends all the limited tattvas from Śiva downwards. That alone is the ultimate reality, the ground where all “things” stand, the very vigor of the universe; by That the universe lives.  

And that Reality is “I”:  
> “That alone am I; therefore I am both beyond the universe and the Self of the universe.”  

For those blinded by Māyā this insight does not arise, because right reasoning and its companions are absent. Attached to narrow scriptures, they never turn toward higher vision and even come to despise genuine reasoning, true scripture, and the Guru’s instruction.  

Therefore only those who have been pierced by a firm descent of Śambhu’s grace refine their thoughts through authentic scripture and the rest, and in that way enter their supreme nature.  

You might object: “If the Supreme Reality is reached through thought, does that not make it *itself* a thought-constructed object?”  

Abhinava answers: No. Thought has done its work the moment it **cuts the scent of duality**. The Supreme Reality always shines as all forms everywhere by its own light, and in relation to That, thought is not needed either to construct or to destroy anything.  

For one who has received a very firm descent of power, **right reasoning arises spontaneously**, like a natural clarity. Such a being is said to be “initiated by the goddesses themselves.” For others, this right reasoning is born through the gradual process of scripture and teaching.  

The Guru’s role is to clarify and unfold the Āgama. The role of Āgama is to generate a stream of doubt-free, mutually supporting thoughts. The **continuous flow of such purified vikalpas** is called **sat-tarka**, right reasoning.  

That right reasoning itself is what Abhinava calls **bhāvanā**: even something indistinct or past is made as vivid as present by being brought into clear awareness again and again.

### Pratibha Commentary

Śāmbhavopāya (chapter 3) is the flash where everything falls away and only naked awareness remains.  

Śāktopāya (chapter 4) is gentler, more human: it works **in and with mind** rather than against it.  

Abhinavagupta’s starting point is brutally simple:  

- We feel bound **because of the stories we believe** about ourselves.  
- Those stories are vikalpas – patterned thoughts soaked in habit and emotion.  
- The same power of vikalpa, when turned back toward its source, becomes medicine.  

Śāktopāya is the path where **thought is not the enemy**.  
Instead, thought is refined until it becomes transparent to the Light that carries it.  

This refinement has three pillars:  

1. **Sat-tarka – right reasoning**  
   Not endless debate, but a style of thinking that always tends toward **non-duality**, that cuts through “I am this limited thing” and reveals “I am the awareness of all.”  

2. **Sad-āgama – authentic scripture**  
   Not just any holy text, but those arising from awakened vision. Their purpose is to **seed liberating vikalpas** – living concepts that tilt you toward direct recognition.  

3. **Sad-guru-upadeśa – the true Guru’s instruction**  
   The Guru does not give you new dogmas; they tune your mind so that right reasoning and living scripture can flow in a **single, undivided stream**.  

In Śāktopāya you don’t try to stop thinking. You learn to notice which thoughts **thicken duality** and which ones **melt it**. Over time, sat-tarka becomes a subtle, self-running inner current:  

> Every time a “small-I” thought appears, another, deeper thought quietly answers:  
> “This too appears in Me. I am the awareness that knows it.”  

That counter-current is Śakti working through vikalpa.  

Eventually, thought does what it alone can do:  
it **disenchants itself**, and what remains is Śiva’s quiet blaze.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

For a week, try this as a Śāktopāya drill:

1. **Catch the binding vikalpa**  
   - Notice when you think, *“I’m failing,” “I’m not enough,” “I’m separate,” “I’m stuck.”*  
   - Don’t suppress it – just name it silently: “Binding thought.”

2. **Invoke the counter-vikalpa**  
   - Add one sentence right after:  
     > “This thought also appears in awareness. I am the one who is aware.”  

3. **Feel the shift**  
   - Let your attention move from the content of the thought  
     → to the space in which it appears.  
   - Rest there for 3 breaths, even if the thought keeps chattering.

4. **Use scripture as fuel**  
   - Choose one short line that you know is true in your bones  
     (from Tantrasāra, Śiva Sūtra, Gītā, Gospel, whatever is alive for you).  
   - When you feel contracted, repeat it once or twice **as a living vikalpa**,  
     not a mantra to escape, but a thought that reminds you who is thinking.

5. **Notice the long arc**  
   - Over time, observe: is it becoming easier to pivot  
     from “I’m inside my thoughts” → to “thoughts are inside Me”?  

That pivot is Śāktopāya in action.

## Yāga, Homa, Japa, Vrata, Yoga – Rituals as Inner Śakti
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 4 (Prose Section)

Apart from right reasoning, which is the light of Pure Knowing, none of the classical limbs of Yoga is, by itself, a direct means to the Truth.  

The observances such as austerity, the restraints such as non-violence, and the prāṇāyāmas such as inhalation are all mainly concerned with the **object-side** of experience – body, breath, behavior. In that sense, what direct work can they do *within* awareness itself?  

Pratyāhāra only refines the field of the senses. Dhyāna, dhāraṇā, samādhi, when practiced step by step, give the meditator a taste of identity with the chosen object of meditation. But actual “practice” has **no place** in the Supreme Principle, in Śiva as one’s own nature.  

Once you stand as awareness, there is nothing to take in and nothing to push away. What “practice” could mean there?  

Still, the sages support reasoning. In this Śāktopāya, **right reasoning is the direct means**. It is Pure Knowledge in motion. And it can be refined in many directions; that is where the traditional forms like **yāga, homa, japa, vrata, yoga** come in.  

- **Yāga (inner sacrifice)** is the firm vikalpa that  
  > “All states and all beings truly rest only in the Supreme Lord;  
  > there is nothing separate from Him.”  
  To stabilize this vision, one offers all experiences to the Lord – not just flowers and incense, but emotions, memories, actions, doubts.  

- **Homa (inner fire-offering)** is the stabilizing vikalpa that  
  > “All phenomena are made of the Lord’s fire-light.”  
  Here, everything that arises is “fed” into the fire of awareness, until only that fire remains as the residue.  

- **Japa** is an inner recognition:  
  > “That Supreme Principle is my very nature.”  
  Repeating a mantra is not about sound alone; it is the repeated touch of this insight, regardless of what objects appear – outer or inner.  

- **Vrata (vow)**, as Nandiśikhā says, is  
  > “The supreme vow is sameness with all.”  
  The vow here is to see the same Lord in body, pot, stone, stranger, friend, thought, breath – everywhere.  

- **Yoga** in Śāktopāya is not a separate limb but the **special vikalpa** that keeps tracing attention back to its own source, again and again, until the natural light stands revealed without effort.  

Abhinava’s point is delicate and radical:  

- Outer forms of ritual and yoga are **not rejected**.  
- But their **true power** is to nourish a stream of liberating vikalpas.  
- Once that stream is strong, their outer forms can fall away without loss.  

What was once a ritual becomes a style of perception.

### Pratibha Commentary

Śāktopāya is where Abhinava quietly rewires the whole idea of spiritual practice.  

Instead of:  
> “Do a lot of techniques to attain a distant goal,”  

he offers:  
> “Let every technique become a **carrier wave** for the insight:  
>  ‘Everything is Śiva, appearing in Me, as Me.’”  

If you do yāga, homa, japa, vrata without this inner vikalpa, they remain just **beautiful actions**. Good for the nervous system, good for the community, but not necessarily liberating.  

If you do **one breath**, one bow, one mantra repetition with this inner orientation, you are already in Śāktopāya.  

He is not telling you to abandon āsana, prāṇāyāma, kīrtan, pūjā.  
He is saying:  

- Let them be **expressions of recognition**, not strategies for “becoming worthy.”  
- Let them constantly whisper inside you: *sarvaṃ śivamayam* – “All this is infused with Śiva.”  

Then you no longer practice **to become** something.  
Practice becomes the way Śakti enjoys.

## The Three Śaktis and the Twelve Kālīs
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 4 (Prose Section)

The Supreme Lord is of the nature of **complete consciousness**. His very fullness is called **Śakti**.  

The scriptures refer to Her with many meaningful names: *kula, sāmarthya, ūrmi, hṛdaya, sāra, spanda, vibhūti, trīśikā, kālī, karṣaṇī, caṇḍī, vāṇī, bhoga, dṛk, nityā*, and so on – each name capturing a particular flavor of Her power, each dear to meditators in its own way.  

When She is seen as the **totality of powers**, fullness itself shines as consciousness. Her powers are beyond counting; in fact, **the universe itself is nothing but Her powers**.  

To make this more graspable, Abhinava says the universe can be summarized in **three main Śaktis** of the Lord:  

1. **Parāśakti** – by which He bears, sees, and illuminates the entire universe from Śiva down to earth as nothing but undifferentiated awareness.  

2. **Parāparāśakti** – by which He appears as both different and non-different, like an elephant seen in a mirror: there is a play of separation and unity at once.  

3. **Aparāśakti** – by which He manifests things as distinctly separate, as if they exist in mutual difference.  

A fourth way of speaking is to say that Parā Herself, under names like Mātṛsadbhāva and Kālakarṣiṇī, **draws all of this back into Herself**. She swallows the entire threefold process of holding the universe, by embracing it in Her own heart through unbroken awareness.  

These four Śaktis, through the Lord’s freedom, each operate in three modes: in **manifestation, maintenance, and dissolution**. In this way they become twelve.  

These twelve modes are the twelve **Kālīs** – goddesses who are nothing but forms of consciousness itself. They whirl like a wheel, governing the rise and fall of worlds, months, time-cycles, experiences. Shining outward as patterns of time, space and events, they **nourish the Lord’s freedom** and are referred to collectively as Śrī-Kālī.

### Pratibha Translation

The Supreme Lord is of the nature of **complete consciousness**. His very fullness is called **Śakti**.  

The scriptures refer to Her with many meaningful names: *kula, sāmarthya, ūrmi, hṛdaya, sāra, spanda, vibhūti, trīśikā, kālī, karṣaṇī, caṇḍī, vāṇī, bhoga, dṛk, nityā*, and so on – each name capturing a particular flavor of Her power, each dear to meditators in its own way.  

When She is seen as the **totality of powers**, fullness itself shines as consciousness. Her powers are beyond counting; in fact, **the universe itself is nothing but Her powers**.  

To make this more graspable, Abhinava says the universe can be summarized in **three main Śaktis** of the Lord:  

1. **Parāśakti** – by which He bears, sees, and illuminates the entire universe from Śiva down to earth as nothing but undifferentiated awareness.  

2. **Parāparāśakti** – by which He appears as both different and non-different, like an elephant seen in a mirror: there is a play of separation and unity at once.  

3. **Aparāśakti** – by which He manifests things as distinctly separate, as if they exist in mutual difference.  

A fourth way of speaking is to say that Parā Herself, under names like Mātṛsadbhāva and Kālakarṣiṇī, **draws all of this back into Herself**. She swallows the entire threefold process of holding the universe, by embracing it in Her own heart through unbroken awareness.  

These four Śaktis, through the Lord’s freedom, each operate in three modes: in **manifestation, maintenance, and dissolution**. In this way they become twelve.  

These twelve modes are the twelve **Kālīs** – goddesses who are nothing but forms of consciousness itself. They whirl like a wheel, governing the rise and fall of worlds, months, time-cycles, experiences. Shining outward as patterns of time, space and events, they **nourish the Lord’s freedom** and are referred to collectively as Śrī-Kālī.

### Pratibha Commentary

Śāktopāya is not just “working with thought.” It is **learning to recognize Śakti in the entire architecture of your experience**.  

Abhinava maps this with almost mathematical precision:

- **Parā** – the pure sense that “everything is awareness.” This is like the still, unbroken ocean of experience.  
- **Parāparā** – the sense that “things are different *and yet* they are me.” This is the shimmering where unity and multiplicity kiss.  
- **Aparā** – the everyday feeling that “this is me, that is not me.” The world of apparently separate objects, responsibilities, relationships.  

Śāktopāya trains you to **notice all three as one Power moving**.  

The twelve Kālīs are not just fierce goddesses on a yantra; they are the **twelve ways your consciousness rhythmically shapes your day**:

- Waking and sleeping  
- Opening and closing  
- Desire and fulfillment  
- Creation, sustaining, dissolving of projects, identities, relationships  

To live Śāktopāya is to feel:  

> “Every surge, every settling, every cycle in my life  
>  is a face of Śakti, a Kālī dancing in the field of my own awareness.”  

At first this is taken as a vikalpa – a deliberate way of thinking.  
Later it becomes **obvious**, like recognizing your own handwriting.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Try this as a daily contemplation:

1. **Parā – pure awareness taste**  
   - At least once, sit for 3–5 minutes and rest in simple “I am.”  
   - No story, no project, just the sense of being.  
   - Whisper inside: *“This is Parā – the one in whom all appears.”*

2. **Parāparā – both/and**  
   - In a conversation or class, feel both:  
     - “I am this person speaking or listening,” **and**  
     - “All of this is arising in Me as awareness.”  
   - Let unity and difference be present together.  
   - This is Parāparā.

3. **Aparā – the ordinary world**  
   - When doing something very practical – emails, cleaning, accounting –  
     consciously notice: “This feels like a separate, solid world.”  
   - Instead of fighting that feeling, **include it**:  
     > “This too is Śakti, playing at being separate.”  

4. **End of day – bow to the twelve Kālīs**  
   - Scan your day: moments of beginning, sustaining, ending.  
   - See each as a Kālī-pulse.  
   - Offer a simple inner bow: *“Thank you, Śakti, for today’s dance.”*  

This is Śāktopāya as **lived tantric phenomenology** – not ideas, but a way of seeing that gently de-solidifies the world without denying its beauty.

## When Thought Needs Support – Descent into the Āṇava Path
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 5 (Prose Section)

Earlier we saw that sometimes **vikalpa** (thought) can refine itself without any external help. When that happens, it falls out of its usual “animal” function of keeping us bound, is blessed by **Śuddhavidyā**, takes on the nature of the Lord’s own Śakti, and becomes a **Śāktopāya**-type knowledge. Chapter 4 was about that.  

Now Abhinava turns to the case where thought **cannot** refine itself alone.  

When vikalpa needs **support** in order to be purified, it takes up **limited supports** as means:  

- **buddhi** (intellect)  
- **prāṇa** (vital energy)  
- **deha** (body)  
- external objects like **ghaṭa** (pot), altar, liṅga, etc.  

By leaning on these limited forms, vikalpa shrinks down into **aṇutva** – the condition of the bound individual – and manifests the knowledge called **āṇava-jñāna**, the knowledge characteristic of the **Āṇava Path**.  

In this setting:

- **Buddhi** takes the form of **dhyāna** (meditation).  
- **Prāṇa** is twofold:  
  - **gross** (sthūla) as **uccāraṇa** (the movement of breath/sound), with five modes: prāṇa, apāna, samāna, udāna, vyāna.  
  - **subtle** (sūkṣma) as **varṇa** (the inner sound/letter).  
- **Deha** is the specially configured body, the **instrument** (*karaṇa*).  
- **Ghaṭa, kumbha, sthāṇḍila, liṅga**, etc. are **outer supports** used in ritual worship.  

These are the tools of Āṇavopāya: working *from the outside in*—through body, breath, senses, and thought—until they become transparent to Consciousness.

### Pratibha Commentary

So far the text has climbed high:

- **Śāmbhavopāya** – sudden recognition through pure awareness.  
- **Śāktopāya** – recognition through refined thought and Śakti-insight.  

Now Abhinava talks to the rest of us.  

Most of the time, our mind is **not** ready to self-liberate. Vikalpa doesn’t spontaneously flip into recognition. It keeps looping:  

> “I am this body, these duties, this story.”  

When that’s the case, we need **handles**:  

- Something to sit on, straighten, and feel. (Body)  
- Something to breathe with, count, and ride. (Prāṇa)  
- Something to think about: deities, elements, mantras, rituals. (Buddhi + external objects)

- When we use these supports **consciously**, Āṇava becomes a **bridge**.  
- When we cling to them as the final truth, they become another **prison**.

Thought is still central, but now it is **supported** by:

- the rhythm of the breath,  
- the posture of the body,  
- the resonance of mantra and ritual.  

Āṇava is the yoga of **skillful limitation**: using limits to reveal the limitless.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

A quick diagnostic you can actually use in your own sādhana:

1. When you sit, can your awareness **open and rest by itself** with a simple shift of attention?  
   - If yes, you’re flirting with **Śāmbhavopāya**.  

2. If not, but **one liberating thought** (“All this is awareness,” “I am the witness,” etc.) can tilt you into stillness,  
   - you’re in **Śāktopāya** territory.  

3. If even that is too airy, but **breath, posture, mantra, visualization** help you enter,  
   - you’re moving through **Āṇavopāya**.  

There’s nothing “lesser” about this. The instruction here is:  

> Use **whatever level actually works today**,  
> and let it carry you upwards, rather than judging yourself for not being “advanced enough.”

## Fire, Sun, and Moon – The Inner Wheel of Perception
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 5 (Dhyāna Section)

Abhinava now gives a **specific Āṇava meditation**.  

First, recall the Supreme Principle we’ve met:  

- **Self-luminous**,  
- containing all tattvas within itself,  
- shining as the inner “I am.”  

1. **Meditate on that Supreme Light in your own Heart.**  
2. Within that Heart-Light, visualize the **triad** of:  
   - **fire** (*vahni*) – the knower (*pramātṛ*),  
   - **sun** (*arka*) – the process of knowing (*pramāṇa*),  
   - **moon** (*soma*) – the known object (*prameya*).  

Let these three fuse into a single **Mahābhairava Fire**, blazing ever more strongly as it is “fanned” by the wind of meditation.  

That fire is encircled by twelve rays (powers), forming a **wheel (cakra)**. Imagine it **leaving the Heart** through one of the inner “spaces” – the cavity of the eye, for example – and **resting** in an outer object.  

Then work this sequence:  

- That outer object is **filled by soma** – the lunar aspect – as if it were “created” and nourished.  
- It is then **illuminated by arka** – the solar aspect – held in steady clarity.  
- Finally it is **dissolved by the fire** of reabsorption.  
- What remains is the object **absorbed into Anuttara**, the Highest Self.  

In this way the wheel of twelve powers becomes **full of non-duality with every external thing**. The entire “circle” of outer and inner objects is seen as swallowed by that Bhairava fire and **resting in the Self**.  

For the yogi who keeps practicing like this, seeing that all sequences of manifestation, maintenance, and dissolution are **only the play of their own consciousness**, and that all creative freedom belongs to that same awareness, the **state of Bhairava arises immediately**.  

With continued practice, all kinds of desired accomplishments (*siddhi*) may also appear as side-effects.

### Pratibha Translation

Abhinava now gives a **specific Āṇava meditation**.  

First, recall the Supreme Principle we’ve met:  

- **Self-luminous**,  
- containing all tattvas within itself,  
- shining as the inner “I am.”  

1. **Meditate on that Supreme Light in your own Heart.**  
2. Within that Heart-Light, visualize the **triad** of:  
   - **fire** (*vahni*) – the knower (*pramātṛ*),  
   - **sun** (*arka*) – the process of knowing (*pramāṇa*),  
   - **moon** (*soma*) – the known object (*prameya*).  

Let these three fuse into a single **Mahābhairava Fire**, blazing ever more strongly as it is “fanned” by the wind of meditation.  

That fire is encircled by twelve rays (powers), forming a **wheel (cakra)**. Imagine it **leaving the Heart** through one of the inner “spaces” – the cavity of the eye, for example – and **resting** in an outer object.  

Then work this sequence:  

- That outer object is **filled by soma** – the lunar aspect – as if it were “created” and nourished.  
- It is then **illuminated by arka** – the solar aspect – held in steady clarity.  
- Finally it is **dissolved by the fire** of reabsorption.  
- What remains is the object **absorbed into Anuttara**, the Highest Self.  

In this way the wheel of twelve powers becomes **full of non-duality with every external thing**. The entire “circle” of outer and inner objects is seen as swallowed by that Bhairava fire and **resting in the Self**.  

For the yogi who keeps practicing like this, seeing that all sequences of manifestation, maintenance, and dissolution are **only the play of their own consciousness**, and that all creative freedom belongs to that same awareness, the **state of Bhairava arises immediately**.  

With continued practice, all kinds of desired accomplishments (*siddhi*) may also appear as side-effects.

### Pratibha Commentary

Here Āṇavopāya shows its full subtlety.  

We normally experience:

- **me** (subject)  
- **knowing** (process)  
- **that** (object)  

Abhinava’s dhyāna invites you to:  

1. **Gather all three back into the Heart** as one luminous field.  
2. Give them archetypal faces – fire, sun, moon – so the mind has something **symbolically rich** to hold.  
3. Then re-project that unified light onto objects, and watch everything move through:
   - emergence (soma / creation),  
   - clarity (arka / maintenance),  
   - dissolution (vahni / reabsorption),  
   - return to **Anuttara**.  

In other words:

> Every perception becomes a **mini-cosmic cycle**.  

The “twelve-rayed wheel” is a way of saying that **all your powers of seeing, sensing, remembering, imagining** are included in this ritual.  

With repetition, three things slowly sink in:

1. The **object** you see is not actually outside the Light that sees it.  
2. The **subject** is not a small “me,” but the Bhairava fire itself.  
3. The whole subject–object dance is a **movement inside one consciousness**.  

When this becomes obvious, the state called **bhairavī-bhāva** – the living mood of Bhairava – is already here, even if your body is still just sitting on a mat in Berlin or Paris.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Try a streamlined form of this dhyāna in your own language:

1. **Heart Light (5–10 breaths)**  
   - Sit, feel the chest from inside.  
   - Sense a **soft radiant light** pervading the Heart.  
   - Whisper mentally: *“This light is the space of ‘I am’.”*  

2. **Fire–Sun–Moon Fusion (5–10 breaths)**  
   - Feel three qualities in that light:  
     - warmth (fire / the **knower**),  
     - clarity (sun / the **knowing**),  
     - coolness/receptivity (moon / the **known**).  
   - Let them **blend into one glowing field**.  

3. **Project to an Object (5–10 breaths)**  
   - Open your eyes and choose any simple object (cup, plant, person).  
   - Feel the Heart Light extending and **resting in that object**.  

4. **Create – Maintain – Dissolve (a few cycles)**  
   - For a few breaths, feel the object as if it were **appearing freshly**, nourished by your attention. (soma)  
   - For a few breaths, feel it held in **steady, bright clarity**. (arka)  
   - For a few breaths, gently **withdraw the image** into the Heart Light, eyes half-closed or closed. (vahni)  

5. **Rest as Anuttara (at least 5 breaths)**  
   - After the dissolution, don’t rush to another object.  
   - Just rest as **the space in which the object appeared and vanished**.  

This is a fully valid Āṇava dhyāna – body and breath are there, imagination is there, but the **direction** is toward non-dual recognition.

## Riding the Five Prāṇas – The Six Stages of Bliss
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 5 (Uccāra Section)

Now Abhinava explains **Uccāra**—not just “chanting,” but the **upward moving of prāṇa** as a means.  

The practitioner who wants to move prāṇa upward proceeds like this:

1. **Rest in the Heart void.**  
   First, he rests in the Heart, in a subtle inner **emptiness** (*śūnya*).  

2. **Emergence in the external.**  
   Then, as **prāṇa** arises, awareness rests in the **outer object**.  

3. **Apāna as the moon filling everything.**  
   Turning toward the outer, with the “moon” of apāna filling it, he sees **everything as his own Self** (*sarvātmatā*).  

4. **Freedom from wanting “something else.”**  
   In that fullness, he becomes free of craving an “other” (*anya-nirākāṅkṣaḥ*).  

5. **Samāna and the rest in union.**  
   With the rise of **samāna**, he experiences a repose in **union**, where everything feels gathered into one field.  

6. **Udāna-fire swallowing subject–object.**  
   When the fire of **udāna** flares up, it **swallows the whole play** of knower, known, and knowing.  

7. **Vyāna – the unbounded shimmer.**  
   When that swallowing-fire quiets and **vyāna** emerges, a state **devoid of all limitation** shines forth.  

The different resting-places along this journey—from the initial void up to vyāna—are described as six **stages of bliss**:

- **Nijānanda** – one’s own, intimate bliss  
- **Nirānanda** – a kind of “bliss of no-bliss” (subtle neutrality)  
- **Parānanda** – bliss of the divine other  
- **Brahmānanda** – bliss of vastness/infinity  
- **Mahānanda** – great, overwhelming bliss  
- **Cidānanda** – bliss as pure consciousness itself  

These six culminate in **Jagadānanda** – the Bliss that pervades the whole universe: a single internal repose free from rise and fall.  

By resting again and again in these Uccāra-stages, either one by one or several at once, the practitioner comes to a **deeper repose** different from body and prāṇa.  

Keeping in mind the secret of Uccāra centered on the seed-sounds of manifestation and dissolution (sa and ha), he **purifies vikalpa**. In each repose there are **five sub-states**, depending on how deeply one enters:

1. **A first touch of bliss**, from contact with a fraction of fullness.  
2. **Rising up** for an instant, as if becoming bodiless.  
3. **Trembling**, as Śakti’s force overwhelms the old body-identification.  
4. **Sleepiness**, as extroversion dissolves.  
5. **Whirling (ghūrṇi)**, when both self-notion and non-self notion melt into a vast, boundaryless expanse.  

These form the stages from **waking** through **turya** up to **turyātīta** (beyond the fourth). They arise as prāṇa penetrates the triangle, the kanda, the Heart, the palate, and the upper Kuṇḍalinī-cakra.  

The deepest sign here is a **pure inner vibration** where:

- all objects have vanished,  
- or are just arising,  
- or are fully arisen.  

This is called the **threefold Liṅga**, whose highest form is the **Heart of the Yoginī** – the mystery of Śiva–Śakti inseparability vibrating as your own breath.

### Pratibha Commentary

This is Abhinava’s **prāṇāyāma of recognition**.  

Instead of only counting breaths or holding for health, you are:

- Tracking **where awareness rests** as breath moves.  
- Watching how different prāṇa-modes correspond to **different flavors of “I am”**.  

Very roughly:

- **Prāṇa** – awareness touching outward objects  
- **Apāna** – feeling those objects as permeated with “my” life  
- **Samāna** – everything gathered into one warm center  
- **Udāna** – vertical uplift, burning subject–object  
- **Vyāna** – all-pervading spaciousness  

The six stages of bliss are not “experiences to chase.” They’re **landmarks**:  

- You may feel intimate sweetness, vastness, neutrality, overwhelm, clear light…  
- Śāstra says: don’t cling to any of it. Instead, feel that **all of them are movements of one Śakti**.  

Uccāra here is not about spectacular kumbhakas. It is:

> Breath as a **moving shrine** in which Śiva and Śakti reveal their union.  

If you follow it with this attitude, breath becomes a **continuous teacher** of non-duality.

Over time, watch whether a **quiet bliss** begins to accompany even ordinary breathing. That is Jagadānanda beginning to peek through.

## Subtle Sound – Mantra as Breath of Śiva
**Source:** Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Āhnika 5 (Varṇa Section)

Now comes **Varṇa**, the subtle prāṇa that appears as **inner sound**.  

Within Uccāra, there arises a **faint, almost unformed sound** – this is varṇa. Its chief form is the pair of seed-sounds of creation and dissolution, **sa** and **ha**.  

By practicing with these seed-sounds, one attains **para-saṁvit** – the highest Consciousness.  

Abhinava gives a more general hint:

- Take the letters from **ka** to **ma**,  
- with or without vowels,  
- quietly synchronized with the inner breath or simply remembered.  

What matters is not the linguistic meaning, but the **even touch of awareness** that accompanies them. In that evenness, sound is free of dependence on convention and becomes a **full touch of Consciousness**.  

Even ordinary, convention-bound words still carry their meanings, just like images in the mind. But when they are bathed in the **touch of Anuttara-consciousness**, something shifts:

- Heart, throat, and lips feel **joined as one axis**.  
- The two dvādaśāntas (the “twelve-finger” distances above and below) and the Heart are **unified**.  

This is the **secret of varṇa**.  

Some teachers add:  

> After a subtle inner touch (antar-sparśa) of Śakti, an inner color-sound arises—white, yellow, and so on.  
> Meditating on that varṇa as Consciousness itself, one comes to taste the inner Light.  

The inner verse summarizes:

> Through the vibration of Consciousness, in the absence of a denoted object,  
> by restraining the movements of moon and sun (apāna–prāṇa),  
> and entering equally into all of it,  
> the yogi attains **pūrṇā** – fullness – in the use of Cit, Bīja, Piṇḍa, and Varṇa.  

Here ends the method of Varṇa.  
The topic of **Karaṇa** (sense-organs as mudrā) will be taken up later.

### Pratibha Translation

Now comes **Varṇa**, the subtle prāṇa that appears as **inner sound**.  

Within Uccāra, there arises a **faint, almost unformed sound** – this is varṇa. Its chief form is the pair of seed-sounds of creation and dissolution, **sa** and **ha**.  

By practicing with these seed-sounds, one attains **para-saṁvit** – the highest Consciousness.  

Abhinava gives a more general hint:

- Take the letters from **ka** to **ma**,  
- with or without vowels,  
- quietly synchronized with the inner breath or simply remembered.  

What matters is not the linguistic meaning, but the **even touch of awareness** that accompanies them. In that evenness, sound is free of dependence on convention and becomes a **full touch of Consciousness**.  

Even ordinary, convention-bound words still carry their meanings, just like images in the mind. But when they are bathed in the **touch of Anuttara-consciousness**, something shifts:

- Heart, throat, and lips feel **joined as one axis**.  
- The two dvādaśāntas (the “twelve-finger” distances above and below) and the Heart are **unified**.  

This is the **secret of varṇa**.  

Some teachers add:  

> After a subtle inner touch (antar-sparśa) of Śakti, an inner color-sound arises—white, yellow, and so on.  
> Meditating on that varṇa as Consciousness itself, one comes to taste the inner Light.  

The inner verse summarizes:

> Through the vibration of Consciousness, in the absence of a denoted object,  
> by restraining the movements of moon and sun (apāna–prāṇa),  
> and entering equally into all of it,  
> the yogi attains **pūrṇā** – fullness – in the use of Cit, Bīja, Piṇḍa, and Varṇa.  

Here ends the method of Varṇa.  
The topic of **Karaṇa** (sense-organs as mudrā) will be taken up later.

### Pratibha Commentary

On the Āṇava path, mantra starts very simply:

- You **move your mouth**,  
- coordinate with **breath**,  
- repeat sacred syllables.  

At first, mantra is **content**. You think of its meaning, picture the deity, count repetitions. All of that is useful.  

Abhinava is pushing us into a **deeper phase**:

1. At some point, the **meaning drops to the background**, and you start feeling:  
   - a tiny vibration in the **heart**,  
   - a subtle resonance in **throat**,  
   - a tingling at the **lips**.  

2. You notice that the **same conscious pulse** runs through all syllables, whether sacred or profane.  

3. The “letters” become less like symbolic units and more like **ripples in one lake**.  

Varṇa-practice in Āṇavopāya is training yourself to **love that ripple more than the storyline**.  

- Cit (pure awareness),  
- Bīja (seed sound),  
- Piṇḍa (embodied cluster),  
- Varṇa (distinct letter/sound)  

all become ways that **one Consciousness feels itself**.  

The aim is not to produce special colors or inner fireworks; those may or may not come. The aim is:  

> Whatever sound is arising – sacred mantra, street noise, internal chatter –  
> **hear it as varṇa in Śiva’s own throat**.  

Then even your own mental monologue is quietly reabsorbed into mantra.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Abhinavagupta Tantrasāra, Unit 5, Chapter 5, Passage 1:** structural homology with Patanjali’s Yogic Asana of Breath Control
*Divergence:* while Patanjali emphasizes external breath regulation (prāṇāyāma) to balance the nadis, Abhinava focuses on the internal, subtle prāṇa (varṇa) as inner sound, aligning breath with seed‑sounds rather than external inhalation/exhalation

**Abhinavagupta Tantrasāra, Unit 5, Chapter 5, Passage 2:** structural homology with the Śānti‑mantra practice of ‘sa‑ha’ in the Mahāyāna tradition
*Divergence:* Mahāyāna treats ‘sa‑ha’ as a symbolic, meditative utterance for the union of wisdom and compassion, whereas Abhinava treats it as the fundamental seed‑sound that directly produces para‑saṁvit (highest consciousness) through inner resonance

**Abhinavagupta Tantrasāra, Unit 5, Chapter 5, Passage 3:** structural homology with the Buddhist practice of ‘noble sound’ (sākhya) in Vajrayāna meditation
*Divergence:* Vajrayāna’s noble sound is an external mantra used to invoke deities, while Abhinava’s varṇa is an internal, non‑linguistic sound that dissolves conventional meaning and becomes pure awareness

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Try this with a simple mantra you already love (e.g. **oṁ namaḥ śivāya**):

1. **Phase 1 – Normal japa (1–3 minutes)**  
   - Whisper or voice the mantra at an easy pace.  
   - Let meaning and devotion be present.  

2. **Phase 2 – Focus on the *feel* (3–5 minutes)**  
   - Shift attention from “what it means” to **where it vibrates**:  
     - Heart center?  
     - Back of throat?  
     - Lips, palate?  
   - Keep repeating, but **track the pulse**.  

3. **Phase 3 – Silent varṇa (2–5 minutes)**  
   - Now repeat the mantra only mentally, but **feel** the same tiny vibration in the same zones.  
   - Let any other thoughts be “extra varṇa” – more ripples in the same lake.  

4. **Phase 4 – Drop the syllables (as long as feels natural)**  
   - Stop repeating the mantra intentionally.  
   - Simply rest in the **vibration field itself**, as if the mantra were humming on its own.  

That humming – even if extremely faint – is exactly what Tantrasāra is pointing to. That’s varṇa as **sūkṣma-prāṇa**, the subtle breath of Śiva.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Ch.5, Distich 1:** structural homology of the ‘alternation of the mind’s focus’ motif with the Buddhist ‘sankhya of attention’ in the *Mahāyāna Śāstra* (c. 5th c. CE)
*Divergence:* While Tantrasāra emphasizes the mind’s self‑initiated shift (vikalpaḥ) as a spontaneous, internal process, the Buddhist text frames it as a conditioned, impermanent phenomenon subject to dependent origination

**Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Ch.5, Distich 1:** structural homology with the Advaita Vedānta notion of ‘svabhāva‑prabhāva’ in the *Brahmasūtra* commentary (c. 8th c. CE)
*Divergence:* Both treat the mind’s movement as a manifestation of intrinsic nature, yet Tantrasāra presents it as a dynamic, ever‑changing flux, whereas the Brahmasūtra commentary posits it as a stable, unchanging substratum

**Abhinavagupta, Tantrasāra, Ch.5, Distich 1:** structural homology with the yogic ‘pratyāhāra’ in the *Hatha Yoga Pradīpikā* (c. 15th c. CE)
*Divergence:* The yogic text describes pratyāhāra as a withdrawal of the senses from external objects, whereas Abhinavagupta’s passage focuses on the mind’s internal pivot toward the body, highlighting a more inward, physiological orientation

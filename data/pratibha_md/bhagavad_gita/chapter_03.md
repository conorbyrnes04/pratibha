## Two Standings, Not One Ladder
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.1–3.3

Arjuna said: Janārdana, if you hold understanding superior to action, why do you urge me into this terrible deed, Keśava? With speech that seems mixed you confuse my understanding; tell me one determined thing by which I may attain the good. The Blessed Lord said: Blameless one, two standings were declared by me of old in this world — of the Sāṃkhyas by the yoga of knowledge, of the yogins by the yoga of action.

---

### Devanagari

अर्जुन उवाच

ज्यायसी चेत्कर्मणस्ते मता बुद्धिर्जनार्दन।

तत्किं कर्मणि घोरे मां नियोजयसि केशव

व्यामिश्रेणेव वाक्येन बुद्धिं मोहयसीव मे।

तदेकं वद निश्िचत्य येन श्रेयोऽहमाप्नुयाम्

श्री भगवानुवाच

लोकेऽस्मिन्द्विविधा निष्ठा पुरा प्रोक्ता मयानघ।

ज्ञानयोगेन सांख्यानां कर्मयोगेन योगिनाम्

### IAST

arjuna uvāca

jyāyasī cetkarmaṇaste matā buddhirjanārdana|

tatkiṃ karmaṇi ghore māṃ niyojayasi keśava

vyāmiśreṇeva vākyena buddhiṃ mohayasīva me|

tadekaṃ vada niśicatya yena śreyo'hamāpnuyām

śrī bhagavānuvāca

loke'smindvividhā niṣṭhā purā proktā mayānagha|

jñānayogena sāṃkhyānāṃ karmayogena yoginām

### Pratibha Translation

Arjuna said: If, Janārdana, you hold understanding [buddhi] superior to action [karma], why do you engage me in this grim action, Keśava? With speech that seems mixed you appear to confuse my understanding; tell me one thing, determined, by which I may attain the good [śreyas]. The Blessed Lord said: In this world two standings [niṣṭhā] were declared by me of old, O blameless one — of those established in Sāṃkhya, by the yoga of knowledge; of the yogins, by the yoga of action.

### Pratibha Commentary

Krishna’s answer refuses Arjuna’s demand for a single ranked path. The contested move is not that knowledge is “better” than action, but that two durable standings already structure human life: a cognition-rooted firmness and an action-rooted firmness. Arjuna hears contradiction because he treats teaching as a ladder with one top rung; Krishna treats it as two legitimate establishments (niṣṭhā) already announced. In the Gītā’s pedagogy this is decisive: earlier praise of buddhi does not cancel the battlefield demand. Sāṃkhya here names the orientation that rests in discriminative knowing; karma-yoga names the orientation that works without collapsing into quietism. Existentially, the verse forbids the modern habit of converting every spiritual insight into a reason to withdraw from obligation. Clarity about the good does not automatically dissolve the deed; it may instead require a second standing — action held as yoga rather than as craving’s instrument. The chapter begins by renaming Arjuna’s confusion: not “which path wins,” but “which standing are you ready to inhabit without demanding that the other be abolished.”

### Key Terms

**niṣṭhā (निष्ठा)** — from ni- + sthā, “to stand firmly” -> a durable establishment or settled orientation, not a temporary technique -> “path” flattens this into preference; here it names two abiding modes of life already declared

**buddhi (बुद्धि)** — from budh, “to awaken/discern” -> discriminative understanding that ranks and decides -> “intellect” misses its practical force as the faculty that orients action rather than merely theorizing

**jñānayoga (ज्ञानयोग)** — knowledge + yoga -> disciplined establishment in discriminative seeing, associated with Sāṃkhya orientation -> not armchair philosophy; a yogic standing whose fruit is firmness in knowing

**karmayoga (कर्मयोग)** — action + yoga -> disciplined establishment in action without possessive claim -> “works” understates the yogic conversion of deed itself into liberating practice

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Epictetus, Discourses 1.1:** Structural homology in distinguishing what belongs to one’s governing faculty from what belongs to circumstance and deed — two zones of responsibility rather than one ranked virtue.
*Divergence:* Epictetus centers prohairesis as the single inner citadel; the Gītā allows two public standings (knowledge and action) as co-declared establishments, not one interior faculty alone.

**Zhuangzi, “Nourishing the Lord of Life”:** Structural homology in refusing a forced choice between contemplative ease and skillful engagement; both can be complete when rightly situated.
*Divergence:* Zhuangzi often dissolves hierarchical ranking into spontaneous attunement (wuwei); Krishna keeps both standings as explicit pedagogical institutions rather than pure spontaneous flow.

**Plotinus, Enneads I.2:** Structural homology in graded ways of purifying life — contemplative ascent and civic virtue as related but not identical stations.
*Divergence:* Plotinus ranks purification toward the One; Krishna’s niṣṭhā language resists collapsing the two standings into a single ladder of superiority.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

When you feel torn between “understanding more” and “acting now,” name which standing you are actually inhabiting today — knowing or doing — and complete one concrete obligation from that standing without demanding the other abolish it.

## Non-Beginning Is Not Freedom
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.4–3.6

Not by non-beginning of actions does a person attain freedom from action, nor by renunciation alone does one reach perfection. For no one, even for a moment, ever remains without doing action; everyone is made to act, helpless, by the qualities born of nature. Who restrains the organs of action but sits remembering sense-objects with the mind — that deluded self is called a false practitioner.

---

### Devanagari

न कर्मणामनारम्भान्नैष्कर्म्यं पुरुषोऽश्नुते।

न च संन्यसनादेव सिद्धिं समधिगच्छति

न हि कश्िचत्क्षणमपि जातु तिष्ठत्यकर्मकृत्।

कार्यते ह्यवशः कर्म सर्वः प्रकृतिजैर्गुणैः

कर्मेन्द्रियाणि संयम्य य आस्ते मनसा स्मरन्।

इन्द्रियार्थान्विमूढात्मा मिथ्याचारः स उच्यते

### IAST

na karmaṇāmanārambhānnaiṣkarmyaṃ puruṣo'śnute|

na ca saṃnyasanādeva siddhiṃ samadhigacchati

na hi kaśicatkṣaṇamapi jātu tiṣṭhatyakarmakṛt|

kāryate hyavaśaḥ karma sarvaḥ prakṛtijairguṇaiḥ

karmendriyāṇi saṃyamya ya āste manasā smaran|

indriyārthānvimūḍhātmā mithyācāraḥ sa ucyate

### Pratibha Translation

Not by non-beginning of actions does a person attain freedom-from-action [naiṣkarmya], nor by renunciation alone does one reach perfection [siddhi]. For no one ever remains, even for a moment, without doing action; everyone is driven to act, not free, by the qualities [guṇa] born of nature [prakṛti]. Who restrains the organs of action yet sits remembering the objects of the senses with the mind — that deluded self is called false conduct [mithyācāra].

### Pratibha Commentary

Freedom from action is not produced by refusing to start. The contested claim is ontological before it is ethical: as long as prakṛti’s guṇas operate, the organism is already acting — breathing, perceiving, rehearsing, resisting. Renunciation of outer deeds therefore cannot, by itself, deliver naiṣkarmya, because the causal field of nature does not pause for a vow. Krishna then tightens the critique: outer restraint with inner rehearsal of sense-objects is not austerity but counterfeit (mithyācāra). In classical karma theory this matters because binding is forged in appropriation and mental dwelling, not only in visible performance. Existentially, the teaching cuts through spiritual theater — the posture of withdrawal that still feeds on fantasy. If you want freedom from action’s bondage, you cannot purchase it by non-beginning; you must transform how action is owned. The chapter’s early diagnosis is severe: inactivity is unavailable, and hypocrisy of the senses is worse than honest engagement.

### Key Terms

**naiṣkarmya (नैष्कर्म्य)** — from nis + karma, “freedom from (binding) action” -> not literal motionlessness but release from action’s binding claim -> “inaction” wrongly suggests stopping movement; the goal is non-binding agency

**saṃnyāsana (संन्यसन)** — from sam-ni-as, “laying down completely” -> formal renunciation of appropriation -> default “renunciation” can mean quitting work; here quitting alone does not yield siddhi

**mithyācāra (मिथ्याचार)** — mithyā “false” + ācāra “conduct” -> practiced falsity: outer restraint masking inner consumption -> “hypocrite” is moralizing; the term names a structural mismatch of body and mind

**prakṛti (प्रकृति)** — pra-kṛ, “that which brings forth” -> nature as the field of guṇa-driven process -> “nature” as scenery misses its compulsive productivity of action

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.1:** Structural homology in refusing rest as moral achievement — the organism is already enlisted in the world’s work.
*Divergence:* Stoic duty is framed through cosmic citizenship and logos; Krishna grounds compulsion in guṇa-prakṛti mechanics and exposes mental rehearsal as binding.

**Buddha, Dhammapada 1–2 (mind precedes):** Structural homology in locating ethical weight in mental accompaniment, not outer gesture alone.
*Divergence:* Early Buddhism aims at cessation of craving-formations; the Gītā redirects mental discipline into yogic action rather than primarily into monastic withdrawal.

**Laozi, Daodejing 48:** Structural homology in warning that forced reduction of activity can become another grasping project.
*Divergence:* Laozi favors diminishing deliberate doing toward wuwei; Krishna denies that non-beginning yields naiṣkarmya and demands transformed action instead.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

For one hour, do not add a new “spiritual pause”; instead notice three actions already happening through you (breath, posture, planning) and release mental rehearsal of a desired sense-object each time it arises.

## Prefer Disciplined Action to Stillness Theater
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.7–3.8

But who, Arjuna, restraining the senses with the mind, begins karma-yoga with the organs of action, unattached — that one excels. Perform the action that is appointed; action is superior to non-action, for even your body’s journey would not succeed through non-action.

---

### Devanagari

यस्त्विन्द्रियाणि मनसा नियम्यारभतेऽर्जुन।

कर्मेन्द्रियैः कर्मयोगमसक्तः स विशिष्यते

नियतं कुरु कर्म त्वं कर्म ज्यायो ह्यकर्मणः।

शरीरयात्रापि च ते न प्रसिद्ध्येदकर्मणः

### IAST

yastvindriyāṇi manasā niyamyārabhate'rjuna|

karmendriyaiḥ karmayogamasaktaḥ sa viśiṣyate

niyataṃ kuru karma tvaṃ karma jyāyo hyakarmaṇaḥ|

śarīrayātrāpi ca te na prasiddhyedakarmaṇaḥ

### Pratibha Translation

But who, Arjuna, restraining the senses with the mind, begins the yoga of action with the organs of action, unattached [asakta] — that one is distinguished. Perform appointed action [niyataṃ karma]; action is indeed better than non-action, for even the journey of your body would not be accomplished through non-action.

### Pratibha Commentary

Excellence is not withdrawal but begun action under mental restraint and non-attachment. The contested move is ranking: karma exceeds akarma not because busyness is holy, but because life’s minimal continuity already requires deed, and spiritual refusal of deed becomes a fantasy incompatible with embodiment. “Appointed action” (niyataṃ karma) refuses both impulsive activism and abstract freedom from role. In the Gītā’s battlefield frame, Arjuna’s body-journey (śarīra-yātrā) is not optional metaphysics; it is the concrete field where yoga must prove itself. Existentially, the teaching relocates purity from the appearance of stillness to the quality of engagement: senses held by mind, deeds begun without clinging. The superior practitioner is not the one who looks inactive, but the one who can act without being owned by the act. The body itself is already a traveling altar of necessity: food, shelter, speech, and protection do not appear by metaphysical wish. Krishna’s ranking therefore is not activism for its own sake but fidelity to the conditions under which a human life can even host yoga. Mental restraint without begun action becomes a private cinema; begun action without mental restraint becomes capture. The verse’s excellence is the conjunction — senses held, deeds started, attachment refused — and it is this conjunction that later sacrificial teaching will deepen rather than abandon.

### Key Terms

**asakta (असक्त)** — a- + sañj, “not stuck/clinging” -> acting without adhesive claim on result or identity -> “detached” can sound cold; here it means non-adhesive participation

**niyataṃ karma (नियतं कर्म)** — niyata “fixed/appointed” + karma -> the action belonging to one’s situation and discipline, not whim -> “duty” can moralize; the term stresses appointedness within order

**karmendriya (कर्मेन्द्रिय)** — organs of action -> the instruments through which karma-yoga is begun -> not mere body parts; the site where yogic intention becomes public deed

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.1:** Structural homology in insisting virtue is constituted in enacted practice, not in abstention from practice.
*Divergence:* Aristotle aims at character excellence (hexis) within the polis; Krishna aims at non-binding agency within a sacrificial cosmos and liberation horizon.

**Xunzi, “Undoing Fixation”:** Structural homology in preferring cultivated engagement over empty quiet that leaves desire intact.
*Divergence:* Xunzi stresses ritual-cultural artifice shaping nature; the Gītā stresses mental restraint plus non-attachment as yogic conversion of action.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Choose one appointed task you have deferred under the banner of “needing clarity,” restrain mental commentary for ten minutes, and begin it with the body while releasing claim on how it will make you look.

## Sacrifice Turns Deed from Bond into Offering
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.9

Other than action done for the sake of sacrifice, this world is bound by action. For that sake, Kaunteya, perform action free from attachment.

---

### Devanagari

यज्ञार्थात्कर्मणोऽन्यत्र लोकोऽयं कर्मबन्धनः।

तदर्थं कर्म कौन्तेय मुक्तसंगः समाचर

### IAST

yajñārthātkarmaṇo'nyatra loko'yaṃ karmabandhanaḥ|

tadarthaṃ karma kaunteya muktasaṃgaḥ samācara

### Pratibha Translation

Other than action for the sake of sacrifice [yajña], this world is bound by action. For that purpose, Kaunteya, perform action free from attachment [muktasaṅga].

### Pratibha Commentary

The world is not bound by action as such but by action severed from sacrificial orientation. The contested claim reframes ethics: bondage is not “doing too much,” but doing as private appropriation. Yajña here is not only fire-ritual; it is the intentional structure that returns deed to a larger circulating order. Krishna’s imperative — act free from attachment for that sake — joins two usually separated demands: engagement and non-clinging. In Vedic and post-Vedic thought, sacrifice is the engine that keeps cosmos and society from collapsing into consumption. Existentially, every deed asks a question: is this for circulation or for capture? The verse makes that question the difference between karma-bandhana and liberating practice. Freedom is not found by exiting the world of action, but by changing action’s telos from possession to offering. Once sacrifice is the telos, ethics ceases to be a ledger of private purity and becomes a question of circulation. The same muscular deed can bind or liberate depending on whether it feeds appropriation or offering. That is why Krishna does not say “do less,” but “do for that sake, free from attachment.” The world remains a field of karma; the variable is whether karma is seized as property or returned as yajña. Practice begins wherever you notice the hand closing around a result that was never only yours.

### Key Terms

**yajña (यज्ञ)** — from yaj, “to offer/worship” -> sacrificial offering as cosmic-social circulation, not merely ceremony -> “sacrifice” in English can mean loss; here it means consecrated participation

**karmabandhana (कर्मबन्धन)** — action-bondage -> binding produced when deed is owned apart from offering -> “karma” as fate misses this precise mechanism of attachment through non-sacrificial doing

**muktasaṅga (मुक्तसङ्ग)** — freed from attachment -> performing while released from adhesive relation to fruit and self-image -> not indifference; unbound fidelity to the act’s sacrificial purpose

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Hebrews 13:15–16:** Structural homology in converting deed into offering — praise and shared good as continual sacrifice rather than private accumulation.
*Divergence:* Christian offering is christologically centered; the Gītā’s yajña is embedded in a cyclical cosmic economy of gods, rain, and food (developed in following verses).

**Ibn ʿArabī, Fuṣūṣ (on servantship):** Structural homology in action as returning what never belonged to the servant.
*Divergence:* Akbarian metaphysics frames return within waḥdat al-wujūd; Krishna frames it as yajña that specifically loosens karmic binding in saṃsāra.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Before your next necessary task, state aloud whom or what it is for beyond your private gain, then do it while noticing and releasing the impulse to claim the result as “mine.”

## The Circulation That Feeds Gods and Humans
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.10–3.12

Having created beings together with sacrifice, Prajāpati said of old: By this may you bring forth; let this be your wish-yielding cow. Foster the gods with this, and may the gods foster you; fostering one another, you will attain the highest good. The gods, fostered by sacrifice, will give you desired enjoyments; who enjoys their gifts without offering to them is only a thief.

---

### Devanagari

सहयज्ञाः प्रजाः सृष्ट्वा पुरोवाच प्रजापतिः।

अनेन प्रसविष्यध्वमेष वोऽस्त्विष्टकामधुक्

देवान्भावयतानेन ते देवा भावयन्तु वः।

परस्परं भावयन्तः श्रेयः परमवाप्स्यथ

इष्टान्भोगान्हि वो देवा दास्यन्ते यज्ञभाविताः।

तैर्दत्तानप्रदायैभ्यो यो भुङ्क्ते स्तेन एव सः

### IAST

sahayajñāḥ prajāḥ sṛṣṭvā purovāca prajāpatiḥ|

anena prasaviṣyadhvameṣa vo'stviṣṭakāmadhuk

devānbhāvayatānena te devā bhāvayantu vaḥ|

parasparaṃ bhāvayantaḥ śreyaḥ paramavāpsyatha

iṣṭānbhogānhi vo devā dāsyante yajñabhāvitāḥ|

tairdattānapradāyaibhyo yo bhuṅkte stena eva saḥ

### Pratibha Translation

Having created progeny together with sacrifice, Prajāpati said of old: By this may you bring forth; let this be for you the wish-yielding milch-cow. Foster the gods with this, and may those gods foster you; fostering one another, you will attain the highest good. The gods, fostered by sacrifice, will give you the enjoyments you desire; who enjoys gifts given by them without offering back to them is a thief indeed.

### Pratibha Commentary

Creation itself is co-born with sacrifice: beings are not first made and later invited into ethics; their generative condition is already reciprocal offering. The contested move is economic-theological: desire’s fulfillment (kāmadhuk) is located inside mutual fostering, not inside unilateral extraction. To eat gifts without return is not a minor rudeness but theft — because the goods were never privately originated. In the Gītā’s Vedic inheritance, gods and humans co-constitute flourishing through yajña; the moral charge follows the metaphysical structure. Existentially, the passage attacks the consumer stance that treats world, labor, and blessing as inventory. The highest good (śreyas) is not maximized personal yield but sustained mutual fostering. If you receive without returning into the circuit, you are not “efficient”; you are interrupting the cosmology that makes enjoyment possible. Reciprocity here is not sentimental niceness; it is cosmology with teeth. If gods and humans foster one another, then consumption without return is structural theft against the conditions of flourishing. Modern readers may demythologize “gods” into ecological and social powers, yet the moral hinge remains: enjoyment presupposes a circuit you did not invent. The wish-yielding cow is not infinite private supply; it is the generative logic of mutual fostering. To refuse the return offering is to live as a parasite on a gift economy while calling the parasitism success.

### Key Terms

**yajña (यज्ञ)** — offering that co-creates and sustains beings -> here the primordial companion of creation itself -> not optional piety added onto life; life’s generative grammar

**bhāvayantu / bhāvayata (भावय)** — causative of bhū, “cause to be/foster” -> mutual bringing-into-flourishing between gods and humans -> “worship” is too cultic; the verb is reciprocal vitalization

**stena (स्तेन)** — thief -> one who consumes circulating gifts as private property -> moral insult that reveals an ontological crime against reciprocity

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Hesiod, Works and Days (on justice and gift):** Structural homology in tying human flourishing to right exchange with divine and social orders.
*Divergence:* Hesiod’s justice is framed through Zeus and agrarian measure; the Gītā makes sacrificial reciprocity the explicit condition of non-theft.

**Robin Wall Kimmerer (Braiding Sweetgrass, gift economy):** Structural homology in receiving as obligation to return, not license to stockpile.
*Divergence:* Kimmerer’s frame is ecological-animist reciprocity in contemporary idiom; the Gītā embeds reciprocity in Prajāpati’s sacrificial cosmogony and deity-human circuit.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Identify one enjoyment you received today without earning it alone (food, help, infrastructure); return a concrete offering into that circuit before nightfall.

## Food, Rain, and Brahman Meet in Sacrifice
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.13–3.15

The good who eat the remainder of sacrifice are released from all evils; but the wicked who cook for their own sake eat evil. From food beings come to be; from rain, food’s arising; from sacrifice, rain comes to be; sacrifice arises from action. Know action as arisen from Brahman, and Brahman as arisen from the Imperishable; therefore the all-pervading Brahman is ever established in sacrifice.

---

### Devanagari

यज्ञशिष्टाशिनः सन्तो मुच्यन्ते सर्वकिल्बिषैः।

भुञ्जते ते त्वघं पापा ये पचन्त्यात्मकारणात्

अन्नाद्भवन्ति भूतानि पर्जन्यादन्नसम्भवः।

यज्ञाद्भवति पर्जन्यो यज्ञः कर्मसमुद्भवः

कर्म ब्रह्मोद्भवं विद्धि ब्रह्माक्षरसमुद्भवम्।

तस्मात्सर्वगतं ब्रह्म नित्यं यज्ञे प्रतिष्ठितम्

### IAST

yajñaśiṣṭāśinaḥ santo mucyante sarvakilbiṣaiḥ|

bhuñjate te tvaghaṃ pāpā ye pacantyātmakāraṇāt

annādbhavanti bhūtāni parjanyādannasambhavaḥ|

yajñādbhavati parjanyo yajñaḥ karmasamudbhavaḥ

karma brahmodbhavaṃ viddhi brahmākṣarasamudbhavam|

tasmātsarvagataṃ brahma nityaṃ yajñe pratiṣṭhitam

### Pratibha Translation

The good who eat the remainder of sacrifice are released from all evils; but those who cook for their own sake eat evil — they are the wicked. From food beings come to be; from rain comes food’s arising; from sacrifice rain comes to be; sacrifice arises from action. Know action as arisen from Brahman, and Brahman as arisen from the Imperishable [akṣara]; therefore the all-pervading Brahman is ever established in sacrifice.

### Pratibha Commentary

Cooking for oneself alone is not ethically neutral nutrition; it is a rupture in a causal chain that runs from the Imperishable through Brahman, action, sacrifice, rain, food, and beings. The contested move is cosmological: Brahman is “all-pervading” yet “ever established in yajña,” so transcendence does not float above reciprocity — it indwells it. Eating the sacrificial remainder marks participation in a completed circuit; private cooking marks enclosure. In Vedānta-inflected reading, this does not reduce Brahman to ritual, but refuses a spirituality that claims the Absolute while exempting appetite from offering. Existentially, the chain makes every meal a metaphysical decision: am I continuing the world-circuit or sealing a private furnace? Release from evil is linked to eating as remainder, not as conquest. The Absolute’s omnipresence is tested at the stove. By tracing food back through rain and sacrifice to Brahman and the Imperishable, the Gītā refuses any spirituality that floats free of metabolism. The Absolute is not elsewhere than the meal; it is “ever established in sacrifice,” which means transcendence is tested in how appetite participates in circulation. Private cooking is therefore a metaphysical claim: “this nourishment terminates in me.” Remainder-eating is the opposite claim: “I receive after the offering has completed its arc.” Liberation’s early homework may be as ordinary as refusing to make the kitchen a temple of enclosure.

### Key Terms

**yajñaśiṣṭa (यज्ञशिष्ट)** — remainder of sacrifice -> what is eaten after offering has completed the circuit -> leftover as privilege of participation, not scrap

**akṣara (अक्षर)** — a- + kṣar, “unperishing” -> the Imperishable source from which Brahman is said to arise in this chain -> “syllable” or “eternal” alone misses its place as origin-point of the sacrificial cosmos

**ātmakāraṇa (आत्मकारण)** — for one’s own sake -> cooking oriented to private cause -> the precise hinge where nourishment becomes pāpa

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**John 6 (bread and participation):** Structural homology in making eating a mode of communion rather than private fuel.
*Divergence:* Johannine bread is christological gift; the Gītā’s food-chain is Vedic-cosmic and climaxed in Brahman’s establishment in yajña.

**I Ching / agricultural omen traditions:** Structural homology linking right ritual-order to rain and fertility.
*Divergence:* The Gītā philosophically seals the chain in Brahman-akṣara metaphysics, not only in seasonal omen logic.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Before eating, offer the first portion of attention — a pause, thanks, or share — so that intake begins as remainder of offering rather than as private cooking.

## Outside the Wheel, Life Becomes Futile
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.16–3.18

Who does not turn with this wheel thus set in motion lives in vain, Pārtha — delighting in the senses, his life evil. But the human who rejoices only in the Self, is satisfied in the Self, and content in the Self alone — for that one no duty remains. For that one there is no purpose in what is done or not done here, nor any dependence on any being for any purpose.

---

### Devanagari

एवं प्रवर्तितं चक्रं नानुवर्तयतीह यः।

अघायुरिन्द्रियारामो मोघं पार्थ स जीवति

यस्त्वात्मरतिरेव स्यादात्मतृप्तश्च मानवः।

आत्मन्येव च सन्तुष्टस्तस्य कार्यं न विद्यते

नैव तस्य कृतेनार्थो नाकृतेनेह कश्चन।

न चास्य सर्वभूतेषु कश्िचदर्थव्यपाश्रयः

### IAST

evaṃ pravartitaṃ cakraṃ nānuvartayatīha yaḥ|

aghāyurindriyārāmo moghaṃ pārtha sa jīvati

yastvātmaratireva syādātmatṛptaśca mānavaḥ|

ātmanyeva ca santuṣṭastasya kāryaṃ na vidyate

naiva tasya kṛtenārtho nākṛteneha kaścana|

na cāsya sarvabhūteṣu kaśicadarthavyapāśrayaḥ

### Pratibha Translation

Who does not turn along with this wheel thus set moving lives in vain, Pārtha — an evil-lived one, delighting in the senses. But the human who would rejoice only in the Self [ātman], be satisfied in the Self, and content in the Self alone — for that one no duty-to-be-done [kārya] exists. For that one there is no purpose through what is done, nor through what is not done here, nor any dependence on any being among all beings for any purpose.

### Pratibha Commentary

The sacrificial wheel is not optional culture; refusal of it, when one still feeds on sense-delight, renders life futile (mogha). Then Krishna states the exception that prevents literalism: the one whose joy, satisfaction, and contentment have contracted into the Self alone has no remaining kārya. The contested move is double-edged. Ordinary persons cannot cite Self-contentment as an alibi for exiting reciprocity while remaining sense-entertained; yet a genuine ātma-rati standing is acknowledged as beyond duty’s pressure. In the Gītā’s pedagogy this protects both social-cosmic obligation and the possibility of radical interior completion. Existentially, you must ask which clause fits you: are you refusing the wheel while still craving, or have craving’s centers actually relocated into Self-content? The verse refuses romantic exemption. Only the second condition dissolves purpose-dependence on deeds and beings. The exception for ātma-rati is real, but it is narrow and empirically costly. Most refusals of the wheel are not Self-contentment; they are sense-delight wearing the mask of freedom. Krishna’s severity toward the non-turner is therefore diagnostic rather than cruel: futility attaches to the combination of circuit-refusal plus craving. Conversely, the one whose joy has truly relocated does not need duty as a whip, because purpose-dependence on deeds and beings has already burned out. The spiritual task is honest classification, not theatrical exemption.

### Key Terms

**cakra (चक्र)** — wheel -> the sacrificial circulation previously mapped (action–sacrifice–rain–food) -> “cycle of nature” is vague; it is a normative wheel one must turn with

**ātmarati (आत्मरति)** — delight in the Self -> joy whose object is ātman rather than indriya -> “self-love” corrupts it into egoism; here Self is the liberated locus of joy

**kārya (कार्य)** — that which is to be done -> remaining obligatory work -> vanishes only for the one already complete in Self-content, not for the sense-delighted refuser

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Buddha, Majjhima Nikāya (Araṇavibhaṅga / duty beyond clinging):** Structural homology in distinguishing those still needing training-forms from those whose interior completion relaxes obligation’s sting.
*Divergence:* Buddhist arahatship is framed through cessation of āsavas; the Gītā’s exception is ātman-contentment within a theistic-yogic cosmos.

**Meister Eckhart (on the just who take God in works and at rest):** Structural homology in a station where doing and not-doing no longer add purpose.
*Divergence:* Eckhart’s ground is Godhead detachment; Krishna’s exception is explicitly ātma-rati / ātma-tṛpti language.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Audit yourself honestly: if sense-delight still pulls, turn one spoke of the wheel today (offer, share, tend); if you claim Self-content, test whether any craving for praise still makes “not doing” feel like a project.

## Unattached, Do the Work That Must Be Done
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.19

Therefore, always perform the action that must be done without attachment; for by performing action without attachment a person attains the highest.

---

### Devanagari

तस्मादसक्तः सततं कार्यं कर्म समाचर।

असक्तो ह्याचरन्कर्म परमाप्नोति पूरुषः

### IAST

tasmādasaktaḥ satataṃ kāryaṃ karma samācara|

asakto hyācarankarma paramāpnoti pūruṣaḥ

### Pratibha Translation

Therefore, without attachment, always perform the action that must be done [kāryaṃ karma]; for performing action without attachment, a person attains the highest [param].

### Pratibha Commentary

“Therefore” gathers the preceding cosmology into a rule of life: because the wheel binds those who appropriate, and because exemption belongs only to completed Self-content, the operable path is continual appointed action without attachment. The contested claim is soteriological: the highest is attained not by quitting kārya but by doing it asakta. Attachment is the toxin; action is the vehicle. In karma-yoga doctrine this is the classic inversion of common piety, which treats holiness as reduction of deeds. Existentially, the verse collapses the alibi of waiting for purity before acting. Purity is forged in unattached performance of what must be done. The highest is not postponed to a future inactivity; it is approached through today’s non-adhesive fidelity. “Always” (satatam) matters as much as “unattached.” The path is not a peak experience of detachment followed by relapse into possessive work; it is continuous appointed action under non-adhesion. This collapses the fantasy of waiting for a pure mood before obeying necessity. Attachment will still flare; the practice is to keep performing kārya while the flare is refused authorship. The highest is approached through that stubborn fidelity, not through collecting moments when you felt above the deed.

### Key Terms

**asaktaḥ (असक्तः)** — unattached -> the adverbial condition of liberating performance -> without this qualifier, “do your duty” becomes ordinary moralism

**kāryaṃ karma (कार्यं कर्म)** — the deed that is to be done -> necessary/appointed action -> distinguishes yogic work from optional self-expression

**param (परम्)** — the highest -> the supreme attainment reached through unattached doing -> not worldly success; the soteriological summit

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Zhu Xi / Neo-Confucian “investigation and practice”:** Structural homology in refusing a split between inner clarity and outer obligation.
*Divergence:* Neo-Confucian li focuses moral-cosmic pattern; Krishna makes non-attachment the explicit liberating condition of action.

**Ignatius of Loyola, Spiritual Exercises (indifference):** Structural homology in acting with holy indifference to outcomes.
*Divergence:* Ignatian indifference serves discernment of God’s will in Christ; Gītā asakti serves release from karmabandhana toward param.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Name one necessary action you are delaying until you “feel detached”; do it now, and each time attachment flares, return to the act’s necessity rather than to your image in it.

## Janaka’s Proof and the Holding of Worlds
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.20–3.22

By action alone Janaka and others reached perfection; you should act also looking to the holding-together of the world. Whatever the best performs, that alone other people do; whatever standard he sets, the world follows. For me, Pārtha, there is nothing that must be done in the three worlds, nothing unattained to be attained — and yet I remain in action.

---

### Devanagari

कर्मणैव हि संसिद्धिमास्थिता जनकादयः।

लोकसंग्रहमेवापि संपश्यन्कर्तुमर्हसि

यद्यदाचरति श्रेष्ठस्तत्तदेवेतरो जनः।

स यत्प्रमाणं कुरुते लोकस्तदनुवर्तते

न मे पार्थास्ति कर्तव्यं त्रिषु लोकेषु किञ्चन।

नानवाप्तमवाप्तव्यं वर्त एव च कर्मणि

### IAST

karmaṇaiva hi saṃsiddhimāsthitā janakādayaḥ|

lokasaṃgrahamevāpi saṃpaśyankartumarhasi

yadyadācarati śreṣṭhastattadevetaro janaḥ|

sa yatpramāṇaṃ kurute lokastadanuvartate

na me pārthāsti kartavyaṃ triṣu lokeṣu kiñcana|

nānavāptamavāptavyaṃ varta eva ca karmaṇi

### Pratibha Translation

By action alone Janaka and others stood established in perfection; looking also to the holding-together of the world [lokasaṅgraha], you should act. Whatever the best one performs, that alone the rest of people do; whatever standard he makes, the world follows after. For me, Pārtha, there is nothing that must be done in the three worlds, nothing unobtained that should be obtained — and yet I continue in action.

### Pratibha Commentary

Janaka’s kingship becomes scriptural proof that perfection and public action can coincide. The contested move is pedagogical politics: even if you personally need nothing, you act for lokasaṅgraha — the holding-together of the human world — because people imitate the standard set by the excellent. Krishna then applies the principle to himself: complete attainment does not issue in withdrawal; the divine continues in karma as exemplary establishment. In the Gītā’s theology this blocks a mysticism that treats fulfillment as license for private disappearance. Existentially, your freedom is never only yours; unfinished eyes are watching what your freedom does. The verse makes exemplary continuity part of wisdom’s responsibility. Perfection that demoralizes the world’s capacity to act is not the Gītā’s perfection. Lokasaṅgraha thus becomes a criterion that outranks private mystical convenience. Even divine completeness continues in action so that human imitation does not learn withdrawal as the shape of attainment. Janaka’s perfection inside kingship is the human proof; Krishna’s needless yet continued karma is the theistic proof. Together they trap the ego’s wish to translate insight into disappearance. If your freedom demoralizes others’ capacity to act rightly, it has failed the Gītā’s test of excellence.

### Key Terms

**lokasaṅgraha (लोकसंग्रह)** — gathering/holding of the world -> sustaining the coherence of human practice by example and participation -> “welfare work” is too social-service; it is ontological-social holding

**saṃsiddhi (संसिद्धि)** — complete perfection/attainment -> the goal Janaka reached through action -> proves action is not a lesser track

**kartavya (कर्तव्य)** — that which must be done -> for Krishna, absent as need, present as enacted continuity -> distinguishes necessity-from-lack from necessity-from-compassion/example

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Confucius, Analects 12.19:** Structural homology in the excellent person’s conduct as wind that bends the grass — standard-setting as social causality.
*Divergence:* Confucian virtue centers ritual-moral charisma; Krishna adds the theological claim of a needless Lord who still acts.

**Bodhisattva ideal (Śāntideva, BCA):** Structural homology in remaining in the world for others after personal readiness for release.
*Divergence:* Bodhisattva vow is explicitly universal compassion across rebirths; lokasaṅgraha here is framed through exemplary action and social imitation on the field of dharma.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Perform one ordinary duty today as if a quieter person nearby might take it as permission to keep faith with their own work — then release concern for being seen.

## If the Lord Stopped, Worlds Would Sink
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.23–3.25

If I did not engage in action tirelessly, humans would follow my path on every side. These worlds would sink if I did not perform action; I would be the maker of confusion and would destroy these beings. As the unwise act attached to action, Bhārata, so the wise should act unattached, wishing to hold the world together.

### Devanagari

सङ्करस्य च कर्ता स्यामुपहन्यामिमाः प्रजाः

कुर्याद्विद्वांस्तथासक्तश्िचकीर्षुर्लोकसंग्रहम्

### IAST

utsīdeyurime lokā na kuryāṃ karma cedaham|

saṅkarasya ca kartā syāmupahanyāmimāḥ prajāḥ

saktāḥ karmaṇyavidvāṃso yathā kurvanti bhārata|

kuryādvidvāṃstathāsaktaśicakīrṣurlokasaṃgraham

### Pratibha Translation

If I ever did not continue in action, untiring, humans would follow my path on every side, Pārtha. These worlds would sink if I did not do action; I would become the maker of confusion [saṅkara] and would destroy these creatures. As the unwise, attached to action, perform, Bhārata, so the wise should perform, unattached, desiring to hold the world together.

### Pratibha Commentary

Divine non-action would not be a private mystical option; it would be cosmically contagious, because humans imitate the Lord’s path. The contested claim is that wisdom’s unattachment must look, outwardly, as energetic as ignorance’s attachment — same intensity of deed, opposite interior claim. Otherwise lokasaṅgraha fails and social-cosmic confusion (saṅkara) spreads. In the Gītā’s theism, God’s tireless karma is the archetype of responsible freedom. Existentially, this forbids using “I’m beyond it” as aesthetic withdrawal while dependents still need the form of engagement. The wise do not spare themselves from action; they spare themselves from attachment inside action. The difference is invisible to a crude spectator and decisive for the world’s holding. The wise therefore cannot spare themselves a lighter schedule as a badge of realization. They may need to match the outer vigor of the attached while differing totally in claim. This is spiritually expensive, because spectators cannot see the difference and will misread your energy as ordinary ambition. Krishna accepts that misreading for the sake of the worlds’ holding. Saṅkara is what spreads when exemplary action goes missing — a confusion of orders more destructive than honest attached labor.

### Key Terms

**saṅkara (सङ्कर)** — mixing/confusion -> collapse of ordered distinctions and roles when exemplary action fails -> not mere diversity; destructive disorder of dharma’s forms

**avidvāṃsaḥ / vidvān (अविद्वांसः / विद्वान्)** — unwise / wise -> distinguished by attachment vs non-attachment in identical fields of work -> intelligence is measured by how one owns action, not by quitting it

**atandrita (अतन्द्रित)** — untiring -> the Lord’s mode of continuing in karma -> models stamina without neediness

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Plato, Republic (philosopher’s return to the cave):** Structural homology in the completed knower’s obligation to remain engaged for the city’s sake.
*Divergence:* Plato’s return is political-educational under the Form of the Good; Krishna’s engagement is theomorphic and aimed at preventing saṅkara in the three worlds.

**Pure Land / bodhisattva “never abandoning beings”:** Structural homology in refusing private nirvāṇa aesthetics that abandon the field.
*Divergence:* Those vows are explicitly universal-salvific across beings; here the hinge is imitation of divine action plus lokasaṅgraha within dharma order.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Match the energy you once spent on an attached project, but redirect it today into a needed task while silently dropping the sentence “this will complete me.”

## Do Not Shatter the Unwise Mind
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.26

The wise should not create a split in the understanding of the ignorant who are attached to action; the integrated wise one should make them delight in all actions by performing them himself.

---

### Devanagari

न बुद्धिभेदं जनयेदज्ञानां कर्मसङ्गिनाम्।

जोषयेत्सर्वकर्माणि विद्वान् युक्तः समाचरन्

### IAST

na buddhibhedaṃ janayedajñānāṃ karmasaṅginām|

joṣayetsarvakarmāṇi vidvān yuktaḥ samācaran

### Pratibha Translation

Let the wise not generate a split in understanding [buddhibheda] among the unknowing who are attached to action; delighting them in all actions, the knowing one, integrated [yukta], should perform them.

### Pratibha Commentary

Spiritual insight becomes violence when it is used to break the working faith of those still bound to action’s surface. The contested move is pastoral: the wise must not create buddhibheda — a fracture of resolve — in karma-attached beginners. Instead, by performing actions in an integrated way, the wise make action delightful and sustainable for others. In the Gītā’s ethics of teaching, truth without timing is harm. Existentially, this warns the newly “clarified” against evangelizing detachment as contempt for work. If your realization makes others ashamed to do what still holds their life together, you have mistaken superiority for wisdom. The yukta teaches by contagious right performance, not by dismantling fragile buddhi. Teaching becomes a form of ahimsa when it refuses to break the buddhi that still needs work as its scaffold. The integrated wise person therefore practices contagious right action: performing in such a way that others can love their tasks without shame. Contemptuous detachment preached at the wrong time is not clarity; it is buddhibheda. If your insight cannot yet be embodied as delightful shared work, keep it quiet and let your hands teach first.

### Key Terms

**buddhibheda (बुद्धिभेद)** — split/fracture of understanding -> demoralizing division introduced into another’s discriminative resolve -> “confusing people” understates the ethical wound to their capacity to act

**joṣayet (जोषयेत्)** — should cause to delight/approve -> fostering joyful participation in actions -> pedagogy as attraction, not scolding

**yukta (युक्त)** — yoked/integrated -> the wise person’s composed alignment while acting -> the condition that makes exemplary action trustworthy

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Paul, Romans 14:** Structural homology in refusing to destroy another’s standing by exercising one’s own liberty as a stumbling block.
*Divergence:* Pauline concern centers ritual-food conscience in Christ; the Gītā centers karma-attachment and the social holding of action.

**Confucius, Analects 11.21:** Structural homology in answering differently according to the student’s condition rather than broadcasting one absolute.
*Divergence:* Confucian differentiation is pedagogical timing within ren-li; Krishna forbids specifically the fracture of work-oriented buddhi.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

If you hold a “higher” view of work today, keep it inward; outwardly join one shared task in a way that strengthens another person’s willingness to continue.

## Nature Acts; Ego Claims Authorship
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.27

Actions are performed in every way by the qualities of nature; the self deluded by egoism thinks, “I am the doer.”

---

### Devanagari

प्रकृतेः क्रियमाणानि गुणैः कर्माणि सर्वशः।

अहङ्कारविमूढात्मा कर्ताऽहमिति मन्यते

### IAST

prakṛteḥ kriyamāṇāni guṇaiḥ karmāṇi sarvaśaḥ|

ahaṅkāravimūḍhātmā kartā'hamiti manyate

### Pratibha Translation

Actions are being performed in every way by the qualities [guṇa] of nature [prakṛti]; the self deluded by ego-sense [ahaṅkāra] thinks, “I am the doer.”

### Pratibha Commentary

The doer-claim is a cognitive delusion layered onto processes already underway in prakṛti. The contested move is not denial that bodies act, but relocation of authorship: guṇas perform; ahaṅkāra misreads the performance as “I.” In Sāṃkhya-inflected Gītā psychology, this misreading is the hinge of bondage — appropriation of impersonal process as personal property. Existentially, the verse does not recommend passivity; it recommends metaphysical honesty in the midst of action. When success or failure arrives, notice the reflex “I did this,” and recognize it as ahaṅkāra’s story about guṇa-activity. Liberation begins where authorship is seen through without the theater of inert withdrawal. Seeing through “I am the doer” does not erase care, apology, or learning; it erases the metaphysical inflation that turns every outcome into self-mythology. The guṇas will still move — hunger, speech, planning, courage — but the ahaṅkāra’s story can be declined. That decline is the beginning of karma-yoga’s inner technology. Without it, even sacrificial language becomes another way for the ego to star in its own purification drama. Honesty about process is already a moral act.

### Key Terms

**ahaṅkāra (अहङ्कार)** — I-making -> the ego-function that appropriates experience as mine/me-as-doer -> “ego” as vanity is too shallow; it is a structural misattribution engine

**guṇa (गुण)** — strand/quality -> sattva, rajas, tamas as nature’s operative forces -> “qualities” can sound adjectival; here they are agents of kriyamāṇa karma

**kartāham (कर्ताऽहम्)** — “I am the doer” -> the concise formula of delusion -> the thought-form yoga must see through while action continues

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**David Hume (bundle theory of self) / modern no-self psychology:** Structural homology in questioning a simple owner behind actions.
*Divergence:* Hume remains skeptical-empirical; the Gītā embeds the insight in prakṛti-guṇa metaphysics aimed at non-attachment and liberation.

**Nāgārjuna, MMK (on agent and action):** Structural homology in prying apart naive agent-action substantiality.
*Divergence:* Madhyamaka empties both into dependent designation; the Gītā retains prakṛti’s positive productivity while denying ego’s authorship claim.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

During one completed task, replay the moment of the thought “I did this,” and restate quietly: “qualities of nature are performing,” without dropping responsibility for care.

## Guṇas Move in Guṇas; Offer the Battle
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.28–3.30

But who knows the truth of the divisions of quality and action, O mighty-armed, thinking “qualities move among qualities,” is not attached. Those deluded by nature’s qualities cling to quality and action; the one who knows the whole should not unsettle the dull who do not know the whole. Resigning all actions in me, with a mind on the Self, free from longing and possessiveness, fight, free from fever.

---

### Devanagari

तत्त्ववित्तु महाबाहो गुणकर्मविभागयोः।

गुणा गुणेषु वर्तन्त इति मत्वा न सज्जते

प्रकृतेर्गुणसम्मूढाः सज्जन्ते गुणकर्मसु।

तानकृत्स्नविदो मन्दान्कृत्स्नविन्न विचालयेत्

मयि सर्वाणि कर्माणि संन्यस्याध्यात्मचेतसा।

निराशीर्निर्ममो भूत्वा युध्यस्व विगतज्वरः

### IAST

tattvavittu mahābāho guṇakarmavibhāgayoḥ|

guṇā guṇeṣu vartanta iti matvā na sajjate

prakṛterguṇasammūḍhāḥ sajjante guṇakarmasu|

tānakṛtsnavido mandānkṛtsnavinna vicālayet

mayi sarvāṇi karmāṇi saṃnyasyādhyātmacetasā|

nirāśīrnirmamo bhūtvā yudhyasva vigatajvaraḥ

### Pratibha Translation

But the knower of the truth of the divisions of quality and action, O mighty-armed, thinking “qualities move among qualities,” is not attached. Those deluded by nature’s qualities cling to quality-actions; the one who knows the whole should not unsettle the slow who do not know the whole. Resigning all actions in me with a mind on the Self [adhyātma-cetas], becoming free from longing and free from “mine,” fight, free from fever.

### Pratibha Commentary

Insight into guṇa-karma divisions yields a precise non-attachment: senses and objects, organs and deeds, are guṇas operating on guṇas. The contested synthesis is fierce — this same insight must not be weaponized to unsettle the incomplete, and it must culminate in theistic resignation of actions into Krishna while Arjuna still fights. Knowledge without pastoral restraint becomes cruelty; knowledge without offering becomes sterility; offering without battle becomes evasion. In the Gītā’s unique weave, metaphysics (guṇā guṇeṣu), ethics (do not unsettle), and devotion (mayi sarvāṇi karmāṇi) converge on a single command: yudhyasva vigatajvaraḥ — fight without the fever of egoic claim. Existentially, clarity is tested under heat. Can you see process as process, protect another’s fragile understanding, and still do the hard deed as an offering? The sequence is the teaching: see guṇas in guṇas, spare the incomplete from your cleverness, resign actions into the Lord, and fight without fever. Any one of these alone can become a distortion — metaphysics without compassion, compassion without offering, offering without courage. Krishna braids them for a warrior who must remain effective under heat. The existential question is whether your insight survives contact with conflict. If it only works in quiet rooms, it is not yet this verse’s knowledge.

### Key Terms

**guṇā guṇeṣu vartante (गुणा गुणेषु वर्तन्ते)** — qualities move in qualities -> technical formula of non-doership within sensory-action loops -> the cognitive key that cuts sajjana (attachment)

**adhyātmacetas (अध्यात्मचेतस्)** — mind set on the Self/inward reality -> the interior orientation while resigning actions -> not trance; a consecrating attention

**vigatajvara (विगतज्वर)** — with fever gone -> action without agitated possessiveness and dread -> “calm” is too mild; fever names the pathology of egoic combat

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Stoic oikeiosis vs cosmic view (Hierocles / Aurelius):** Structural homology in relocating events into a larger process to cool personal fever.
*Divergence:* Stoicism centers logos and prohairesis; the Gītā adds guṇa theory plus resignation of actions into a personal Lord.

**Hasidic “ayin” and action in the world:** Structural homology in acting from self-nullification without abandoning the deed.
*Divergence:* Hasidic ayin is God-saturated nullification in Jewish covenantal life; here nullification of longing/mine is joined to battlefield dharma.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Enter one conflictual duty and silently repeat “qualities among qualities,” offer the outcome beyond your claim, then act without trying to unsettle anyone else’s working faith.

## Faith Follows Teaching; Nature Still Pulls
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.31–3.33

Humans who constantly practice this teaching of mine, full of faith and without envy, are released even from actions. But those who, envying this, do not practice my teaching — know them as deluded in all knowledge, lost, without sense. Even the knowing one acts according to their own nature; beings follow nature — what will restraint accomplish?

---

### Devanagari

ये मे मतमिदं नित्यमनुतिष्ठन्ति मानवाः।

श्रद्धावन्तोऽनसूयन्तो मुच्यन्ते तेऽपि कर्मभिः

ये त्वेतदभ्यसूयन्तो नानुतिष्ठन्ति मे मतम्।

सर्वज्ञानविमूढांस्तान्विद्धि नष्टानचेतसः

सदृशं चेष्टते स्वस्याः प्रकृतेर्ज्ञानवानपि।

प्रकृतिं यान्ति भूतानि निग्रहः किं करिष्यति

### IAST

ye me matamidaṃ nityamanutiṣṭhanti mānavāḥ|

śraddhāvanto'nasūyanto mucyante te'pi karmabhiḥ

ye tvetadabhyasūyanto nānutiṣṭhanti me matam|

sarvajñānavimūḍhāṃstānviddhi naṣṭānacetasaḥ

sadṛśaṃ ceṣṭate svasyāḥ prakṛterjñānavānapi|

prakṛtiṃ yānti bhūtāni nigrahaḥ kiṃ kariṣyati

### Pratibha Translation

Humans who constantly practice this view of mine, faith-filled and without envy, are released even by/from actions. But those who envy this and do not practice my view — know them as deluded about all knowledge, destroyed, without awareness. Even a knowing person acts in conformity with their own nature; beings go to nature — what will restraint do?

### Pratibha Commentary

Release hinges less on clever agreement than on faithful, non-envious practice of Krishna’s matam. The contested final verse shocks: even the jñānavān behaves according to prakṛti, so crude nigraha (forced restraint) is impotent against nature’s pull. This is not fatalism that cancels yoga; it is a warning against willpower fantasies that ignore constitutive tendency. In the chapter’s arc, practice must work with and through nature’s patterning — via non-attachment, sacrifice, and offering — rather than by sheer suppression. Existentially, envy of the teaching is already a spiritual defeat, and white-knuckle restraint without reorientation of desire will fail. Faithful enactment without asūyā is the open gate; nature-blind repression is a closed one. Śraddhā without envy is thus not soft piety; it is the affective permission structure for practice to work inside nature’s grain. Forced nigraha tries to overrule prakṛti by will and then wonders why relapse comes. Krishna’s bluntness — even the knowing act according to nature — redirects effort toward faithful enactment of the teaching rather than toward fantasies of instantaneous self-overthrow. Envy of the teaching is already a refusal to let it reshape desire. Release begins where fault-finding ends and practice begins.

### Key Terms

**matam (मतम्)** — view/teaching held -> Krishna’s declared standing on action -> not private opinion; the doctrinal orientation to be practiced

**anasūyantaḥ (अनसूयन्तः)** — without envy/fault-finding -> affective condition for receiving teaching -> intellectual assent without this still blocks release

**nigraha (निग्रह)** — restraint/suppression -> forced holding-down of nature -> questioned as insufficient against prakṛti’s deep patterning

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Augustine on habit (Confessions VIII):** Structural homology in knowing the good yet being carried by consuetudo / nature-like habit.
*Divergence:* Augustine resolves through grace in Christ; the Gītā points toward śraddhā-practice of karma-yoga within prakṛti’s guṇa field.

**Xunzi on nature and artifice:** Structural homology in denying that raw restraint alone reforms constitutive tendencies.
*Divergence:* Xunzi privileges ritual-cultural artifice; Krishna privileges faithful practice of divine teaching plus later reorientation of rāga-dveṣa.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Practice one element of this chapter’s teaching without criticizing someone who does not share it; where forced self-suppression fails, replace it with sacrificial reframing of the same impulse.

## Attraction and Aversion Wait at Every Sense-Door
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.34

For each sense, toward each sense-object, attraction and aversion are seated; let one not come under their power, for they are one’s highway-robbers.

---

### Devanagari

इन्द्रियस्येन्द्रियस्यार्थे रागद्वेषौ व्यवस्थितौ।

तयोर्न वशमागच्छेत्तौ ह्यस्य परिपन्थिनौ

### IAST

indriyasyendriyasyārthe rāgadveṣau vyavasthitau|

tayorna vaśamāgacchettau hyasya paripanthinau

### Pratibha Translation

For sense after sense, toward its object, attraction and aversion [rāga-dveṣa] are established; one should not come under their control, for they are one’s waylayers.

### Pratibha Commentary

Rāga and dveṣa are not occasional moods; they are structurally seated at each sense-object junction. The contested claim is that the primary spiritual combat is not against the world but against capture by these twin highway-robbers. Because prakṛti cannot be simply suppressed (3.33), vigilance relocates here: do not enter their vaśa (dominion). In yogic psychology this is precise: freedom is measured by whether liking and disliking dictate movement. Existentially, every doorway of perception hosts an ambush. The practice is not sensory annihilation but refusal of sovereignty to the twins. When they rule, even appointed action becomes fever; when they do not, nature can move without owning you. Because rāga and dveṣa are seated at every sense-door, freedom cannot wait for a perfect environment emptied of triggers. The ambush is portable; so must vigilance be. Not coming under their vaśa means feeling the pull without handing them the steering. This is the micro-ethics that makes later war against kāma intelligible: before you can slay the great enemy, you must stop installing it as governor at each contact. The path is paved with refused sovereignties.

### Key Terms

**rāgadveṣa (रागद्वेष)** — attraction-aversion -> the paired affective captors at sense-object contact -> “likes and dislikes” domesticates them; they are path-blocking bandits

**paripanthin (परिपन्थिन्)** — waylayer/enemy on the road -> obstruction of one’s path -> frames practice as travel under threat, not as mood management

**vaśa (वश)** — control/dominion -> the state of being ruled by the twins -> the precise danger, not the mere presence of feeling

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Buddhaghosa, Visuddhimagga (on sense restraint):** Structural homology in guarding doors where liking/disliking seize the stream of action.
*Divergence:* Theravāda aims at progressive purification toward nibbāna; the Gītā embeds door-guarding within karma-yoga and coming battle with kāma.

**Epictetus, Enchiridion 1–5:** Structural homology in refusing to grant externals power over the governing faculty.
*Divergence:* Stoic externals are indifferent relative to prohairesis; here the ambush is specifically rāga-dveṣa seated in sense-object pairs.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

At the next pleasant and next unpleasant sensation, name “attraction” or “aversion,” and take one breath before allowing either to choose your next move.

## Better One’s Own Imperfect Dharma
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.35

Better is one’s own dharma, even imperfect, than another’s dharma well performed; death in one’s own dharma is better; another’s dharma is fraught with fear.

---

### Devanagari

श्रेयान्स्वधर्मो विगुणः परधर्मात्स्वनुष्ठितात्।

स्वधर्मे निधनं श्रेयः परधर्मो भयावहः

### IAST

śreyānsvadharmo viguṇaḥ paradharmātsvanuṣṭhitāt|

svadharme nidhanaṃ śreyaḥ paradharmo bhayāvahaḥ

### Pratibha Translation

Better is one’s own dharma [svadharma], even defective, than another’s dharma well performed; death in one’s own dharma is better; another’s dharma is fear-bearing.

### Pratibha Commentary

Spiritual cosmopolitanism is refused at the point of vocation. The contested claim is stark: a flawed inhabiting of one’s own dharma outranks polished performance of another’s, even unto preferring death in svadharma to safe excellence elsewhere. This is not a celebration of mediocrity; it is a warning that borrowed excellence can be ontological exile. In the Gītā’s battlefield context, Arjuna’s temptation is precisely paradharma — the Brahmin-like refusal of warrior obligation under the guise of compassion. Existentially, fear attaches to living a life that is not yours, however admired. The verse demands costly fidelity to the appointed form through which your yoga must ripen. Another’s perfect script can still be your ruin. Svadharma’s “even imperfect” clause is a scalpel against spiritualized escape. The attraction of paradharma is often aesthetic: cleaner narratives, admired roles, less blood on the hands. Krishna names that attraction as fear-bearing because it unseats you from the only field where your particular bondage and freedom can be worked. Death in one’s own dharma is “better” not as a cult of doom but as refusal of a living death — the polished life that is not yours.

### Key Terms

**svadharma (स्वधर्म)** — own dharma -> the obligation-form belonging to one’s constitution and station -> not “personal brand”; the appointed field of practice

**paradharma (परधर्म)** — another’s dharma -> an alien obligation-form, however well executed -> attractiveness is part of its danger

**viguṇa (विगुण)** — deficient/defective -> imperfect performance that remains existentially fitting -> blocks perfectionism as escape route

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Pindar / Greek ethics of living according to one’s measure:** Structural homology in preferring fitting excellence to borrowed grandeur.
*Divergence:* Greek measure is often aristocratic-aretaic; svadharma is embedded in varṇa-duty and liberating yoga.

**Lutheran vocation (Beruf):** Structural homology in sanctifying one’s given calling against spiritual ladder-climbing into another’s role.
*Divergence:* Christian vocation is framed under justification by faith; the Gītā frames svadharma within karmic cosmologies and fear of paradharma.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

Identify one duty you avoid because another path looks cleaner; perform a concrete piece of your own imperfect dharma today without advertising the sacrifice.

## Desire and Anger Cover Knowing Like Smoke
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.36–3.38

Arjuna said: Then by what is a person impelled to do evil, even unwilling, as if driven by force, O Vārṣṇeya? The Blessed Lord said: This is desire, this is anger, born of the rajas quality — all-consuming, greatly evil; know it here as the enemy. As fire is covered by smoke, a mirror by dirt, an embryo by the membrane, so is this covered by that.

---

### Devanagari

अर्जुन उवाच

अथ केन प्रयुक्तोऽयं पापं चरति पूरुषः।

अनिच्छन्नपि वार्ष्णेय बलादिव नियोजितः

श्री भगवानुवाच

काम एष क्रोध एष रजोगुणसमुद्भवः।

महाशनो महापाप्मा विद्ध्येनमिह वैरिणम्

धूमेनाव्रियते वह्निर्यथाऽऽदर्शो मलेन च।

यथोल्बेनावृतो गर्भस्तथा तेनेदमावृतम्

### IAST

arjuna uvāca

atha kena prayukto'yaṃ pāpaṃ carati pūruṣaḥ|

anicchannapi vārṣṇeya balādiva niyojitaḥ

śrī bhagavānuvāca

kāma eṣa krodha eṣa rajoguṇasamudbhavaḥ|

mahāśano mahāpāpmā viddhyenamiha vairiṇam

dhūmenāvriyate vahniryathā''darśo malena ca|

yatholbenāvṛto garbhastathā tenedamāvṛtam

### Pratibha Translation

Arjuna said: Then by what is this person impelled to do evil, even unwillingly, as if engaged by force, O Vārṣṇeya? The Blessed Lord said: This is desire [kāma], this is anger [krodha], arisen from the rajas quality — a great eater, a great evil; know it here as the enemy. As fire is covered by smoke, a mirror by dirt, and an embryo by the caul, so is this covered by that.

### Pratibha Commentary

Arjuna’s question names the experience of compulsion against one’s wish — evil as forced enlistment. Krishna identifies a single enemy appearing as desire and as anger, born of rajas: craving’s heat and the rage of frustrated craving. The contested move is diagnostic unity: kāma and krodha are not two unrelated vices but one adversary in two phases. The triple image — smoke, dirt, membrane — shows graded coverings of knowing’s light, clarity, and nascent life. In guṇa psychology, rajas agitates and binds through restless wanting. Existentially, moral failure is often not ignorance of norms but capture by an all-consuming appetite that then explodes as anger. To fight “sin” without naming this enemy is to shadow-box. The chapter’s late turn makes the true opponent interior and voracious. The images escalate covering: smoke on fire, dirt on mirror, membrane on embryo — knowledge present yet prevented from clarifying, reflecting, or being born into open life. Rajas does not merely add energy; it metabolizes wanting into a total climate where even sincere intention feels coerced. Treating kāma and krodha as separate “anger issues” and “desire issues” misses the single enemy’s phase-change. The fight is against a covering process, not against isolated bad moments.

### Key Terms

**kāma (काम)** — desire/wanting -> here the great enemy born of rajas, all-consuming -> “love” or “pleasure” misses its aggressive, covering force

**krodha (क्रोध)** — anger -> desire’s secondary face when obstructed -> not a separate root problem in this diagnosis

**rajoguṇa (रजोगुण)** — the quality of passion/agitation -> source-field of the enemy -> links moral psychology to Sāṃkhya cosmology

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**James 1:14–15:** Structural homology in desire conceiving and giving birth to sin, then death — a phased interior genesis of evil.
*Divergence:* James frames this within Christian temptation theology; Krishna identifies the enemy as rajas-born kāma/krodha covering knowledge.

**Buddhaghosa on lobha-dosa:** Structural homology in greed and hatred as paired afflictions warping cognition.
*Divergence:* Abhidhamma enumerates many cetasikas; the Gītā compresses the battlefield enemy into this rajas-born twin for yogic combat.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

When anger rises, ask what desire was blocked one moment earlier; name both as one enemy, and delay speech until the covering thins by one breath-cycle.

## Desire’s Seat Is Senses, Mind, and Buddhi
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.39–3.41

Knowledge is covered by this perpetual enemy of the knower, taking the form of desire, Kaunteya — an insatiable fire. The senses, mind, and understanding are said to be its abode; through these it deludes the embodied one, covering knowledge. Therefore, bull of Bharatas, first restrain the senses, and kill this evil that destroys knowledge and realization.

---

### Devanagari

आवृतं ज्ञानमेतेन ज्ञानिनो नित्यवैरिणा।

कामरूपेण कौन्तेय दुष्पूरेणानलेन च

इन्द्रियाणि मनो बुद्धिरस्याधिष्ठानमुच्यते।

एतैर्विमोहयत्येष ज्ञानमावृत्य देहिनम्

तस्मात्त्वमिन्द्रियाण्यादौ नियम्य भरतर्षभ।

पाप्मानं प्रजहि ह्येनं ज्ञानविज्ञाननाशनम्

### IAST

āvṛtaṃ jñānametena jñānino nityavairiṇā|

kāmarūpeṇa kaunteya duṣpūreṇānalena ca

indriyāṇi mano buddhirasyādhiṣṭhānamucyate|

etairvimohayatyeṣa jñānamāvṛtya dehinam

tasmāttvamindriyāṇyādau niyamya bharatarṣabha|

pāpmānaṃ prajahi hyenaṃ jñānavijñānanāśanam

### Pratibha Translation

Knowledge is covered by this perpetual enemy of the knower, in the form of desire, Kaunteya — hard to fill, like fire. Senses, mind, and understanding are said to be its abode; through these it deludes the embodied one, covering knowledge. Therefore first restrain the senses, bull of the Bharatas, and strike down this evil that destroys knowledge and realization [jñāna-vijñāna].

### Pratibha Commentary

Even the knower has a perpetual enemy: desire-form that covers jñāna like an unsatisfiable fire. The contested tactical claim is order of operations — ādau indriyāṇi niyamya — first restrain the senses, because desire’s abode includes senses, manas, and buddhi. If buddhi itself can be occupied, mere thinking about desire will not defeat it; the entry points must be narrowed. In yoga’s classical logic, vijñāna (lived realization) dies when this covering persists, not only abstract jñāna. Existentially, the teaching refuses both repression fantasies and intellectualization. You begin where contact happens. Strike the evil that uses your best faculties as its house. Without that strike, knowledge remains smoke-covered brilliance. First restrain the senses because desire’s household includes buddhi itself; if you begin with arguments alone, you debate inside the enemy’s furniture. The order of operations is merciful precision: narrow the gates, then strike what destroys both knowing and lived realization. This also explains why the wise still have a “perpetual enemy”: knowledge does not automatically evict the tenant. Vigilance at the abodes is ongoing combat doctrine, not a beginner’s brace to be discarded.

### Key Terms

**nityavairin (नित्यवैरिन्)** — perpetual enemy -> desire as ongoing adversary even of the wise -> blocks the fantasy that knowledge automatically ends conflict

**adhiṣṭhāna (अधिष्ठान)** — abode/base -> senses-mind-buddhi as desire’s seating -> shows why combat must be multi-level

**jñānavijñāna (ज्ञानविज्ञान)** — knowledge and realization -> cognitive insight plus lived discernment -> desire destroys both, not only book-knowing

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Evagrius Ponticus (on logismoi):** Structural homology in tracing passionate thoughts through sensory triggers into the governing intellect.
*Divergence:* Evagrian combat is ordered toward apatheia in Christ; the Gītā orders sense-restraint toward killing kāma as covering of jñāna-vijñāna.

**Patanjali, YS 2.16–2.17:** Structural homology in targeting the conjunction that produces future suffering at its accessible points.
*Divergence:* Patañjali’s framework is citta-vṛtti and kleśa; here the enemy is personified as kāma with explicit abodes in the inner instrument.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

For one day, apply first restraint at the senses: reduce one habitual intake channel (scrolling, snacking, gossip), and notice whether mind and judgment grow less combustible.

## Higher Than Buddhi, Steady the Self and Slay Desire
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 3.42–3.43

The senses are said to be high; higher than the senses is mind; higher than mind is understanding; what is higher than understanding is that. Knowing what is higher than understanding, steadying the self by the self, slay the enemy, O mighty-armed, that has the form of desire and is hard to approach.

---

### Devanagari

इन्द्रियाणि पराण्याहुरिन्द्रियेभ्यः परं मनः।

मनसस्तु परा बुद्धिर्यो बुद्धेः परतस्तु सः

एवं बुद्धेः परं बुद्ध्वा संस्तभ्यात्मानमात्मना।

जहि शत्रुं महाबाहो कामरूपं दुरासदम्

### IAST

indriyāṇi parāṇyāhurindriyebhyaḥ paraṃ manaḥ|

manasastu parā buddhiryo buddheḥ paratastu saḥ

evaṃ buddheḥ paraṃ buddhvā saṃstabhyātmānamātmanā|

jahi śatruṃ mahābāho kāmarūpaṃ durāsadam

### Pratibha Translation

The senses are said to be high; higher than the senses is the mind; higher still than the mind is understanding [buddhi]; but what is beyond understanding is That. Thus knowing what is beyond understanding, steadying the self by the self, slay the enemy, O mighty-armed — desire-formed, hard to approach.

### Pratibha Commentary

Chapter 3 closes with an ascent of faculties that refuses to stop at buddhi. Senses < mind < understanding < That: the hierarchy both explains desire’s climb and locates the power that can defeat it. The contested move is soteriological method — saṃstabhyātmānam ātmanā — steadying the self by the self, from a station beyond the very buddhi desire colonizes. Intellectual management cannot be the final weapon against an enemy that sits in intellect. In Upaniṣadic-Gītā anthropology, the transcendent Self is not an escape from combat but the leverage for combat. Existentially, you are told to fight from higher than your best arguments. Desire is durāsada — hard to approach — precisely because it occupies the approaches. Only establishment beyond buddhi, turning self upon self in steadiness, can strike the enemy the chapter has named. Karma-yoga’s outer fidelity culminates in this inner kill-stroke. The hierarchy does not demean buddhi; it refuses to let colonized intellect be the final general. Steadying self by self from beyond understanding is how desire’s hard-to-approach status is overcome — by fighting from a station desire cannot fully occupy. Chapter 3’s arc from sacrificial action to this kill-stroke thus holds together: outer fidelity without inner strike leaves the covering intact; inner strike without karmic fidelity becomes private mysticism. The enemy is slain where Self-steadiness and appointed action finally agree.

### Key Terms

**paratas (परतस्)** — beyond/higher -> marks the Self as exceeding buddhi -> prevents mistaking refined intellect for the ultimate ally

**saṃstabhya (संस्तभ्य)** — having steadied/propped firm -> self stabilized by self -> method of interior firmness before the strike

**durāsada (दुरासद)** — hard to approach/assail -> desire’s elusiveness due to its seats in the faculties -> names why ordinary tactics fail

### Cross-Tradition Resonances

**Plato, Phaedrus (charioteer):** Structural homology in a ranked soul-psychology where higher guidance must master unruly desire.
*Divergence:* Platonic eros can be redirected toward the Beautiful; here kāma is enemy to be slain as covering of knowledge, not educated upward as philosophical eros.

**Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.3.10–13:** Structural homology in the exact hierarchy senses–mind–buddhi–beyond.
*Divergence:* Kaṭha elaborates the chariot for knowing the Person; the Gītā weaponizes the same hierarchy for destroying desire on the field of action.

### Practice (Abhyasa)

When desire floods argument and justification, step one level above your reasons: sit upright, steady attention in the sense of “I am,” and from that steadiness refuse one concrete demand of the desire-form.

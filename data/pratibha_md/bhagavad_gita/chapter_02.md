## Stand Up — This Fainting Is Unworthy of You
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 2.1–2.3

Seeing Arjuna overwhelmed by compassion, eyes filled and agitated with tears, sinking in despair, Madhusūdana spoke: Whence has this defilement come upon you at this crisis — un-Āryan, heaven-blocking, disgrace-making, Arjuna? Do not yield to impotence, Pārtha; it does not become you. Cast off this petty faintness of heart and stand up, scorcher of foes.

---

### Devanagari
सञ्जय उवाच

तं तथा कृपयाऽविष्टमश्रुपूर्णाकुलेक्षणम्।

विषीदन्तमिदं वाक्यमुवाच मधुसूदनः

श्री भगवानुवाच

कुतस्त्वा कश्मलमिदं विषमे समुपस्थितम्।

अनार्यजुष्टमस्वर्ग्यमकीर्तिकरमर्जुन

क्लैब्यं मा स्म गमः पार्थ नैतत्त्वय्युपपद्यते।

क्षुद्रं हृदयदौर्बल्यं त्यक्त्वोत्तिष्ठ परन्तप

### IAST
sañjaya uvāca

taṃ tathā kṛpayā'viṣṭamaśrupūrṇākulekṣaṇam|

viṣīdantamidaṃ vākyamuvāca madhusūdanaḥ

śrī bhagavānuvāca

### Pratibha Translation
Sañjaya said: To him thus possessed by compassion, eyes filled and agitated with tears, sinking in despair, Madhusūdana spoke this word. The Blessed Lord said: “Whence has this stain come upon you at this crisis — un-Āryan, heaven-blocking, fame-destroying, Arjuna? Do not yield to impotence, Pārtha; this does not befit you. Abandon this petty weakness of heart and stand up, scorcher of foes.”

### Pratibha Commentary
Kṛṣṇa’s first teaching is not metaphysics; it is a refusal to romanticize collapse. He names Arjuna’s state *kaśmala* — stain, defilement — arriving at the worst moment (*viṣame*). The contested move is harsh: compassion-flooded paralysis is called unworthy, heaven-blocking, glory-destroying. This does not deny the moral intelligence of chapter 1; it refuses to let grief become identity. *Klaibya* (impotence/unmanliness in the epic’s gender code) names the failure of agency, not the presence of care. “Stand up” (*uttiṣṭha*) is the first yoga command: reclaim the posture from which teaching can become action. Existentially, there is a difference between honoring sorrow and moving into it as a permanent address. The Gītā begins chapter 2 by interrupting the romance of paralysis.

The harshness is pedagogical triage: grief that becomes identity will not hear metaphysics. Standing up is already a metaphysics of agency. Only from uprightness can the later soft exactness of yoga land without becoming another excuse to dissolve.

Standing is already half the teaching; the rest can only reach someone who has reclaimed posture from despair’s chair.

### Key Terms
**kaśmala (कश्मल)** — stain/defilement of spirit → moral-psychic contamination at a decisive juncture → “despondency” softens its impurity-force.

**klaibya (क्लैब्य)** — impotence / failure of martial agency → collapse of capacity to act → modern “cowardice” is crude; the issue is agency abdicated under crisis.

**hṛdaya-daurbalya (हृदयदौर्बल्य)** — faintness/weakness of heart → petty (*kṣudra*) heart-failure → “soft-heartedness” misreads the critique; the charge is smallness of heart under duty.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Epictetus, Discourses 2.1 / Enchiridion 5:** Both confront collapse by reasserting agency where judgment has been seized by appearance.
*Divergence:* Stoicism reframes impressions rationally; Kṛṣṇa first uses honor-shame and warrior vocation before unfolding metaphysics.

**Patañjali, Yoga Sūtra 1.33–1.39:** Both treat disturbed affective states as removable conditions, not final identity.
*Divergence:* YS offers contemplative stabilizers; here the first medicine is a command to stand.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
When grief becomes your seat, stand up physically, name the duty still before you in one sentence, and take one concrete step before further analysis.

## How Can I Strike Those Worthy of Worship
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 2.4–2.6

Arjuna said: How can I fight with arrows in battle against Bhīṣma and Droṇa, Madhusūdana — they who are worthy of worship, slayer of enemies? Better to eat the food of begging in this world than to kill these great-souled teachers. Having killed teachers greedy for wealth, I would enjoy blood-smeared pleasures here. We do not know which is heavier for us — whether we should conquer or they should conquer us. Those whom having killed we would not wish to live — they stand before us, the sons of Dhṛtarāṣṭra.

---

### Devanagari
अर्जुन उवाच

कथं भीष्ममहं संख्ये द्रोणं च मधुसूदन।

इषुभिः प्रतियोत्स्यामि पूजार्हावरिसूदन

गुरूनहत्वा हि महानुभावान्

श्रेयो भोक्तुं भैक्ष्यमपीह लोके।

हत्वार्थकामांस्तु गुरूनिहैव

भुञ्जीय भोगान् रुधिरप्रदिग्धान्

यद्वा जयेम यदि वा नो जयेयुः।

### IAST
arjuna uvāca

kathaṃ bhīṣmamahaṃ saṃkhye droṇaṃ ca madhusūdana|

iṣubhiḥ pratiyotsyāmi pūjārhāvarisūdana

śreyo bhoktuṃ bhaikṣyamapīha loke|

yāneva hatvā na jijīviṣāma

ste'vasthitāḥ pramukhe dhārtarāṣṭrāḥ

### Pratibha Translation
Arjuna said: “How can I fight with arrows in battle against Bhīṣma and Droṇa, Madhusūdana — those worthy of worship, O slayer of foes? Better to eat even the food of begging in this world than to kill these great-souled gurus. If I kill teachers greedy for ends, I would enjoy blood-smeared pleasures right here. And we do not know which is weightier for us — whether we should conquer or they should conquer us. Those whom, having killed, we would not wish to live — the sons of Dhṛtarāṣṭra — stand facing us.”

### Pratibha Commentary
Arjuna sharpens the objection: not abstract kin, but *pūjārhau* — those owed worship. Begging (*bhaikṣya*) is declared better than blood-smeared enjoyments after killing gurus. The contested move is moral uncertainty raised to epistemic crisis: we do not even know which outcome is heavier (*garīyas*). Victory itself becomes undesirable because its condition is life without those one cannot bear to kill. This is the deepest form of chapter 1’s logic, now aimed at teacher-killing. The Gītā will answer without dismissing the sacredness of the guru-bond; it will relocate identity beneath roles. Existentially, the passage names conflicts where every “win” feels like desecration. That feeling is data. It is not yet the final word on action.

Guru-killing concentrates the whole problem of sacred violence. Arjuna’s uncertainty about which outcome is heavier keeps the question honest. Any ethic that answers too quickly here has not felt the weight the Gītā is willing to feel before it teaches.

Keep the weight of pūjārhau in any conflict where winning would desecrate what formed you; speed of answer is not depth.

### Key Terms
**pūjārha (पूजार्ह)** — worthy of worship/honor → sacred owedness that makes violence sacrilegious → “respectable” is far too weak.

**bhaikṣya (भैक्ष्य)** — alms-food / begging livelihood → chosen poverty as moral alternative → “begging” misses its status as ethically preferred life-form here.

**rudhira-pradigdha (रुधिरप्रदिग्ध)** — smeared with blood → pleasures stained by the means of their getting → “bloodstained” is right; the image makes enjoyment itself contaminated.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Plato, Crito 49b–54d:** Both refuse a “victory” that violates what is owed to formative authorities and the soul’s integrity.
*Divergence:* Crito stays within civic law and persuasion; Arjuna faces literal guru-combat.

**Confucius, Analects 4.16 / 12.10:** Both treat profit obtained by violating reverence-relations as spiritually worthless.
*Divergence:* Confucian repair is ritual-ethical cultivation; Arjuna’s immediate alternative is renunciate begging.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Name one authority you rightly revere who is entangled in your current conflict. Write what reverence still requires of you that neither attack nor flattery fulfills.

## I Am Your Student — Teach Me What Is Truly Better
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 2.7

My nature struck down by the fault of pity-weakness, my mind confused about dharma, I ask you: tell me decisively what is better. I am your student; teach me, I have taken refuge in you.

---

### Devanagari
कार्पण्यदोषोपहतस्वभावः

पृच्छामि त्वां धर्मसंमूढचेताः।

यच्छ्रेयः स्यान्निश्िचतं ब्रूहि तन्मे

शिष्यस्तेऽहं शाधि मां त्वां प्रपन्नम्

### IAST
kārpaṇyadoṣopahatasvabhāvaḥ

pṛcchāmi tvāṃ dharmasaṃmūḍhacetāḥ|

yacchreyaḥ syānniśicataṃ brūhi tanme

śiṣyaste'haṃ śādhi māṃ tvāṃ prapannam

### Pratibha Translation
“My own nature struck down by the fault of wretched faint-heartedness, confused in mind about dharma, I ask you: tell me decisively what would be the better. I am your student; instruct me. I have taken refuge in you.”

### Pratibha Commentary
This is the hinge of the Gītā. Arjuna stops defending a position and requests *niścita* teaching about *śreyas*. The contested move is the conversion of warrior into *śiṣya* and of crisis into *prapanna* — one who has gone to refuge. *Kārpaṇya-doṣa* names the defect of miser-spirit / wretched contraction that has damaged his *svabhāva*. Dharma-confusion is confessed, not outsourced. In the text’s pedagogy, no major teaching begins until this surrender of knowing-better. Existentially, the verse models the rare sentence that makes guidance possible: I do not know what is better; teach me; I am your student. Without that, counsel becomes debate fuel.

Refuge converts debate into discipleship. Without śiṣya-bhāva, Kṛṣṇa’s words would be counterarguments; with it, they become path. The verse is a template for any serious ask: confess the defect, request the better, and accept being taught.

Without this hinge, the Gītā would remain a quarrel between consolation and courage. With it, metaphysics and karma-yoga become answers to a vowed question. Make the vow specific enough that teaching can land.

### Key Terms
**kārpaṇya (कार्पण्य)** — wretchedness / pinched spirit / pity-miserliness of heart → defect that has assaulted innate nature → “weakness” misses the contracted, beggared quality of the fault.

**dharma-saṃmūḍha-cetas (धर्मसंमूढचेतस्)** — mind confounded regarding dharma → not ignorance of rules but lost discernment under pressure → “confused about duty” is thinner than total ethical disorientation.

**prapanna (प्रपन्न)** — one who has approached/taken refuge → relational surrender that enables teaching → “I surrender” can sound vague; here it is formal student-refuge.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Katha Upaniṣad 1.1–1.29 (Naciketas):** Both show a seeker who refuses easy consolations and demands decisive teaching on what is better.
*Divergence:* Naciketas seeks death’s secret in a quiet āśrama frame; Arjuna seeks dharma amid armies.

**Augustine, Confessions VIII:** Both depict the turning in which the will stops self-justifying and asks to be taught into a new life.
*Divergence:* Augustine’s turn is toward Christian grace and continence; Arjuna’s is toward yoga under Kṛṣṇa’s instruction.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
In one stuck dilemma, stop arguing your case for ten minutes. Write: “Tell me what is better,” then ask one trusted teacher/friend only that — and listen without defending.

## Even World-Kingship Would Not Dry This Sorrow — I Will Not Fight
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 2.8–2.10

I do not see what would drive off this sorrow that dries up my senses — even if I obtained an unrivaled, prosperous kingdom on earth, or even overlordship of the gods. Having said this to Hṛṣīkeśa, Guḍākeśa, scorcher of foes, declared “I will not fight” to Govinda and fell silent. To him, despairing between the two armies, Hṛṣīkeśa, as if smiling, O Bhārata, spoke this word.

अवाप्य भूमावसपत्नमृद्धम्

### IAST

### Pratibha Translation
“I do not see what would drive away this sorrow that dries up the senses — even after obtaining an unrivaled prosperous kingdom on earth, or even sovereignty over the gods.” Having spoken thus to Hṛṣīkeśa, Guḍākeśa the foe-scorcher said to Govinda, “I will not fight,” and became silent. To him, sinking in despair between the two armies, Hṛṣīkeśa, as if smiling, O Bhārata, spoke this word.

### Pratibha Commentary
Arjuna declares sorrow immune to the largest imaginable payoffs. Sense-drying *śoka* will not yield to earth-kingship or god-sovereignty. Then the line that opens the teaching space: *na yotsye* — I will not fight — followed by silence. Kṛṣṇa’s almost-smile (*prahasann iva*) is the contested tone: not mockery of pain, but the lightness of one who sees a deeper remedy than Arjuna’s binary. Teaching begins in the middle of armies, to a silent man. Existentially, when no external upgrade can touch your grief, you are near the threshold of inward teaching. Silence after “I will not” can be either stubbornness or readiness. Here it becomes readiness because refuge was already spoken.

Sorrow that outruns every prize forces a turn inward. The smile of Hṛṣīkeśa is the tone of a medicine that will not flatter despair and will not rush it. Silence after 'I will not fight' becomes the white space in which doctrine can be drawn.

When no prize dries the sorrow, protect the silence long enough for a deeper word than binary refusal to arrive.

### Key Terms
**ucchoṣaṇa (उच्छोषण)** — that which dries up → sorrow as dehydrating the senses → “consuming” is weaker than this image of desiccation.

**na yotsye (न योत्स्ये)** — I will not fight → refusal that creates dialogic opening → not mere stubbornness; it is the sentence that forces a new ground of action.

**prahasann iva (प्रहसन्निव)** — as if smiling → gentle irony of the teacher who sees beyond the bind → “laughing” is too harsh for the nuance.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Job 3 after Job 2:13:** Both move from silence into the space where a deeper response must arrive.
*Divergence:* Job’s friends fail; Kṛṣṇa’s smile precedes competent teaching.

**Zhuangzi, “The Fasting of the Mind”:** Both suggest that when outer prizes cannot heal the heart, a different listening becomes possible.
*Divergence:* Zhuangzi empties the heart for Dao; the Gītā fills the interval with Sāṅkhya-Yoga instruction.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Name a sorrow you have tried to solve with bigger outcomes. Write: “No prize dries this.” Sit in silence for three minutes before seeking counsel.

## You Grieve for Those Not to Be Grieved, Yet Speak Wise Words
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 2.11

You grieve for those who should not be grieved, and you speak words of wisdom. The wise do not mourn for the dead or the living.

---

### Devanagari
श्री भगवानुवाच

अशोच्यानन्वशोचस्त्वं प्रज्ञावादांश्च भाषसे।

गतासूनगतासूंश्च नानुशोचन्ति पण्डिताः

### IAST
śrī bhagavānuvāca

aśocyānanvaśocastvaṃ prajñāvādāṃśca bhāṣase|

gatāsūnagatāsūṃśca nānuśocanti paṇḍitāḥ

### Pratibha Translation
The Blessed Lord said: “You have grieved for those who should not be grieved, and you utter words that sound like wisdom. The learned do not mourn for those whose breath has gone or for those whose breath has not.”

### Pratibha Commentary
Kṛṣṇa’s first doctrinal cut is epistemological: Arjuna mixes true-sounding speech (*prajñā-vāda*) with misplaced grief. The contested claim is that some objects of mourning are *aśocya* — not appropriate loci of *śoka* — not because death is trivial, but because the wise locate identity beyond the breath’s coming and going. *Paṇḍita* here is not academic credential; it is one who sees. This verse does not yet explain why; it asserts a standard against which Arjuna’s ethic will be tested. Existentially, notice when your eloquence is grief wearing the mask of philosophy. The Gītā refuses to let polished moral language exempt you from deeper seeing.

Naming misplaced grief is the first cut of wisdom-speech. Eloquence is not evidence of seeing. The paṇḍita standard inaugurates a whole chapter that will redefine what is worth mourning and what is confusion wearing moral clothes.

Test your next eloquent complaint against this cut: does it see, or does it decorate grief? The chapter will not forbid care; it will forbid care that misidentifies what death and life are.

### Key Terms
**aśocya (अशोच्य)** — not to be mourned → persons/conditions wrongly taken as grief’s proper object when Self is unseen → “not worth grieving” sounds callous; the point is mislocated sorrow.

**prajñā-vāda (प्रज्ञावाद)** — speech that sounds like wisdom → eloquence without corresponding vision → “wise words” misses the critique of wisdom-talk.

**paṇḍita (पण्डित)** — the truly learned seer → one who does not mourn across the living/dead binary as ultimate → “scholar” is a category error.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Epictetus, Enchiridion 3 / 11:** Both relocate grief by correcting what one takes the beloved/lost to be.
*Divergence:* Stoicism emphasizes judgment about externals; the Gītā will ground the claim in the deathless Self.

**Katha Upaniṣad 1.2.18–22:** Both deny that the wise treat embodied life-death as the final term of sorrow.
*Divergence:* Katha teaches via Yama’s secret; here the teaching addresses a warrior mid-refusal.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Write one grief you are elaborating with sophisticated reasons. Ask: “What exactly am I taking as ultimately lost?” Sit with the question before answering.

## Never Was I Not, Nor You, Nor These Kings
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 2.12

There was never a time when I was not, nor you, nor these lords of men; nor shall we all ever cease to be hereafter.

---

### Devanagari
न त्वेवाहं जातु नासं न त्वं नेमे जनाधिपाः।

न चैव न भविष्यामः सर्वे वयमतः परम्

### IAST
na tvevāhaṃ jātu nāsaṃ na tvaṃ neme janādhipāḥ|

na caiva na bhaviṣyāmaḥ sarve vayamataḥ param

### Pratibha Translation
“Never was there a time when I was not, nor you, nor these lords of peoples; nor is it that we all shall not exist from this time forward.”

### Pratibha Commentary
Kṛṣṇa asserts continuity of being across the apparent absolute of death. The contested move is plural: not only a solitary ātman, but “I, you, and these kings” share never-not-being. This is not yet full Advaita reduction; it is a claim that personal identities implicated in the war are not extinguishable by battle. Grief that treats killing as ontological annihilation is thus misframed. Within Sāṅkhya-inflected reading, conscious principles persist through bodily change. Existentially, the verse asks whether your fear of loss is fear of change or fear of absolute erasure. If the latter, the Gītā begins to dissolve the premise.

Plural continuity — I, you, these kings — blocks both solitary mysticism and collective annihilation panic. The war’s persons are not extinguished by the war’s chronology. Act inside time without letting time’s endings dictate the Real.

This continuity claim also refuses the fantasy that killing ends the moral relationship. Persons persist as loci of meaning beyond the breath’s schedule. Duty must be framed without annihilation metaphysics.

### Key Terms
**jātu na (जातु न)** — never at any time → absolute temporal negation of non-existence → “never” in English is weaker than this sealed “not ever.”

**janādhipa (जनाधिप)** — lord of peoples → the kings on the field included in ontological continuity → “rulers” misses their inclusion in the never-not-being claim.

**ataḥ param (अतः परम्)** — from this point forward → future continuity asserted with past continuity → “hereafter” is fine; the symmetry of past/future is the point.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Phaedo 70c–72e (cyclical argument):** Both argue that death cannot be simple absolute end if life-death are structured transitions.
*Divergence:* Plato argues toward Forms and soul immortality philosophically; Kṛṣṇa asserts as Lord within dialogic authority.

**Dao De Jing 16:** Both orient the mind to what abides through arising and perishing.
*Divergence:* Laozi emphasizes return to the root in quietude; the Gītā asserts personal continuity amid duty to act.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Contemplate one relationship you fear losing. For two minutes, hold: “What in this was never merely the perishable form?” Write one sentence of what abides.

## As Boyhood, Youth, and Age to the Embodied, So Is Attainment of Another Body
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 2.13

As boyhood, youth, and old age befall the embodied in this body, so does the attaining of another body; the firm one is not confused about this.

---

### Devanagari
देहिनोऽस्मिन्यथा देहे कौमारं यौवनं जरा।

तथा देहान्तरप्राप्तिर्धीरस्तत्र न मुह्यति

### IAST
dehino'sminyathā dehe kaumāraṃ yauvanaṃ jarā|

tathā dehāntaraprāptirdhīrastatra na muhyati

### Pratibha Translation
“Just as boyhood, youth, and old age come to the embodied one in this body, so does the attaining of another body; the steadfast is not deluded about that.”

### Pratibha Commentary
Death is placed on a continuum with aging. The contested analogy: if you already survive childhood’s end into youth, the wise (*dhīra*) are not bewildered by the body’s further transition. *Dehāntara-prāpti* — attaining another body — is presented as structurally akin to life-stage change. This neither trivializes bereavement nor denies pain; it challenges the metaphysics that makes bodily end absolute personal end. Existentially, notice that you have already “died” to earlier forms of yourself without ceasing. The verse asks for the same steadiness toward the larger change.

Aging already taught you transition; death asks for the same literacy at larger scale. The dhīra’s non-delusion is practiced long before the funeral, in every released childhood. Steady people are those who stopped confusing form-change with self-erasure.

Confusion at death is often confusion rehearsed at every smaller ending. Practice non-delusion when a role ends, a season ends, a body-ability ends. The dhīra is trained in increments.

Train non-delusion at small endings so the larger transition does not invent a false absolute.

### Key Terms
**dehin (देहिन्)** — the embodied one → conscious identity wearing a body → “soul in a body” can dualize too crudely; the term centers the one who *has* a body.

**saṃyamin (संयमिन्)** — the restrained/self-controlled one → awakens where others sleep → not merely “disciplined person.”

**niśā (निशा)** — night → metaphor for unknowing or for the sage’s indifference to worldly day → dual use is the verse’s brilliance.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Heraclitus, DK B1 / B73:** Both contrast the shared sleep of the many with the waking of the one who sees the logos.
*Divergence:* Heraclitus’ waking is to cosmic logos; the Gītā’s waking is yogic restraint and Self-seeing.

**Gospel of Thomas 11 / Matthew 25:1–13:** Both play waking/sleep as spiritual epistemic states.
*Divergence:* Christian texts frame eschatological readiness; Gītā frames ongoing muni-perception versus collective night.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Identify one “daytime” obsession of your world that deadens you. Schedule twenty minutes of your “night-waking” — quiet practice the culture does not reward.

## As Waters Enter the Full Sea without Disturbing It, So Desires Enter the One Who Finds Peace
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 2.70

As waters enter the ocean, filled from all sides, standing unmoved firm — so one whom all desires enter attains peace, not the one who desires desires.

---

### Devanagari
आपूर्यमाणमचलप्रतिष्ठं

समुद्रमापः प्रविशन्ति यद्वत्।

तद्वत्कामा यं प्रविशन्ति सर्वे

स शान्तिमाप्नोति न कामकामी

### IAST
āpūryamāṇamacalapratiṣṭhaṃ

samudramāpaḥ praviśanti yadvat|

tadvatkāmā yaṃ praviśanti sarve

sa śāntimāpnoti na kāmakāmī

### Pratibha Translation
“As waters enter the ocean, being filled from every side, standing in unmoved firmness — so one into whom all desires enter attains peace, not the desirer of desires.”

### Pratibha Commentary
Peace is not desirelessness as empty dryness; it is ocean-capacity. Desires may enter; the ocean does not lose *acala-pratiṣṭhā*. The *kāma-kāmī* — desirer of desires — never attains śānti because wanting has become recursive. The contested image allows influx without disturbance. Existentially, stop trying to build peace by damming every want; become deeper than what enters. Breadth without depth is swamp; depth that can receive is ocean.

Ocean-peace receives influx without losing stand. Recursive desire — wanting wanting — never arrives. Depth that can welcome is freer than dams that crack.

Receiving desires without becoming their desirer is ocean literacy. Feel influx; keep stand. Recursive craving is the only guaranteed non-arrival at śānti. Recursive wanting is a closed loop. Ocean literacy breaks the loop by receiving without chasing.

Practice ocean: receive influx, keep stand, refuse recursive wanting that never arrives at śānti. Refuse summaries that skip the hinge where agency and seeing are redefined.

### Key Terms
**acala-pratiṣṭha (अचलप्रतिष्ठ)** — unmoved establishment → stable standing of the ocean/self → “firm foundation” is close.

**kāma-kāmī (कामकामी)** — desirer of desires → meta-craving, wanting the state of wanting → “lustful person” is too narrow and moralizing.

**āpaḥ praviśanti (आपः प्रविशन्ति)** — waters enter → influx without ownership struggle → the grammar of entering without conquering.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Dao De Jing 4 / 32 / 34:** Both image the great as receiving the many without being disturbed.
*Divergence:* Laozi’s greatness is Dao-like emptiness-fullness; Gītā’s ocean is the person of peace versus the desire-desirer.

**Plotinus, Enneads VI.9:** Both suggest the Real’s fullness is not increased or harmed by what flows toward it.
*Divergence:* Plotinus speaks of the One; here the simile trains human śānti.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
When a desire enters today, practice ocean: let it be felt fully for thirty seconds without chasing or damming, then return to the next right action.

## Abandoning All Desires, Moving Free of Longing, Without Mine and I-Making
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 2.71

Abandoning all desires, the person who moves without longing, without sense of mine, without I-making — that one attains peace.

---

### Devanagari
विहाय कामान्यः सर्वान्पुमांश्चरति निःस्पृहः।

निर्ममो निरहंकारः स शांतिमधिगच्छति

### IAST
vihāya kāmānyaḥ sarvānpumāṃścarati niḥspṛhaḥ|

nirmamo nirahaṃkāraḥ sa śāṃtimadhigacchati

### Pratibha Translation
“Abandoning all desires, the person who moves without longing, without ‘mine,’ without I-making — that one attains peace.”

### Pratibha Commentary
Motion continues: *carati* — he goes about — but without *kāma*, without *spṛhā*, without *mama*, without *ahaṃkāra*. Peace is compatible with a life of walking; incompatible with possessive self-manufacturing. The contested claim is that *nirahaṃkāra* is not personality-erasure but release of the appropriating “I-am-the-doer/owner” reflex. Existentially, practice peace as a way of moving through tasks without the private stamp of mine and me.

Peace walks. Nirmama and nirahaṃkāra are how motion stays clean. Drop the private stamp of mine and me-making while the day continues.

Moving without mine and I-making is peace as locomotion. Try it in speech and errands. The day continues; the appropriating stamp does not have to. Peace that cannot walk is incomplete. Practice nirmama in motion until the stamp of mine goes quiet.

Move through an errand without mine-stamping; let peace prove itself as locomotion, not as escape. Return once more to the exact image the verse uses; images carry claims arguments alone miss.

### Key Terms
**niḥspṛha (निःस्पृह)** — without longing → absence of ache-to-obtain while moving → “desireless” is blunter.

**nirmama (निर्मम)** — without mine-ness → non-possessive field of experience → “unselfish” is moral; this is ontological de-mining.

**nirahaṃkāra (निरहंकार)** — without I-making → release of ego-construction → “egoless” is popular; the Sanskrit targets the fabricating function.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Īśā Upaniṣad 1:** Both pair moving in the world with non-grasping (*mā gṛdhaḥ*).
*Divergence:* Īśā opens with the Lord’s filling; Gītā specifies desire, mine, and I-making as the triad dropped for peace.

**Eckhart, talks on detachment (Abegescheidenheit):** Both make peace contingent on release of possessive self-reference.
*Divergence:* Eckhart aims at birth of the Word in the soul; Gītā aims at śānti as mark of sthita-prajña leading to brahma-nirvāṇa.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Complete one chore narrating silently without “my” and without self-credit: only verbs and objects. Notice the body’s quieter peace.

## This Is the Brahman-Standing — Attaining It, One Is Not Deluded; Fixed in It Even at Death, One Reaches Brahma-Nirvāṇa
**Source:** Bhagavad Gītā 2.72

This is the Brahman-standing, Pārtha; attaining it, one is not deluded. Standing in it even at the time of end, one reaches brahma-nirvāṇa.

---

### Devanagari
एषा ब्राह्मी स्थितिः पार्थ नैनां प्राप्य विमुह्यति।

स्थित्वाऽस्यामन्तकालेऽपि ब्रह्मनिर्वाणमृच्छति

### IAST
eṣā brāhmī sthitiḥ pārtha naināṃ prāpya vimuhyati|

sthitvā'syāmantakāle'pi brahmanirvāṇamṛcchati

### Pratibha Translation
“This is the Brahman-standing, Pārtha; attaining it, one is not deluded. Abiding in it even at the hour of death, one reaches [brahma-nirvāṇa].”

### Pratibha Commentary
Chapter 2 closes by naming the portrait’s summit: *brāhmī sthiti* — standing as/in Brahman. Attaining it ends *vimoha*; abiding in it at *anta-kāla* yields *brahma-nirvāṇa*. The contested synthesis is that the whole psychology of desire-chains, sense-restraint, and ocean-peace is not merely coping — it is Brahman-mode. Death becomes a test of abiding, not a separate problem. Existentially, practice is rehearsal for the last hour by becoming undeceived now. The chapter that began with “stand up” ends with a standing that death cannot unsettle.

Brahman-standing gathers the chapter’s psychology into a mode of being undeceived. Rehearse it now so the last hour is not your first attempt at abiding. 'Stand up' completes itself as a standing death cannot unsettle.

Brahma-nirvāṇa is not an exit fantasy from duty; it is the fruit of undeceived abiding, including at the end. Practice undeception in small endings so the last hour recognizes its seat.

Rehearse undeceived abiding in a small ending so the last hour recognizes its seat.

### Key Terms
**brāhmī sthiti (ब्राह्मी स्थिति)** — Brahman-standing / state pertaining to Brahman → stable abiding in the absolute’s mode → “divine state” is vague; Brahman-specific standing is exact.

**vimuhyati (विमुह्यति)** — is deluded/led astray → negated upon attainment → delusion as preventable status, not fate.

**brahma-nirvāṇa (ब्रह्मनिर्वाण)** — extinguishing/blissful dissolution in Brahman → end-attainment from abiding → neither simple annihilation nor crude heaven; the compound holds Brahman and nirvāṇa together.

### Cross-Tradition Resonances
**Mandukya Upaniṣad (turiya):** Both point to a standing beyond waking delusion that refigures the sense of end.
*Divergence:* Mandukya maps states of consciousness; Gītā binds Brahman-standing to ethical-yogic marks and death-hour abiding.

**Nirvāṇa in early Buddhism (extinguishing of the fires):** Both use cessation-language for ultimate ease.
*Divergence:* Buddhist nirvāṇa centers cessation of greed-hatred-delusion without Brahman-metaphysics; *brahma-nirvāṇa* yokes extinguishing to Brahman-abiding.

### Practice (Abhyasa)
Once today, sit as if rehearsing the last hour: drop one clinging narrative, rest in undeceived presence for three minutes, then return to duty without panic.
